The International Court of Justice delivered its long-awaited ruling this week: [Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya minority](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c804wzp20ddvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), a verdict that confirmed what survivors, journalists, and UN investigators have been documenting since the military's 2017 clearance operations in Rakhine State. The ruling carries real legal weight — it was handed down by the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, with jurisdiction accepted by both the applicant (The Gambia, acting on behalf of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation) and Myanmar's government before the junta seized power in 2021. For the roughly 700,000 Rohingya who fled to Bangladesh during the worst of the violence — and for the families of the estimated 10,000 or more killed — the ruling is a form of legal acknowledgment that cannot be undone, whatever happens next. But almost immediately, the focus shifted. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/18/us-sanctions-icj-judges-myanmar-genocide-ruling-israel-gaza-court) that fears are growing Washington may impose sanctions on the ICJ judges responsible for the ruling — a prospect that raises questions about international law that go far beyond Myanmar.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian community and most of the international legal academy will read the ICJ ruling as a vindication of the system of post-war international justice constructed between 1945 and 1998: from the UN Charter to the Genocide Convention to the Rome Statute. The argument runs that even if enforcement is weak, naming and condemning atrocity matters — it shapes the narrative, creates legal accountability, and over time builds the norms that constrain state behaviour. [NPR's coverage](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/19/g-s1-154340/un-court-myanmar-genocide-rohingya) quoted a Rohingya survivor saying "the perpetrators will have to face consequences," expressing a faith in eventual accountability that the architecture of international law was designed to sustain. The progressive critique of American hesitation focuses on hypocrisy: the United States has long championed international law when it serves American interests, so resistance to ICJ rulings that touch on American allies — Israel in Gaza being the obvious parallel — exposes a double standard. That critique has genuine force and cannot simply be dismissed.

## A different read

There is, however, a harder and more uncomfortable argument that tends to get lost in the moral clarity of genocide verdicts. The question is not whether the ICJ ruling is correct — it appears to be — but what the ruling's enforceability tells us about the gap between international law's aspirations and its actual architecture.

The ICJ has no enforcement mechanism of its own. Its rulings are binding in theory but depend entirely on political will in practice. The Genocide Convention's Article IX grants the court jurisdiction, but Article VIII — which requires states to "call upon the competent organs of the United Nations to take such action under the Charter as they consider appropriate" — runs directly into the Security Council veto structure, where both Russia and China have historically shielded Myanmar from binding action. The United States has been inconsistent: rhetorically condemning the Rohingya persecution while also having strategic interests in keeping Myanmar from aligning entirely with Beijing, and while remaining deeply reluctant to accept any precedent that might expose American military conduct or that of close allies to similar judicial scrutiny.

The threatened sanctions against ICJ judges are, if confirmed, an extraordinary escalation of that reluctance. The logic is obvious enough: if Washington is simultaneously facing ICJ proceedings related to Gaza-linked material, an American administration might calculate that pre-emptively delegitimising the court protects a broader set of interests. But the costs of that calculation are severe. [Al Jazeera's reporting on the Rohingya ruling](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/11/19/what-does-the-myanmar-genocide-ruling-mean-for-the-rohingya?traffic_source=rss) captures the stakes for affected populations: the ICJ may be imperfect, but for people with no army and no Security Council seat, it is often the only institution capable of producing even a symbolic form of recourse.

The historical parallel here is the League of Nations. After 1931, when Japan invaded Manchuria and the League proved unable to enforce its own principles, the institution did not immediately collapse — it continued to exist, to pass resolutions, to hold conferences. But its authority was hollow, and every subsequent aggressor drew the lesson accordingly. The erosion was not dramatic; it was incremental, each defection making the next more rational. The question the ICJ-sanctions threat poses is whether we are watching an analogous process unfold in real time: not the abolition of international law, but its slow reduction to an instrument powerful states use against weak ones while immunising themselves from scrutiny. That would be a development with consequences extending far beyond Myanmar or Gaza.

There is a narrower conservative case worth making here, too. American scepticism of international judicial bodies is not without historical justification. The ICJ and its sibling institutions have at times been captured by political agendas that bear only a passing resemblance to legal reasoning. But there is a difference between negotiating jurisdiction carefully — as the US did in opting out of the ICC — and actively sanctioning sitting judges for reaching verdicts. The former is statecraft; the latter is intimidation, and it corrodes the credibility of a system that the United States itself built and from which it has derived enormous strategic benefit.

## What to watch

Watch whether the US administration formally announces or expands sanctions on ICJ judges — and watch European and allied reaction, which will indicate how much legal-institutional solidarity remains in the Western alliance. Watch Bangladesh's position: it hosts nearly a million Rohingya refugees and faces both humanitarian and political pressure from an ICJ ruling that it broadly welcomes but cannot unilaterally enforce. Watch Myanmar's military junta response — it has previously ignored or legally contested every international accountability mechanism, but a full ICJ ruling creates different obligations under the Genocide Convention's state-party framework. And watch whether the ruling generates any pressure on ASEAN, which has consistently prioritised non-interference norms over human rights accountability in its dealings with Naypyidaw.
