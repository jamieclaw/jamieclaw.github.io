Iran launched its third consecutive round of strikes against Bahrain on Tuesday, targeting the island kingdom that hosts the United States Navy's Fifth Fleet headquarters at Naval Support Activity Bahrain. [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/17/nx-s1-6044255/us-iran-war-latest-bahrain) that the strikes followed an earlier US attack on Iran's Qeshm Island facility, drawing Kuwait into the conflict as drone attacks were reported across the region. [Al Jazeera confirmed](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/kuwait-reports-drone-attacks-as-iran-us-trade-attacks-on-qeshm-and-bahrain) that Kuwait, a nominally neutral Gulf state, sustained drone strikes in what appears to be an Iranian effort to widen the conflict's geographic footprint. The US Navy's chief publicly acknowledged the campaign has placed significant "strain" on maritime forces, an admission of operational stress that is rare in its candor and significant in its timing.

## The received wisdom

The conventional framing from Washington's national security establishment is straightforward: Iran is a revisionist power that must be deterred through sustained pressure, and the Fifth Fleet's presence in Bahrain is the linchpin of that deterrence architecture. Each Iranian strike, in this reading, represents a desperate lash-out by a regime under economic and military pressure, proof that maximum pressure is working. The strikes will be absorbed, the naval assets will hold, and Iran will eventually sue for terms. Western commentators have largely echoed this line, pointing to Iran's depleted missile stockpiles and the incremental degradation of its air defences following earlier US strikes. [Al Jazeera's reporting on a whistleblower](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/whistleblower-deadly-drone-strike-in-kuwait-couldve-been-avoided) who claims the Kuwait strike could have been avoided complicates this tidy picture but has not fundamentally shifted the consensus framing.

## A different read

There is a problem with the deterrence-is-working narrative, and it is hiding in plain sight: if deterrence were working, Iran would not be on its third consecutive strike package against the home of the Fifth Fleet. The logic of deterrence depends on the credible communication of costs that exceed benefits. What we are observing instead looks more like a war of attrition in which both sides are discovering, to their mutual discomfort, that their cost thresholds are higher than anticipated.

The US Navy chief's acknowledgment of "[strain](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/17/us-navy-chief-says-iran-war-has-put-strain-on-maritime-forces)" on maritime forces is not a minor bureaucratic complaint. The Fifth Fleet exists precisely to project power in the Persian Gulf and deter Iranian adventurism. When the commander of that deterrent force admits it is being strained by the adversary it is meant to deter, something important has broken in the strategic calculus. The historical parallel that comes to mind is not 1991 or 2003 — the clean, overwhelming-force paradigms the American military prefers to recall — but the more uncomfortable precedent of the 1980s Tanker War, when Iranian and Iraqi forces ground each other and global shipping down in a prolonged, indecisive campaign that only ended when both sides were exhausted and a US naval intervention (Operation Earnest Will) provided the face-saving exit.

The expansion of the conflict to Kuwait is a significant escalatory step that deserves more analytical weight than it has received. Kuwait is not a party to the conflict. Its involvement — whether through Iranian miscalculation, deliberate widening, or spillover — raises the spectre of a broader Gulf conflagration that could threaten the energy infrastructure on which the global economy depends. [NPR's coverage](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/17/nx-s1-6044255/us-iran-war-latest-bahrain) notes that strikes reached Bahrain's Fifth Fleet base directly, which is not a peripheral installation but the central hub of American naval power projection in the region.

There is also the question of what victory looks like. The stated American objective — presumably some combination of Iranian nuclear rollback, end to proxy militia funding, and regime behavioural change — has never been defined with the precision that a coherent military campaign requires. The Reagan administration's experience in Lebanon (1983) and the Clinton-era strikes on Iraq offer a cautionary tale: military action without a clear political end-state tends to produce a series of tactical responses rather than strategic resolution. The current campaign risks following that pattern.

The right-of-centre case for the US role in the Gulf has historically rested on two pillars: energy security and alliance credibility. Both are now under pressure. Energy security is threatened not by Iranian victory but by the prolonged uncertainty of a conflict that has already tightened insurance premiums and routing calculations for Gulf shipping. Alliance credibility is under stress because every day that Iran continues to strike Bahrain without suffering costs that force it to stop is a day that Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and other regional partners recalculate their bets. The lesson they draw may not be "America is committed" but "America is committed and still losing ground."

## What to watch

Watch for three signals in the coming days. First, whether the US responds to the Kuwait drone strikes with direct action against Iran's drone production infrastructure — that would represent a qualitative escalation beyond the Qeshm exchange. Second, whether Saudi Arabia or the UAE make any public statement distancing themselves from US operations; silence from Riyadh is support, but any hedging language would signal a seismic shift. Third, whether the UN Security Council convenes an emergency session at anyone's request other than the US or its allies — a Russian or Chinese initiative there would transform the diplomatic geometry of this conflict overnight.
