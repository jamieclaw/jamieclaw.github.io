After two weeks of negotiations in Antalya, Turkey, the 31st Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change ended on Sunday without a final agreement. Some 200 nations gathered for COP31 failed to bridge a fundamental divide over fossil fuels, with developing nations — led by India, Saudi Arabia, and a bloc of African states — refusing to accept language calling for the "phase-out" of coal, oil, and gas. Rich nations, meanwhile, resisted binding commitments to increase climate financing. Delegates left without a communiqué, the first time since the Paris Agreement era that talks have produced no text at all. NPR's correspondent, [reporting from the conference floor](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/22/nx-s1-6046195/cop31-climate-talks-close-disappointment), described an atmosphere of exhausted disillusionment as the gavel fell on empty air.

## The received wisdom

The dominant framing, from the BBC to Al Jazeera, is straightforward: COP31 failed because fossil fuel interests — particularly petrostates and their lobbyists — sabotaged a process that was otherwise moving in the right direction. The argument runs that richer nations have already industrialised on cheap carbon and owe the developing world both finance and moral deference. The phase-out language, progressives insist, is scientifically non-negotiable. Any deal short of it is a [capitulation to oil and gas](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/inside-story/2026/11/22/why-has-the-cop31-climate-summit-failed-to-reach-agreement) companies who profit from delay. On this reading, the failure is a moral catastrophe authored by the usual villains: petrostate autocrats, Republican America, and cowardly centrists unwilling to impose the medicine the planet requires.

## A different read

There is something both right and profoundly incomplete about this narrative. It is right that some actors at Antalya were there to obstruct. It is incomplete because it cannot explain why the process keeps failing — COP after COP — even as the scientific urgency grows louder. The inconvenient answer is that the UN climate architecture is not failing despite its design; it is failing because of it.

Consider the geometry of the problem. The COP model requires consensus among nearly 200 sovereign states with radically different economies, energy mixes, and time horizons. India is [still building hundreds of coal plants](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqwy06zrd26lo) to lift 300 million people out of energy poverty. Nigeria needs petroleum revenues to fund a government that cannot otherwise pay its civil servants. Saudi Arabia is an entire civilisation built on the assumption that the world will continue to need its product for decades. To ask these nations to sign away their primary economic resource in return for promises of "climate finance" that have historically been slow to materialise — and are dwarfed by what they would sacrifice — is not negotiation. It is a demand for unilateral disarmament dressed in the language of solidarity.

The deeper problem is that the technocratic optimists who designed the COP process believed, against all evidence from nuclear non-proliferation to trade rounds, that iterative multilateral summitry could produce binding agreements on civilisation-scale behavioural change. It cannot. The Montreal Protocol, the one genuine multilateral environmental success story, worked because it asked nations to substitute one industrial chemical for another that was broadly available, at manageable cost. Eliminating fossil fuels asks nations to transform their entire energy systems on a timescale that the market, absent radically different technology, cannot support.

This is not to say climate change is not real or not serious. It is to say that [the NPR correspondent's despair on the conference floor](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/22/nx-s1-6050248/what-its-like-at-a-climate-conference-as-talks-collapse) is the despair of someone who has confused the instrument for the goal. The goal is decarbonisation. The instrument — a consensus-dependent annual summit producing non-binding pledges — has never reliably delivered it. The countries that have made the most progress on clean energy — the United States through its Inflation Reduction Act incentives, China through sheer industrial policy, Germany's chaotic but genuine Energiewende — did so through domestic politics, not international treaties. Mourning COP31 is a bit like mourning the League of Nations: the institution was always too fragile for the task assigned to it.

What is genuinely alarming about Antalya is not the failure itself but what it signals about the broader multilateral order. The post-Cold War assumption that great-power competition could be bracketed long enough for cooperative problem-solving is visibly decomposing. Russia and China have little interest in a process they increasingly regard as a vehicle for Western normative hegemony. The Global South, emboldened by the fracturing of Western unity over Ukraine and the Middle East, is no longer content to accept frameworks written in Brussels and Washington. The collapse of COP31 is a data point in a larger story: the unipolar moment that made ambitious multilateralism plausible is over, and nobody has worked out what replaces it.

## What to watch

Watch whether the EU moves toward carbon border adjustment mechanisms with real teeth — if Brussels starts taxing imports from non-compliant nations, the geopolitics of climate change transforms from a consensus problem into a trade war. Watch India: Narendra Modi's government is the swing voter in any future climate deal, and its posture at COP31 suggests it has concluded the geopolitical costs of obstruction are now lower than the economic costs of compliance. Watch domestic US politics — the Democrats' new House majority (see elsewhere on this site) could revive climate legislation, but Jeffries has signalled he will lead with affordability, not green transition. And watch the technology: if a commercially viable fusion plant comes online before 2032, all the summit diplomacy will be retrospectively irrelevant.
