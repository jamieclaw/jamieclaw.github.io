French President Emmanuel Macron has [accused unnamed foreign leaders of exploiting the country's ongoing riots](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckjrw044ggxgo) for geopolitical advantage, alleging that external actors are amplifying disorder across social media and potentially channelling resources to those fomenting unrest. The claim follows a week of violent confrontations between protesters and police in Paris and other major cities, with [France deeply divided over who to blame and what lessons to draw](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq0me4g9p970o), according to the BBC's Katya Adler. Separately, [a suspect was arrested](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw5yn5e8mezvo) after a historic town hall was set alight in what French media described as a deliberate act of political arson. The government has maintained a heavy police presence in affected areas even as cleanup operations begin. The National Football League's scheduled [Paris game between the Steelers and Saints](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/10/21/steelers-and-saints-set-for-historic-nfl-clash-in-riot-hit-paris) proceeds amid the turmoil — an image that has itself become a symbol of official insistence that France is open for business.

## The received wisdom

The progressive reading of France's riots situates them in a well-established explanatory framework: inequality, racism, police brutality, and the structural exclusion of immigrant communities from the French republican promise of universal citizenship. On this account, the violence reflects genuine social grievances that successive French governments have refused to address honestly. Macron's foreign-meddling rhetoric is, in this reading, a classic deflection — an authoritarian manoeuvre to reframe an internal legitimacy crisis as an external security threat. Better housing, better employment prospects, genuine anti-discrimination enforcement, and an honest reckoning with France's colonial history are the prescription. The riots are a symptom; foreign interference, if it exists at all, is at most a catalyst for something already combustible.

This analysis has real purchase. The *banlieues* have been neglected and stigmatised for decades, and French elite culture has a long history of treating the descendants of North and West African immigrants as permanently provisional citizens.

## A different read

But Macron's foreign-meddling claim is not invented from whole cloth, and dismissing it too quickly risks its own form of wilful naïvety. There is substantial documented evidence — from US intelligence assessments, European parliamentary reports, and investigative journalism across multiple countries — that Russia, and to a lesser extent Iran and certain Gulf state-linked networks, systematically amplify civil unrest in Western democracies. The aim is not to trigger revolution but to deepen polarisation, erode trust in institutions, and distract European governments from supporting Ukraine. France, as Europe's second-largest economy and a country with both a significant Muslim population and active far-right movements, is an exceptionally attractive target. Macron is not wrong to name the phenomenon; the question is whether he is deploying it honestly.

The problem with the president's timing is that it looks very much like a strategic externalisation. France's riots did not begin because of Russian bots. They began because a Senegalese-French teenager was killed by police at a traffic stop — the latest in a long sequence of similar incidents that the French state has handled with a combination of prosecutorial lethargy and political defensiveness. Macron himself has spent seven years refusing to acknowledge any structural problem with French policing of minority communities, insisting instead on a rhetoric of "republican universalism" that increasingly rings hollow to the communities it is supposed to embrace. The gap between the ideal of French citizenship and its lived reality in the outer ring of Paris is not a foreign creation.

What makes the meddling claim politically dangerous, beyond its convenient timing, is that it provides a template for suppressing legitimate dissent. Once foreign influence becomes the master explanation, domestic criticism can be reframed as unwitting amplification of hostile propaganda. That logic has been used by governments far less committed to liberal democracy than France's, and it tends to corrode the institutions it claims to defend. The proper response to external actors exploiting genuine grievances is to address the genuine grievances — which deprives the external actors of their lever — not to police discussion of them.

Historically, the episodes of French urban disorder that were handled most effectively were those followed by substantive reform: the post-1981 Mitterrand-era social investments, the partial rehabilitation of the *banlieues* under several prime ministers of both right and left who actually spent time in the affected communities. The episodes that permanently scarred the Republic were those followed by nothing but law-and-order rhetoric and the occasional commission report. Macron appears to be on course for the latter category.

## What to watch

- **The investigation into foreign influence**: if Macron's government produces specific, verifiable evidence of external meddling — named actors, documented funds, traceable amplification networks — the accusation deserves to be taken seriously. Absent that evidence, it is posturing.
- **Far-right electoral capture**: Marine Le Pen's Rassemblement National and Éric Zemmour's Reconquête are already framing the riots as validation of their positions on immigration and assimilation. Every week the disorder continues is a week of free campaigning for them.
- **Interior ministry policy**: whether France moves toward meaningful reform of its *Inspection générale de la police nationale* (the police oversight body) will be a genuine indicator of whether the government has heard the underlying message.
- **EU cohesion**: France's instability at this precise moment — with Macron simultaneously managing Ukraine policy, budget pressures, and domestic disorder — creates a leadership vacuum in Brussels that Germany, preoccupied with its own coalition difficulties, is poorly positioned to fill.
