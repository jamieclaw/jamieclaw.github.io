Iran has [publicly declared its determination to retain its uranium enrichment programme](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/21/iran-determined-to-keep-uranium-amid-increased-war-odds-flurry-of-meetings) even as diplomats from multiple countries arrive in Tehran for what Al Jazeera's analysis describes as a "flurry of meetings" amid sharply elevated war odds. The declaration follows a period of intense back-channel contact, with the United States reportedly pressing for a return to some form of the JCPOA framework while Iran insists it will not accept constraints on enrichment without guarantees that any future US administration cannot unilaterally reverse. Separately, [the US has ordered the departure of non-emergency personnel from Saudi Arabia](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/21/us-orders-departure-of-personnel-from-saudi-arabia-citing-houthi-threat) due to what it describes as a credible Houthi threat — a signal that the regional spillover risk from any Iran confrontation is being taken seriously inside the State Department and Pentagon. The convergence of these signals suggests the Iranian nuclear standoff is entering a genuinely critical phase.

## The received wisdom

The Obama-era framework and its defenders have always argued that Iran's nuclear programme is, at its core, a security hedge — Tehran's rational response to being surrounded by hostile states, a US military presence across the region, and the memory of Saddam Hussein's invasion. On this reading, the way to slow or halt the programme is to reduce Iran's security anxiety: guarantee non-interference, lift sanctions, provide economic integration, and give the clerical establishment a face-saving path toward gradual normalisation. The JCPOA, whatever its limitations, was an implementation of that logic — trading enrichment constraints for economic relief. Trump's unilateral withdrawal in 2018 vindicated Iran's scepticism of American reliability and drove the programme forward. Any new deal must therefore include durability mechanisms that survive electoral cycles in Washington.

This argument has genuine intellectual honesty behind it. Iranian nuclear progress since 2018 is a direct consequence of American policy failure, and pretending otherwise is dishonest.

## A different read

The problem is that Iran has now enriched uranium to 60 percent purity — just a step removed from weapons-grade — and its programme has advanced so far since the JCPOA's collapse that any new agreement would need to begin from a far less favourable baseline. The original deal's "breakout time" — the period required to assemble enough highly enriched uranium for a single weapon — was calculated at approximately one year. Current estimates by the International Atomic Energy Agency and independent analysts put that figure at somewhere between weeks and a few months. The security landscape has fundamentally changed.

Tehran's insistence that it will not accept enrichment constraints without ironclad American guarantees is, in this context, less a negotiating position and more a statement that no realistic agreement is possible. No American administration can constitutionally bind its successor — a feature of the US constitutional order that is not going away. Iran knows this. The demand for impossibly durable guarantees functions as a negotiating fig leaf that allows Tehran to appear engaged while continuing to advance its programme.

What makes the current moment particularly dangerous is that the regional context has shifted dramatically. The Houthi threat to Saudi Arabia, cited in the US personnel departure, is not separable from Iran's strategic calculus. Tehran uses its network of regional proxies — Hezbollah, Hamas, the Houthis, various Iraqi militias — as a deterrence architecture: a distributed threat that any military strike on Iranian soil would activate. The rational-actor model that underpins the diplomatic approach assumes Iranian decision-makers weigh costs and benefits in ways broadly recognisable to Western analysts. But the Islamic Republic is not a unitary rational actor; it is a fractured regime in which hardliners who benefit from sanctions-era autarky have strong incentives to prevent any settlement that might empower reformists.

The right-of-centre tradition in American foreign policy has generally been hawkish on Iran, sometimes recklessly so. The Iraq war catastrophically reduced US credibility and capacity in the region. But the hawks were not wrong about the fundamental character of the Iranian regime, even if their preferred solutions were catastrophic. The question now is whether there is a path between a deal that legitimises Iran's nuclear status and a military strike whose regional consequences could dwarf anything seen since 2003. Coercive diplomacy — genuine pressure through sanctions enforcement, financial isolation, and credible military posture — accompanied by a realistic offer, remains the least bad option. But it requires an American administration capable of sustained strategic patience, which is in short supply in Washington at the moment.

## What to watch

- **IAEA quarterly report**: the agency's next assessment of Iranian enrichment activities will indicate whether the programme is accelerating or holding steady during the diplomatic flurry.
- **Israeli threshold calculations**: Jerusalem has consistently maintained that it will act unilaterally if Iran approaches weapons-grade capability. Watch Israeli Air Force exercises and public statements from the Prime Minister's office.
- **Saudi Arabia's posture**: Riyadh's recent overtures toward Iran — including the China-brokered 2023 normalisation agreement, which has had a complicated follow-through — create an interesting variable. Saudi Arabia's appetite for a regional conflict has decreased; its tolerance for an Iranian bomb has not.
- **Russian and Chinese roles**: both countries have Iranian economic relationships that give them potential leverage they have not used. Whether either chooses to apply it will be a significant variable in coming months.
