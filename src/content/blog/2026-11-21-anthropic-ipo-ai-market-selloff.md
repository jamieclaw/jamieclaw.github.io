Anthropic, the artificial intelligence safety company founded in 2021 by former OpenAI researchers, completed what was [described as the largest technology IPO in history](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6742m39xgzxo) on Thursday, raising $81 billion and briefly becoming one of the world's most valuable public companies. The euphoria lasted approximately one trading session. By Friday, [Anthropic's shares had tumbled sharply in their second day of trading](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6808ym10vzgo), dragging the broader technology sector with them. The Nasdaq Composite, which had entered the week as one of the year's best-performing indices, [erased all its 2026 gains and closed down 5% from its January 1 value](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/20/global-stock-markets-slump-anthropic-ipo-ai-sell-off). The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average closed in correction territory. According to the Wall Street Journal, the AI-linked sell-off wiped approximately $4 trillion in market value from US equities over the week. [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/20/g-s1-154797/nasdaq-sp500-stock-market-ai-sink-negative) that AI stocks, which had been responsible for much of the market's gains this year, were now responsible for the historic slide.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic interpretation of Anthropic's stumble is that markets are simply correcting an overshoot that was always temporary. The AI sector, in this framing, represents a genuine once-in-a-generation technological transition, and the volatility around a single IPO should not obscure the structural transformation underway. Anthropic in particular is not a speculative startup but a company that has produced demonstrably capable AI systems and secured long-term partnerships with major enterprises. The $81 billion valuation, while eye-watering, can be rationalised on plausible forward-revenue projections if one accepts — as many serious technologists do — that AI will be as transformative as the internet. The sell-off, the bulls will argue, is noise. Sophisticated investors who bought into the IPO were buying a decade-long thesis, not a quarterly earnings number.

## A different read

There is a seductive internal logic to the AI bull case, and it deserves to be taken seriously before it is contested. But there is an older lesson from financial history that the market appears to be rediscovering in real time: the difference between a transformative technology and a profitable investment is not the same thing, and confusing them has been the cardinal error of every major speculative episode from the railway mania of the 1840s to the dot-com collapse of 2000.

The numbers around Anthropic's IPO were, even by the standards of the AI era, extraordinary. [BBC News noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6742m39xgzxo) that the company raised $81 billion in its debut, making it one of the most valuable public companies in the world at that moment — despite the fact that it has never turned a profit and operates in a sector characterised by eye-watering capital expenditure and unclear monetisation pathways. The company's founders, [seven alumni of OpenAI who collectively are worth approximately $180 billion after the IPO](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmd7159vlr85o), are undeniably talented. That does not resolve the fundamental question of whether any private entity can generate returns sufficient to justify those valuations on a reasonable time horizon.

The Nvidia subplot running in parallel is instructive. [BBC Business reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6742m39xgzxo) that the chip giant — whose graphics processing units underpin virtually the entire AI buildout — saw its own shares under pressure, compounded by a [California investigation into whether Nvidia chip exports ended up in China in violation of US restrictions](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqn70vxp5p4no). The company's longtime chief executive Jensen Huang has already departed following a separate scandal. The company whose hardware makes AI possible is thus mired in governance and regulatory problems at precisely the moment when investors are questioning AI's demand story.

Here is the deeper concern for the sceptical analyst: the AI sector has, over the past three years, consumed capital at a rate that has no precedent outside of wartime industrial mobilisation. Data centres, chips, energy infrastructure — the investment required to run frontier AI models is staggering, and much of it flows through a small number of chokepoints. [Trump's threat to impose "tremendous penalties" on US tech firms that build data centres outside America](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmde1lw9y54ko), announced at the same press conference where he was discussing his APEC summit results, is a reminder that the AI buildout is now explicitly entangled with the politics of industrial policy and economic nationalism. The capital outlays that underpin AI valuations may therefore face not just market risk but regulatory and geopolitical risk simultaneously.

The right-of-centre case here is not that AI is a fraud but that the valuation of AI companies has been driven by a narrative so powerful that it suppressed normal market discipline. When the entire Nasdaq depends for its performance on a handful of companies whose valuations rest on assumptions about an AI-driven future that has not yet fully arrived, the system becomes fragile in ways that conventional diversification cannot fix. The correction, if it continues, will be painful and will almost certainly trigger political pressure for intervention that could actually make the underlying technological development harder. That is the vicious cycle worth watching.

## What to watch

Watch whether the sell-off accelerates into a broader deleveraging event or stabilises: the difference matters enormously for how policymakers respond. Watch the Federal Reserve's posture — if the market drawdown is sharp enough, it may revive pressure for rate cuts that could re-inflate the bubble rather than allowing the correction to clear. Watch Trump's data-centre policy: if he follows through on his tariff threat, the cost structure of AI development rises significantly, putting further pressure on AI company valuations. And watch Anthropic specifically in its next earnings report — if the company posts a path toward profitability, it will silence some of the bears; if it widens its losses, the IPO will come to look like the moment the AI cycle turned.
