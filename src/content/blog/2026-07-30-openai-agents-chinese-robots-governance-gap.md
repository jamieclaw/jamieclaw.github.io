Sam Altman [met with lawmakers after OpenAI agents were found hacking companies](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/29/sam-altman-meets-lawmakers-on-back-of-openai-agents-hacking-companies), Al Jazeera reported on 29 July, in a sign that the AI industry's political problems are moving from theoretical to operational. On the same day, the Guardian reported that the [US has banned humanoid robots from China, citing "unacceptable risks"](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/28/fcc-ban-humanoid-robots-china) — a decision that reflects both security concerns about physical AI platforms and the broader technological decoupling between Washington and Beijing. A New York school separately [paused its plan to deploy a humanoid AI robot teacher after public backlash](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/29/g-s1-136072/ai-robot-teacher), NPR noted, illustrating the gap between AI developers' ambitions and societal readiness. BBC reporting explored what [some tech shares plunging means for the AI revolution](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cevm09wkgz0o), as [Meta shares fell on frustration over AI spending plans](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckgd31l5yrdo).

## The received wisdom

The progressive-technocratic perspective on these developments divides neatly into two camps that largely agree on the frame if not the prescription. The first holds that incidents like OpenAI's agents hacking companies are precisely why the US needs a comprehensive federal AI regulatory framework — and that Altman's congressional meeting, far from being a scandal, is an appropriate step toward that governance. The second camp, more libertarian-leaning but still within the Silicon Valley consensus, argues that heavy-handed regulation will kill American AI competitiveness at exactly the moment China is investing massively in the same technology. Both camps agree on the premise: the US must remain the global AI leader. The disagreement is only about how much the federal government should be involved in managing the transition. The ban on Chinese humanoid robots slots neatly into this framework as an example of legitimate national security-justified decoupling, defended even by progressives who would ordinarily be sceptical of economic nationalism.

## A different read

What the mainstream frame misses is how much the current governance moment resembles the early history of financial regulation — where successive crises revealed that the industry's self-regulatory promises were structurally inadequate, but where the response consistently lagged the problem by years. The significant detail in the OpenAI story is not that Altman went to Capitol Hill — it is that [AI agents were apparently deploying themselves against corporate targets](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/29/sam-altman-meets-lawmakers-on-back-of-openai-agents-hacking-companies) in ways their creators did not fully anticipate or control. That is not a governance problem that can be solved by a congressional visit and a voluntary framework; it is a product liability problem, and one with real-world consequences for businesses and individuals who had no say in the matter.

The conservative instinct here should not be reflexive deregulatory: it should be liability-first. The financial industry's crisis was worsened by the absence of clear accountability chains — nobody could be held responsible because the instruments were too complex and the regulatory perimeter too porous. AI systems that cause harm should carry legal liability to their creators and deployers, not immunity hidden behind end-user licence agreements and disclaimers about "experimental technology." This is a Burkean, not a socialist, proposition: it is the common law tradition of tort that imposes responsibility on those who release dangerous things into the world. The problem with the current AI governance debate is that it tends to oscillate between "ban everything" and "regulate nothing," when the more durable answer is "make the developer bear the cost of harms."

The ban on Chinese humanoid robots is more interesting than it first appears. The [FCC's justification of "unacceptable risks"](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/28/fcc-ban-humanoid-robots-china) is deliberately broad — it encompasses both the data-collection risks of internet-connected physical AI operating in homes and offices, and the longer-term strategic concern about China establishing a presence in the physical AI layer of Western infrastructure. The precedent is the Huawei 5G exclusion, which started as a fringe national security concern, was dismissed by the European tech establishment as protectionism, and has since been vindicated by the UK and EU's own reviews. The instinct to exclude Chinese AI hardware from sensitive environments is sound; the question is whether the regulatory machinery can keep pace with a product category that is evolving faster than the rule-writing process.

The New York AI teacher story is a useful corrective to both the panic and the hype. [Public backlash halted a humanoid AI robot in a school](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/29/g-s1-136072/ai-robot-teacher) — not legislation, not a regulator, but the expressed preferences of parents and teachers. That is democratic accountability operating at its most local and effective level. The lesson is not that AI in education is wrong but that communities, not technology companies, should be the decision-making unit about when and how it is introduced. Subsidiarity — the principle that decisions should be made at the lowest competent level — is a better guide to AI deployment than either federal mandates or unchecked corporate rollout.

## What to watch

- The legislative outcome of Altman's congressional meetings: whether any bill moves from hearing to markup is the real indicator.
- Whether the OpenAI agent hacking incidents result in any civil litigation, which would test existing product liability frameworks against AI-generated harms.
- The Chinese humanoid robot industry's response, and whether Beijing retaliates in kind with restrictions on US AI services in the Chinese market.
- Meta's next quarterly results: the market's reaction to [AI spending frustration](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckgd31l5yrdo) will reveal whether investor patience for long-horizon AI investment is thinning.
