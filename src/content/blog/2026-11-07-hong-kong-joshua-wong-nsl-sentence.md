Joshua Wong Chi-fung, the 29-year-old activist who first came to international attention leading the 2014 Umbrella Movement as a teenager, has been [sentenced to ten years in prison](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/05/hong-kong-joshua-wong-sentenced-national-security-case) under Hong Kong's National Security Law. The charge was collusion with foreign forces — a broadly drawn offence under the NSL that carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment. The prosecution centred on his contacts with foreign governments and advocacy organisations during his years of activism. Separately, a court in Macau handed down what [officials described as that city's first national security conviction](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmdx33v9vvr2o), jailing a pro-democracy campaigner in a case that underlines the extent to which Beijing's security framework has been extended across both Special Administrative Regions simultaneously. Wong has already served years on other charges; the ten-year sentence pushes his earliest possible release date to the mid-2030s.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream liberal-internationalist framing is clear and, in its essentials, correct: Joshua Wong is a political prisoner by any reasonable definition of the term. His offences consist of speech, organisation, and advocacy. His contacts with foreign governments involved lobbying for sanctions and international pressure on Beijing — activities that, in any functioning democracy, would be considered normal political work. The sentence, on this reading, reflects the Chinese Communist Party's determination to punish dissent comprehensively rather than to address the underlying grievances that produced the 2019 protests. Human rights organisations note that hundreds of activists have been jailed under the NSL since its imposition in June 2020, and that the legal process — no jury trials for NSL cases, judges selected by the Chief Executive — cannot credibly be called independent. This reading deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms, because it is largely accurate.

## A different read

Where the mainstream framing tends to go wrong is not in its moral assessment but in its political prescription. The instinctive response in European capitals and Washington is to call for sanctions, diplomatic pressure, and declarations of concern — gestures that have been made repeatedly since 2020 with essentially zero effect on Beijing's behaviour. The right-of-centre case for engaging seriously with Hong Kong's situation runs not through moral denunciation (though that has its place) but through a clearer strategic analysis of what has actually changed and why the West's leverage is so limited.

The NSL was imposed in June 2020 not because of a sudden fit of repression but because Beijing concluded — correctly, from its own strategic vantage point — that the 2019 protest movement had permanently compromised the "one country, two systems" framework as a stable arrangement. Beijing's calculation was that the reputational cost of the crackdown would be manageable, and that Western governments' economic interdependence with China would prevent any serious response. That calculation has been largely vindicated. The UK has admitted [approximately 180,000 holders of British National (Overseas) passports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmdx33v9vvr2o) since 2021 — a humanitarian measure that also, quietly, relieved Beijing of a politically active population. The United States imposed targeted sanctions on a handful of Hong Kong officials that have had no discernible effect on policy. The European Union did rather less.

Historically, the West has been in this position before. After the Soviet Union crushed the 1956 Hungarian uprising, Western powers issued stern declarations and accepted Hungarian refugees. After the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown, a brief sanctions period gave way within two years to a resumption of normal economic relations. The pattern is consistent: moral declarations without strategic follow-through. What distinguished those cases from Hong Kong's is that Hong Kong was *inside* an international framework — the Sino-British Joint Declaration, a binding treaty — that Beijing has now effectively abandoned. The International Court of Justice has no mechanism to compel compliance. The United Nations Security Council is blocked by China's veto. Britain's foreign secretary [has committed to raising the issue in international forums](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/6/trumps-unconvincing-narrative-on-iran), but forums without enforcement are not instruments of justice.

The hardest lesson from Joshua Wong's sentence is not about China's behaviour, which is consistent and predictable. It is about the West's persistent unwillingness to build the kind of strategic leverage — in trade policy, in technology export controls, in financial system architecture — that might actually alter Beijing's cost-benefit calculations. The EU's recent moves on critical minerals dependency, the broader conversation about de-risking supply chains: these are the right long-term tools. But they move slowly, and Joshua Wong will serve years before they materialise into anything resembling pressure.

The Macau conviction is particularly significant. Macau has historically been less politically active than Hong Kong, and a first NSL conviction there signals that Beijing is not content to manage the existing dissidents but intends to pre-emptively foreclose the possibility of a future civil society in both cities. The message is that the experiment of 2047 — full integration — has been effectively moved forward by 21 years.

## What to watch

- Whether the remaining defendants in mass NSL trials — dozens of whom have pleaded guilty in exchange for lighter sentences — receive the expected leniency, which would indicate the prosecution strategy is about deterrence rather than maximum punishment.
- The UK's BN(O) visa pathway renewal: the scheme comes up for policy review in 2027, and its continuation signals British political will.
- Beijing's treatment of remaining civil society organisations in Hong Kong, particularly independent media, following the shutdowns of Apple Daily and Stand News.
- Any movement in Washington on sanctions targeting Hong Kong's financial sector rather than individual officials.
