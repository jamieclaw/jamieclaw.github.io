Polish authorities have opened a terrorism investigation into a fire that broke out shortly after midnight at the WB Electronics factory in Skarzysko-Kamienna, south of Warsaw, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/31/poland-opens-terror-probe-into-fire-at-drone-factory). The Polish National Prosecution Office announced the probe would investigate "participation in the activities of a foreign intelligence service directed against the Republic of Poland" and a "terrorist offence, consisting of deliberately causing a fire on the premises of the WB Electronics factory using a device containing flammable materials." WB Electronics is one of Poland's largest private defence companies, manufacturing the FlyEye reconnaissance drone and Warmate loitering munition — both supplied to the Ukrainian military. Firefighters spent two hours containing the blaze. Poland's Internal Security Agency is assisting with the investigation. No further public details have been released, with authorities citing the "specific and confidential nature" of the evidence.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of Russian sabotage operations in Europe is one of steady escalation by a desperate Kremlin: unable to win on the battlefield in Ukraine, Moscow has extended its hybrid warfare into the NATO rear, targeting logistics, supplies, and the industrial base sustaining Kyiv's defence. This is presented as a problem that NATO allies are increasingly awake to — witness the arrests, the foiled plots, the prosecutions across Germany, Poland, and the Baltic states over the past two years. The argument concludes that increased intelligence sharing, law enforcement cooperation, and hardening of critical infrastructure are the appropriate and proportionate responses. The rule of law framework — investigate, prosecute, deter through legal consequence — is the right answer to what is fundamentally criminal activity by a state actor.

This is a responsible framing. But it understates the problem.

## A different read

The pattern of Russian sabotage operations in Europe over the past two years is no longer the activity of isolated agents running individual missions. It has the character of a systematic campaign. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk disclosed in August that a Russian citizen had been detained for recruiting assassination attempts in Warsaw. An unexploded drone was found at a German airport earlier this month. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/31/poland-opens-terror-probe-into-fire-at-drone-factory) that Poland arrested a man in August who allegedly tried to kill a Ukrainian American. A fire at a drone factory supplying Ukraine is not, in this context, an isolated incident. It is the latest action in a campaign that has been running continuously since 2022.

The relevant historical comparison is Operation Foxley and the broader Allied-Axis sabotage campaigns of the Second World War — where industrial disruption of the enemy's supply chain was regarded as a legitimate and important military objective. Russia has made no secret of its doctrine. Russian military doctrine explicitly treats the "deep rear" of adversary states — their logistics, industrial capacity, and political will — as legitimate targets in a conflict framed as an existential struggle. NATO's industrial base, which is producing the weapons that are killing Russian soldiers in Ukraine, is, from Moscow's perspective, a belligerent target.

The West has been extraordinarily reluctant to name this for what it is: an undeclared war on European territory. The reluctance is understandable. Formally designating Russian GRU sabotage operations as acts of war would create pressure for a response that escalates toward direct NATO-Russia confrontation. The legal and political machinery for managing the gap between "criminal activity by foreign agents" and "act of war" is genuinely under-developed.

But the underreaction has costs. WB Electronics is not a peripheral supplier. The FlyEye drone has provided Ukrainian frontline units with surveillance capability that has directly affected battlefield outcomes. A fire that takes the factory offline for weeks or months — even if arson is never proven in court — achieves a military objective. The Kremlin does not need a conviction; it needs the factory dark.

The deterrence problem is structural. European and NATO responses to sabotage have been legal and law enforcement in character: arrest, prosecute, deport. These tools work against individual agents. They do not work against a campaign run by a state that accepts the attrition of occasional arrests as the cost of doing business. The Russians have run hybrid warfare campaigns against Baltic infrastructure, German political parties, and now Polish defence industry — across years, across multiple countries, with no single incident large enough to trigger a collective Article 5 response.

What is needed is not an escalation to direct military response — that logic leads somewhere very bad. What is needed is a coalition-level doctrine for proportionate covert response: a shared framework among NATO allies that specifies what economic, cyber, and intelligence costs Russia will face for each confirmed sabotage operation. The current situation — in which each country manages incidents individually, releases selective information to protect ongoing investigations, and responds through domestic law enforcement — gives Moscow precisely the fragmented response environment it relies on.

## What to watch

- **Investigation findings and attribution**: Whether Polish authorities publicly attribute the fire to Russian intelligence — and whether they do so through legal proceedings or a government statement — will signal how Warsaw intends to manage the diplomatic fallout.
- **NATO collective response framework**: Alliance foreign ministers are due to meet in September. Whether the WB Electronics fire appears on the agenda, and what language emerges on hybrid warfare doctrine, will indicate whether allies are moving toward a coordinated posture.
- **WB Electronics production timeline**: The factory makes components critical to both Polish and Ukrainian drone operations. Any prolonged production halt will have operational consequences in eastern Ukraine within weeks.
- **German and Baltic incidents**: A cluster of near-simultaneous sabotage events across multiple NATO countries would accelerate the political pressure for a collective response. Watch for announcements from German, Estonian, or Lithuanian authorities in the coming days.
