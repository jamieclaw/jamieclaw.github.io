The United States has [lost its status as a country where measles is eliminated](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/14/nx-s1-6041119/us-measles-elimination-status-vaccines-cdc-paho), according to researchers with direct knowledge of the determination by the Pan American Health Organization and global health authorities. Measles elimination — defined as an interruption of endemic transmission for twelve or more consecutive months — was achieved in the US in 2000, one of the signal public-health victories of the late twentieth century. The CDC has tracked an accelerating decline in measles-mumps-rubella vaccination rates since 2021, with clusters of unvaccinated children now dense enough in several states to sustain chains of transmission. The determination does not mean measles is endemic again in the classical sense; it means the buffer of herd immunity that prevented importations from becoming outbreaks has been eroded to the point where it no longer reliably holds.

## The received wisdom

The standard public-health and media framing is unambiguous and largely correct in its facts: declining vaccination rates, driven by misinformation spread through social media and amplified by politically connected vaccine sceptics including figures now inside the federal government, have produced a preventable public-health regression. The loss of elimination status is a direct consequence of a decade-long campaign to undermine institutional trust in vaccines, the argument runs, and the responsibility lies squarely with those who promoted or tolerated that campaign. Children who cannot be vaccinated due to immune conditions are the most direct victims. The solution is straightforward in principle: mandatory school vaccination requirements, vigorous public-health communication, and political leadership willing to say clearly that MMR vaccines are safe and effective.

## A different read

The factual core of that account is not wrong. MMR vaccines are safe, effective, and the reason measles killed hundreds of thousands of children annually before widespread vaccination. Anyone who suggests otherwise is propagating a falsehood, and this column will not equivocate on that. But the collapse of vaccination rates did not emerge from nowhere, and explaining it purely as the product of misinformation campaigns is itself a form of evasion that forecloses the more uncomfortable institutional audit.

Vaccine hesitancy in the United States — including MMR hesitancy — was measurably rising before COVID, before RFK Jr., and before the most recent political cycle. The acceleration of that trend in the COVID era is inseparable from the specific pattern of elite mismanagement of COVID public-health communication: the initially dismissive treatment of the lab-leak hypothesis; the overclaiming about vaccine durability and transmission-blocking that turned out to be incorrect; the mask guidance reversals; the school-closure policies that diverged markedly from European peer nations; and the sustained pressure on social media platforms to suppress heterodox views that sometimes turned out to be correct. Each of those episodes was defended, in real time, by the same institutional apparatus that is now wondering why the public doesn't trust it about MMR.

This is not a defence of vaccine refusal. It is an argument that the institutions responsible for public-health communication made a series of credibility-destroying choices in 2020-22 that rational people noticed and updated on. When you tell a population that the science has changed and therefore last month's guidance is now wrong — four times in eighteen months — you should not be surprised when some fraction of that population concludes that "the science" is a managed social process rather than a neutral authority. That fraction then becomes available to genuinely bad actors who do traffic in misinformation. The public-health establishment handed the misinformers their recruitment pitch.

The domestic political dimension is also more complicated than the standard framing allows. The states with the lowest vaccination rates are not uniformly Republican; they include California counties with affluent, college-educated populations whose vaccine hesitancy traces to lifestyle-progressive sources rather than Trump-aligned ones. The vaccine-sceptic coalition in the United States has always been an ideologically unusual one — anti-establishment libertarians, religious communities with specific objections, and green-organic-wellness progressives who distrust pharmaceutical companies. The tendency to collapse all of these into "right-wing misinformation" is analytically wrong and politically counterproductive, because it misidentifies the population that needs to be persuaded and flatters the political tribe doing the misidentifying.

Fixing this requires something harder than mandatory requirements, though requirements have their place: it requires public-health communicators to be more honest about uncertainty, more accountable for failures, and less nakedly political in their messaging choices. That is a cultural reform within institutions, which is slower and less satisfying than passing a law — but it is what the actual problem demands.

## What to watch

Watch whether the PAHO determination triggers a formal CDC response with specific state-level intervention targets — the absence of a clear federal action plan would itself be diagnostic. Watch whether any Republican governors, in states with declining rates, respond to the elimination-status loss as a governance failure on their watch rather than an opportunity to attack CDC credibility; there are genuine pragmatic conservatives who understand that childhood disease resurgence is not ideologically useful. Watch measles case counts this winter, when importation risk from international travel is higher. And watch whether the broader conversation about institutional trust in public health — which has real policy stakes for pandemic preparedness — is conducted with the seriousness it deserves, or collapses once again into a partisan proxy war.
