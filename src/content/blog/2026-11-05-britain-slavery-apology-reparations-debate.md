Britain's foreign secretary David Miliband this week told the BBC that the country cannot "sweep its past under the carpet" as he confirmed the government would engage in structured dialogue on reparatory justice following the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Antigua. [Miliband was at pains to stress](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm8ezneewr9lo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that a slavery apology would carry "no cost for UK taxpayers" — a claim that raises more questions than it answers, since the entire point of reparations, as distinct from an apology, is precisely that they do involve financial transfer. King Charles, addressing Commonwealth leaders, [called the slave trade a "shameful chapter"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr89znnnn7dno?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in Britain's history — carefully calibrated language that fell short of a formal apology but went further than previous royal statements. And a [Guardian investigation revealed](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/01/uk-was-vehement-slavery-reparations-should-not-be-on-commonwealth-summit-agenda) that Britain had until very recently been "vehement" that reparations should not even appear on the formal CHOGM agenda — a position it has since reversed, at least partially, under pressure from Caribbean member states.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for a formal apology and structured reparations dialogue is grounded in an argument about historical accountability that commands genuine moral weight. Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade was not peripheral or incidental; it was central and profit-driven. The Royal African Company, chartered by the Crown, transported more enslaved Africans than any other entity in the history of the trade. The 1833 Slavery Abolition Act compensated slave owners — not the enslaved — at enormous public expense: a debt that, by some calculations, British taxpayers only finished paying off in 2015. The asymmetry is stark and documented. Caribbean nations whose economies, demographics, and social structures were shaped by forced labour have a legitimate grievance, and the argument that Britain should acknowledge it formally is not unreasonable on its face. The King's speech and Miliband's engagement represent, in this reading, a belated but necessary step toward the kind of reconciliation that post-imperial Britain has been deferring for decades.

## A different read

The problem is not with the moral diagnosis, which is largely correct, but with the political and practical architecture of what is being proposed — and with the gap between the language of "dialogue" and the destination toward which it leads.

Miliband's claim that a slavery apology would involve "no cost to taxpayers" is, charitably, an oversimplification. Formal apologies of the kind that are being discussed always open the door to legally actionable claims. The precedent set by Germany's reparations payments to Holocaust survivors — however morally appropriate in that specific context — took decades of negotiation and required a direct line of legal liability from identifiable perpetrators to identifiable victims. The slavery case involves neither living perpetrators nor, in most practical respects, living direct victims. What it involves is the much more contested question of intergenerational harm, which is real in aggregate but extraordinarily difficult to translate into individual legal claims without creating a framework of national liability that no treasury can honestly describe as "costless."

There is also a question of political legitimacy. Britain's current electorate had no role in the slave trade. Many of its members — including substantial communities of South Asian, East African, and Caribbean heritage themselves — arrived after abolition. The notion that a government elected by this population can bind it to financial obligations for acts committed two centuries earlier by different people under different constitutional arrangements is a significant step, one that deserves democratic deliberation rather than a negotiated concession at a Commonwealth summit.

Compare the historical precedents that actually work: South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission was powerful not because it transferred money but because it created a shared national narrative that, however imperfect, gave victims an authoritative acknowledgment of their experience. Germany's reparations functioned partly because of the direct generational proximity and partly because they were embedded in a broader process of constitutional and cultural transformation. Britain's situation differs on both counts. A "structured dialogue" that begins with predetermined financial commitments — however vaguely framed — and proceeds under international diplomatic pressure, without a domestic deliberative process, risks generating a settlement that pleases almost no one: too little for the Caribbean states that believe the case for substantial reparations is overwhelming, and too much for a British public that was never consulted.

None of this means the underlying historical reckoning should be avoided. It means it should be pursued with more honesty about what is actually being proposed.

## What to watch

Watch whether Miliband's "no cost to taxpayers" claim is ever subjected to legal scrutiny — specifically whether Caribbean governments or advocacy groups attempt to use any formal apology as the basis for juridical claims. Watch the domestic Conservative and Reform UK response: the reparations question has the potential to become a significant culture-war flashpoint ahead of the next election, and how Labour handles that pressure will test the government's political nerve. And watch the Caribbean states themselves: the caricom nations have been pursuing this issue with considerable sophistication for years, and their negotiating position at CHOGM will reveal whether they regard "structured dialogue" as a genuine pathway or a polite deferral.
