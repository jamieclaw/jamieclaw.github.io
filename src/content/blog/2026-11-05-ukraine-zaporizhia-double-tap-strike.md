At least forty people were killed and dozens more may still lie under rubble after a Russian double-tap strike on the Ukrainian village of Komyshuvakha in Zaporizhia Oblast. [Bodycam footage released by Ukrainian authorities](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c98rz6penv86o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) shows rescue workers arriving at the scene of an initial strike, only to be caught in a second, more devastating attack. [Al Jazeera's report](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/4/ukraine-raises-death-toll-from-russian-strike-on-zaporizhia-to-40-people?traffic_source=rss) confirmed the death toll had risen to forty, with rescue operations repeatedly interrupted by drone incursions as emergency personnel attempted to extract survivors from the debris. The double-tap technique — deliberately targeting first responders who arrive after an initial strike — has been documented extensively in Syria and has now become a recurring feature of Russia's campaign in Ukraine. It is, by any definition that the laws of armed conflict recognise, a war crime.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and humanitarian consensus on this strike, and on Russia's conduct in Ukraine more broadly, is essentially correct in its moral diagnosis. Russia is deliberately targeting civilian infrastructure, using cluster munitions in populated areas, and systematically employing tactics designed to maximise civilian suffering. The International Criminal Court has issued warrants; human rights organisations have documented the pattern exhaustively. The argument for continued Western military and financial support for Ukraine rests, in this telling, on a foundational principle: that territorial conquest achieved through mass atrocity cannot be rewarded without inviting its replication. To allow Russia to consolidate its gains would be to teach every revisionist power on earth that the rules-based international order is a fiction maintained only until a major power decides otherwise. The deaths in Komyshuvakha are, in this framing, precisely why Western aid must continue.

This argument has force, and the moral clarity it offers should not be dismissed as naive. Sometimes the right answer is the obvious one.

## A different read

And yet the double-tap strike on Komyshuvakha, precisely because it is so unambiguous in its criminality, forces a question that Western governments have been adept at avoiding: what is the theory of victory? Nearly three years of sustained Western support — billions in artillery, air defence, training, and intelligence — has produced a war that has settled into a grinding attritional slog without a plausible endpoint that either side can accept. Ukraine cannot, without weapons its Western partners have declined to supply, strike the logistical nodes inside Russia that sustain this kind of offensive pressure. Russia cannot, without escalation that would trigger NATO responses it is not yet prepared to risk, break the Ukrainian military's backbone entirely. The result is a war of deliberate cruelty, of which Komyshuvakha is merely the latest atrocity, sustained indefinitely.

The double-tap tactic itself repays strategic analysis. It is not merely sadism, though there is surely an element of that in the command culture that authorises it. It is a deliberate attempt to degrade Ukrainian civil resilience — to make rescue workers afraid to respond, to drain the population's psychological reserves, to force Kyiv to divert military assets to protection of civilian areas that would otherwise go to frontline operations. It is, in other words, a form of coercion aimed at Ukrainian society's will to continue, and it has historical precedents: the German bombing of British cities in 1940 was premised on a similar theory of social demoralisation, a theory that proved comprehensively wrong. The Blitz did not break British morale; if anything, it hardened it. The evidence from Ukraine suggests a similar dynamic — Russian strikes on civilian areas have, if anything, stiffened Ukrainian resolve rather than softened it.

But the Blitz analogy also contains a warning. Britain endured the Blitz because it had a genuine prospect of eventual American intervention on a transformative scale. Ukraine's strategic situation in 2026 is more ambiguous. [American politics are in post-midterm flux](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/11/4/us-midterm-election-results-live-trump-reacts-as-more-results-awaited?traffic_source=rss), with a Republican administration that has treated Ukraine policy as a bargaining chip rather than a strategic commitment. European capacity to substitute for American support is improving but not yet adequate. The question is not whether Russia's conduct is monstrous — it obviously is — but whether the West has a sufficiently coherent and resourced strategy to translate that moral clarity into a military and political outcome that actually ends the killing.

The bodycam footage from Komyshuvakha will, rightly, circulate widely and prompt renewed calls for accountability. What it will not do, by itself, is change the military mathematics that have produced a stalemate. That requires decisions in Washington, Berlin, and Brussels that political leaders have so far lacked the will to make.

## What to watch

The immediate military indicators to watch are whether Ukraine deploys additional air defence assets to Zaporizhia Oblast and whether the strike prompts renewed Western discussion of the longer-range artillery systems Ukraine has repeatedly requested. Diplomatically, watch whether the Democratic midterm victory in the United States translates into a congressional push for more robust Ukraine funding — Democrats have historically been more hawkish on the issue than the Trump White House. And watch Russia's information posture: if Moscow is escalating the use of double-tap strikes and bodycam-visible atrocities, it may be calculating that Western war fatigue is approaching a threshold that makes a negotiated settlement on its terms achievable, a calculation that should be vigorously tested.
