Ukraine conducted simultaneous drone strikes on two major oil refineries deep inside Russian territory on Thursday, with President Volodymyr Zelensky confirming hits on the Bashneft-Novoil refinery in Russia's Bashkortostan republic — [approximately 1,300 kilometres from the Ukrainian border](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwymv212xrxo) — and the Slavneft-Yanos refinery in Yaroslavl, about 700 kilometres away. The Yaroslavl governor confirmed the Slavneft-Yanos facility was ablaze after what he described as "the largest enemy drone attack" on the region, with four people injured and 93 drones shot down over the area overnight. Ukraine also struck "two military patrol boats and shadow fleet vessels" in the Black Sea. Meanwhile, Russian attacks killed at least eleven people in Ukraine, including three in Balakliya, two at a train station in Lozova, and five in Dnipropetrovsk region. The southern city of Kherson suffered a complete electricity blackout after a critical infrastructure facility was struck, with internet connectivity falling to 31% at its lowest point.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of Ukraine's deep-strike campaign treats it as a legitimate and proportionate response to Russian aggression: Kyiv is targeting the economic infrastructure that funds Moscow's war machine, including the oil export revenues that have sustained Russia's military spending even under Western sanctions. The shadow fleet — the tankers Russia uses to illegally export sanctioned oil — represents a direct funding mechanism for the war. Hitting refineries, as Zelensky said, aims to "limit the oil revenues Moscow uses to finance" its operations. This is not terrorism; it is conventional military targeting of economic assets with direct military value. The counter-argument — that targeting Russian civilian infrastructure is escalatory — applies with equal force to Russia's own systematic campaign against Ukrainian power stations, hospitals, and water treatment facilities, a campaign that has been underway since the full-scale invasion in February 2022.

## A different read

All of that is true as far as it goes. The refinery campaign is strategically coherent. Disrupting Russian refining capacity raises the cost of domestic fuel production, complicates military logistics, and reduces export revenues. The 2014–2024 precedent of Western reluctance to allow Ukraine to use Western-supplied weapons for strikes inside Russia was arguably a strategic error: it created asymmetric escalation pressure in which Russia could freely target Ukrainian infrastructure while its own remained sanctuaried. That constraint has largely dissolved.

But there are three dimensions of the current campaign that deserve more attention than they receive in celebratory Western coverage.

First, the distance. A [1,300-kilometre drone strike](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwymv212xrxo) on Bashkortostan — deep in Russia's Ural region — represents a qualitative change in the conflict's geographic parameters. For most of modern European history, the concept of a "front line" implied a zone of relative safety for the civilian and industrial hinterland of both belligerents. Ukraine is challenging that concept systematically, which Russia will interpret (and has interpreted) as justification for its own deep strikes. The Kherson blackout and the simultaneous strikes across Ukrainian cities are Russia's answer. This cycle of reciprocal infrastructure destruction is likely to continue regardless of its utility, because both sides have concluded that they cannot afford to accept the constraint unilaterally.

Second, the munitions question. Ukraine's drone fleet is its comparative advantage in deep strikes — it is relatively cheap to manufacture and does not depend on Western precision munitions. But the air defence burden falls on those Western-supplied Patriot batteries and THAAD interceptors, of which [CSIS estimates the US has now expended roughly 60%](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o) during the Iran campaign. Russia intercepted or shot down 605 Ukrainian drones in a single night, according to its own military statement — a figure that, even discounted for propaganda, suggests massive expenditure of Russian air defence missiles. The Russian air defence stockpile is also finite; pressure on it has real strategic value.

Third, the static front line. Both Zelensky and the BBC have noted that "the vast front line remains virtually static" and all mediation efforts have produced no breakthrough. Deep strikes on economic infrastructure are, in part, a way to sustain strategic pressure and demonstrate continued capability in the absence of ground movement. They serve a political purpose — showing Western partners and the Ukrainian public that the war remains active and forward-leaning — as much as a strictly military one. That is not a criticism: wars are always partly political. But it means the refinery campaign should be evaluated partly on its political logic, not only its military economics.

The West has invested enormously in Ukraine's survival, and rightly so. The alternative — a Russian victory in Ukraine — would validate the proposition that territorial conquest through military force is a viable geopolitical strategy in twenty-first-century Europe, with consequences that would reverberate from Finland to Taiwan. But the investment requires honest accounting: what is the theory of victory, who pays for it, and what is the sustainable level of support for a conflict that has no visible diplomatic off-ramp?

## What to watch

- **Bashkortostan follow-up**: Whether Russia responds to the deep Ural strike with a qualitatively new category of attack inside Ukraine — a long-range missile barrage on Kyiv or a strike on decision-making infrastructure.
- **Black Sea shadow fleet**: Ukraine's continued targeting of the shadow fleet is a direct sanction-enforcement mechanism. Watch for insurance market and flag-state responses that could reinforce or undermine that effort.
- **Winter energy stocks**: With winter approaching in the Northern Hemisphere, the damage to Russian refining capacity will be felt in domestic heating fuel prices. Russia's ability to absorb that cost domestically is a metric of its economic resilience.
- **Western air defence transfers**: Trump's statement that a Ukraine Patriot transfer would be a "big step" suggests the decision remains live. A positive decision, given the strained US stockpiles, would be a significant political commitment.
