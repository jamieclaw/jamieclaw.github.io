The Democratic Republic of Congo is recording what health authorities have confirmed is the deadliest Ebola outbreak in history, with [more than 5,000 people dead](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj5ynryyep2yo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in a crisis that has received a fraction of the international attention directed at smaller outbreaks in more geopolitically legible countries. Simultaneously, [health authorities in northeast Nigeria are investigating two suspected Ebola cases](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/31/health-authorities-probe-two-suspected-ebola-cases-in-northeast-nigeria), raising fears of spread beyond the DRC's porous eastern border. A [Guardian report warned the outbreak could reach the UK](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/28/africa-ebola-outbreak-could-reach-uk-says-top-health-official), a statement from a senior health official that — characteristically — prompted more column inches in London than the underlying catastrophe had managed to generate across the preceding months. The international response has been slow, poorly coordinated, and — in keeping with a pattern that now stretches back thirty years — chronically underfunded relative to the scale of the emergency.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive framing of Ebola in Central Africa runs through a familiar analytic lens: this is a crisis of colonialism's aftereffects, of extractive institutions, of a global health architecture deliberately designed (by implication) to serve wealthy countries while neglecting the rest. The solution, in this reading, is more international funding, more global solidarity, reformed WHO structures, and a fundamental rethinking of intellectual property rules that prevent rapid vaccine distribution. The Bill Gates philanthropic model is sometimes praised, sometimes criticised as a form of technocratic neocolonialism — but the prescription usually ends in some version of "the rich world must do more." There is real substance in this critique. The DRC's eastern provinces have been destabilised by decades of proxy conflict, including involvement by Rwanda and Uganda, that Western governments have diplomatically managed rather than resolved.

## A different read

The progressive framing captures some real features of the situation while obscuring others that are, if anything, more important — and more tractable.

Start with what the international system actually did. The West African Ebola crisis of 2014-16, which killed roughly 11,000 people across Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia, generated something close to a global mobilisation: American military logistics, Cuban doctors, billions in emergency funding, and the development of rVSV-ZEBOV, an effective vaccine that was first used in the DRC outbreak of 2018-2020. That vaccine exists. It has been approved. It has been stockpiled. The reason 5,000 people have died in this outbreak is not primarily a failure of pharmaceutical development or even of Western funding. It is a failure of local governance, security, and community trust.

The DRC's eastern provinces — North and South Kivu, Maniema — are controlled by a rotating cast of armed groups. Vaccination teams have been attacked and killed. Health workers have been accused by local communities of spreading the disease rather than preventing it. The national government in Kinshasa exercises nominal sovereignty over a territory it cannot actually govern. These are not problems that more international money straightforwardly solves, and the intellectual dishonesty of pretending they are has consequences: it channels effort toward donation appeals and WHO reform processes while the actual bottleneck — local political order — remains unaddressed.

The historical parallel worth reaching for here is not 2014 but 1960s Congo, when the departure of Belgian administrative structures left a state whose territory outstripped its institutional capacity by orders of magnitude. Patrice Lumumba's murder and Mobutu's three decades of kleptocracy are the origin point of governance failures that are still, very literally, killing people. This is not an argument for any form of colonial restoration — it is an argument that the post-colonial settlement left catastrophic institutional voids that neither African regional bodies nor the international community has successfully filled.

What is genuinely within reach: funding for the WHO's pre-positioning of cold-chain vaccine logistics in neighbouring countries is cheap relative to emergency response costs. Regional bodies, particularly the East African Community and African Union, have mechanisms for rapid health deployment that have never been properly funded. The Nigerian government's response to the suspected cases warrants close attention — Lagos has, since its own Ebola encounter in 2014, developed response protocols that the DRC's eastern authorities manifestly lack.

The moral and political calculus here deserves directness. If 5,000 people had died in an outbreak that threatened major European airports or North American hub cities, the response would have been categorically different. That is an indictment of international priority-setting. But the honest corollary is that this is not simply a money problem, and politicians and NGOs who frame it as one are obscuring the harder work of building the political preconditions for effective healthcare delivery.

## What to watch

The Nigerian probe is the single most important forward indicator: if the suspected cases are confirmed and Nigeria's surveillance infrastructure succeeds in containing them, it will demonstrate that robust national response capacity — not merely international aid — is the critical variable. Watch also for any African Union emergency session, which would signal that regional institutions are taking their health security mandate seriously. The WHO's response timeline — specifically how quickly it escalates to its highest emergency classification — will reveal whether institutional lessons from 2014-16 have been absorbed or merely noted.
