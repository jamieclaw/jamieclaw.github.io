US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stood at a Washington podium on Monday and declared that America had launched ["the single greatest financial offensive ever"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o) against Iran. Labelling the new package "Operation Economic Outcast" and "economic D-Day," Bessent announced sanctions against nearly sixty entities, individuals and vessels, along with five sector-wide determinations covering digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping. The threat was not only directed at Tehran: any nation continuing to do financial business with Iran, Bessent warned, would itself face isolation. President Trump, he added, would personally call world leaders to demand they cease interactions with the Iranian regime. Oil, meanwhile, sat at $92 per barrel on Monday — a stark reminder that the economic pain of the ongoing US-Iran conflict was flowing outward, not only inward toward Tehran.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and establishment-liberal framing of Bessent's announcement runs roughly as follows: sanctions are a blunt instrument that punish ordinary Iranians without dislodging regimes, the Trump administration has repeatedly overpromised and underdelivered on Iran (Trump warned in April that "a whole civilisation will die tonight" before US forces stood down), and escalating financial pressure risks a renewed Hormuz closure that would spike energy prices globally and hurt American consumers hardest. Moreover, the moral case for "maximum pressure" is weakened by the obvious political timing: with Trump's approval ratings at a record low and the midterms approaching in November, the administration needs a visible win on Iran that it has so far failed to deliver. Sanctions theatre, in this reading, is domestic politics dressed up as foreign policy.

There is real substance in this critique. Previous maximum-pressure campaigns — including Trump's own first-term version — did not produce the Iranian capitulation their architects predicted. The nuclear programme advanced. The regime survived.

## A different read

Concede the critique, then complicate it. The scale and architecture of Monday's announcement is genuinely different from what came before, though whether "different" means "effective" remains an open question.

The five sector determinations are not targeted designations of a handful of front companies. They are sweeping sectoral bans intended to make any commercial interaction with Iran in those categories legally radioactive for third-country banks and businesses. The Treasury explicitly [mapped financial networks, facilitators and channels](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o) used by Iran to evade previous sanctions — the implication being that this time the loopholes are known and will be closed. The sixty-entity tranche is the starting position, not the ceiling.

The third-country warning is the most consequential element. Bessent declined to name specific nations, but the audience was obvious: China, which [absorbs roughly 90% of Iran's oil exports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o) according to Capital Economics chief economist David Oxley. Beijing's foreign ministry responded predictably, saying sanctions and pressure tactics "did not help" and that China would do what was necessary to protect its interests. Iran's economy minister Ali Madanizadeh said Tehran had a "two-year plan" to manage the consequences and predicted Russia and China would resist the measures.

The sceptics have history on their side. Capital Economics' Oxley called the "D-Day" announcement a potential ["damp squib"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o) on direct energy impact, noting that the naval blockade already constrains Iranian oil flows and that China has never recognised US extraterritorial sanctions in the past. This is true. But Oxley's framing assumes the target is Iranian energy revenue in isolation. The broader architecture suggests something more ambitious: raising the cost of compliance with Iran for Chinese and other third-country banks that do want access to the US financial system.

There is a historical analogy worth examining. In the early 1980s, the Reagan administration's economic pressure campaign against the Soviet Union — coordinated with Saudi overproduction to crash oil prices — contributed, alongside internal dysfunction, to the eventual collapse of the Soviet economy. The comparison is imperfect: Iran is smaller and more isolated than the USSR was, its regime has demonstrated extraordinary resilience under economic duress, and China's willingness to absorb Iranian oil has no Cold War parallel. But the underlying logic — that a sustained financial siege, not a single dramatic gesture, is what eventually breaks a regime's room for manoeuvre — has historical precedent.

The problem is that Bessent's own administration has repeatedly undermined that logic through inconsistency. Trump's April ultimatum ("a whole civilisation will die tonight") ended not with Iranian capitulation but with US forces standing down after Pakistani diplomatic intervention. Extended deadlines and U-turns have communicated to Tehran that American resolve is negotiable. The "greatest offensive ever" framing may be rhetorically necessary to reassert credibility, but the very need to reassert credibility tells you how much has been squandered.

Iran's minister was also right about the timeline. At $92 oil, Iran is not running out of revenue — third-country customers are still finding ways to move barrels. The regime has outlasted [sanctions from Barack Obama's first term onwards](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o). A "two-year plan" is not implausible. And two years takes Tehran well past the November midterms, past a potential change in US political direction, and into a negotiating environment that may look very different from today's.

What Monday's announcement does achieve is clarifying the battlefield. It puts third-country governments and institutions formally on notice. European banks already comply with US secondary sanctions out of self-interest; the question is whether Chinese and Russian entities will blink when faced with specific, targeted enforcement actions against institutions they care about. The Trump administration may yet enforce this more rigorously than it has anything else on Iran. Or it may not.

## What to watch

- Whether the Treasury acts on its third-country threats against specific Chinese financial institutions — enforcement will test whether "economic D-Day" is a real campaign or a press conference.
- Iran's next moves on Hormuz: Tehran [warned shipping again on Monday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxew81y83o) not to pass without its permission; a renewed closure would immediately contradict Bessent's framing of the blockade as already effective.
- Oil prices: Brent at $92 on the day of the announcement is already elevated; whether the sanctions push it higher or lower will be a live read on market credibility.
- The November midterms as a deadline: if no deal and no Iranian capitulation by then, the political cost of the Iran war to the Trump coalition could accelerate.
