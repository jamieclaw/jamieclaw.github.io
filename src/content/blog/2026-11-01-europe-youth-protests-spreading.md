Hundreds of thousands of demonstrators filled the streets of Paris on Friday and into the weekend, with [protests spreading to Germany and other European capitals](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/31/france-germany-protests-paris-europe-latest-news-updates) in what observers are calling the continent's most significant outbreak of civil unrest in a decade. [Clashes between police and masked rioters](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/31/police-clash-rioters-after-largely-peaceful-protests-across-france) followed largely peaceful marches, while [NPR described the movement as youth-led and spreading rapidly](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/31/g-s1-151121/france-seethes-again-as-youth-led-protests-sweep-europe). The immediate trigger varies by city — pension reform frustration in Paris, housing costs in Berlin — but the underlying current is identical: a generation that was promised prosperity through education and openness and has instead inherited stagnant wages, unaffordable cities, and a political class that appears congenitally incapable of honest accounting.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream European press and most progressive commentators will read this week's protests as a vindication of their own preferred agenda. Young people, they will argue, are demanding more: more social spending, more housing investment, more redistribution from the wealthy to the precarious. The far right, in this reading, is the great threat lurking at the margins of the demonstrations, ready to hijack legitimate grievance into nativist reaction. The solution, therefore, is obvious — give the protesters what they want, or at least appear to. President Macron's government has form here: respond with solemn national consultations, announce committees, deploy empathetic language, and wait for the energy to dissipate. [The BBC noted the breadth of the demonstrations across Europe](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/ck0j0y87j303o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), framing them as a democratic pressure valve functioning as intended.

## A different read

The mainstream framing gets the symptom right but misidentifies the disease — and therefore prescribes remedies that will accelerate rather than arrest the decay.

Consider what European governments have actually tried. France's social spending as a share of GDP is among the highest in the developed world — above 30 percent for much of the last two decades. Germany has run the most generous unemployment and retraining programmes on the continent. The EU has transferred hundreds of billions in cohesion funds to its poorer regions. If government expenditure solved legitimacy crises, these protests would not be happening.

What is actually failing is not the quantity of state intervention but its quality — and, more damagingly, its honesty. European governments have been running what might be called a confidence trick in slow motion: promising entitlement-state permanence while quietly loading the bill onto younger cohorts through pension system liabilities, deferred infrastructure spending, and housing supply restrictions that preserve wealth for the already-propertied. The young people in the streets have not been radicalised by right-wing populism; they have done arithmetic.

The historical parallel that European elites are most reluctant to confront is not 1968 — the romantic year of student barricades that ultimately changed very little and which the French establishment absorbed through co-optation — but rather the long Italian malaise of the 1980s and 1990s. There, a system of guaranteed positions, clientelism, and institutional self-protection created an economy so closed to young entrants that entire generations simply left. [Al Jazeera's reporting on the scale of French demonstrations](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/31/tension-and-clashes-as-hundreds-of-thousands-protest-again-in-france) captures the sheer persistence of this mobilisation; this is not a single weekend of cathartic rage but a sustained campaign with organisational memory.

The political danger is real and runs in both directions. A cynical dismissal of the protests — "they're just students who want someone else to pay for their lifestyle" — misses the genuine economic grievance at the core. But an uncritical embrace — "we must simply spend more, borrow more, regulate more" — simply kicks the structural problem into the next electoral cycle while delivering short-term concessions that cannot be sustained.

What neither Macron in France, nor Scholz's successors in Germany, nor the broader European centre has managed to articulate is a credible story about how the continent will generate the growth and housing supply that would actually address the underlying conditions. Planning reform, pension restructuring that shares pain across generations rather than concentrating it on the young, and supply-side housing policy are not items that photograph well at protest marches. But they are the only routes out.

The right-of-centre reading here is not that these protesters should be suppressed or dismissed. It is that they are being systematically deceived by the political forces claiming to be on their side — forces that have had power for large parts of the last thirty years and used it to entrench the very rigidities now crushing their constituents' children.

## What to watch

Four signals will determine whether this wave of unrest produces genuine institutional reform or simply further populist realignment. First, watch whether any major European government produces a credible housing supply commitment — not a committee, a commitment — within the next three months. Second, track Le Pen's polling in France; [her presidential campaign launch](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmeweppr990qo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) was timed for precisely this moment of centre exhaustion. Third, observe whether the German government, already weakened, moves toward early elections rather than attempting to absorb the protests through another grand coalition. Fourth, and most tellingly, watch whether the protests themselves maintain their non-partisan character or begin to be captured by organised political structures on either flank.
