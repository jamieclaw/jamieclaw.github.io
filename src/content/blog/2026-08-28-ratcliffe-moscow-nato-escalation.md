CIA Director John Ratcliffe flew secretly to Moscow on Tuesday aboard a US military aircraft, where he met his Russian counterpart Sergei Naryshkin of the SVR. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z8l8xdg2o) that the visit followed a US intelligence assessment that Russia may be planning a limited assault on a NATO ally — most likely a Baltic state — possibly ranging from a sophisticated cyber-attack to a small-scale ground incursion. President Trump described the trip as "semi-routine" and later told reporters Ratcliffe had arrived with "no message," while simultaneously saying of Russia: "They're not going to attack." [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/27/britain-ukraine-war-russia) that a separate assessment revealed a "beyond critical" shortage of Patriot missile interceptors in Europe, driven partly by the US campaign against Iran, leaving NATO exposed to precisely the high-speed ballistic missiles Russia has been deploying against Ukraine. Meanwhile, Russia's foreign ministry warned Britain it was "playing with fire" after Prime Minister Andy Burnham pledged to share Storm Shadow cruise-missile technology with Kyiv, and a drone found at Leipzig airport carrying explosives was assessed by security experts as probably Russian in origin.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream interpretation of the Ratcliffe visit is reassuring: a strong America is keeping its deterrence commitments, the CIA chief went to Moscow to deliver a firm warning, and the fact of the meeting itself demonstrates that Washington's back-channel diplomacy remains functional even in a second Trump term. Progressive commentary adds that this is precisely the kind of responsible, adult statecraft that critics feared Trump would abandon — the National Security Council is working, the intelligence community is engaged, and the alliance holds. The Patriot shortage, in this view, is a manageable logistics problem that pre-positions the case for defence spending increases. Russia's hybrid provocations — drones, arson at munitions depots, disinformation — are irritants, not preludes to open war. The Kremlin is rational; it knows an Article 5 invocation would be catastrophic.

## A different read

The reassuring framing skips a crucial detail: the Patriot shortage is not an abstract budget concern, it is a live operational constraint documented by serving American and NATO officials speaking on condition of anonymity. [The Guardian's sourcing](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/27/britain-ukraine-war-russia) is specific — a US defence official in Europe and a NATO official both confirm the low inventories, and both connect the shortfall directly to Trump's war with Iran draining intercept stocks. That is a geopolitically uncomfortable admission: two simultaneous confrontations — Russia-Ukraine and the US-Iran standoff — are cannibalising the same limited supply of advanced interceptors. The Soviets had a name for this kind of strategic overextension. They also had a doctrinal appreciation of precisely when to probe.

The parallel to 2021 is worth lingering on. [The BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z8l8xdg2o) that the last time a CIA director made this journey was when William Burns flew to Moscow as Russia was quietly drawing up its invasion of Ukraine. Burns warned; the Kremlin denied any intent; the invasion came three months later. The warning-denial cycle is identical. One should not conclude that history must repeat exactly — Russia's position in 2026 is materially different, its economy more attrited, its manpower depleted. But the structural logic Putin is likely running is also not hard to model: Ukrainian drone strikes have humiliated the Kremlin on its home soil, cutting oil-refinery output and creating petrol queues visible to ordinary Russians. Domestic pressure to "do something" is real. And if Trump's periodic disdain for NATO leads Moscow to believe Article 5 is a paper guarantee, the temptation of a limited probe in, say, Estonia's Russian-speaking northeast, or a coordinated hybrid attack across the Suwalki Gap, becomes calculable.

The Suwalki Gap — the 60-mile strip of land separating Kaliningrad from Belarus — is the most dangerous geography in Europe. Seizing it would cut the Baltic states from their NATO land links. Poland deploying its new South Korean Black Panther tanks near the Kaliningrad border this week is not a coincidence; Warsaw is doing the operational planning that the Ratcliffe visit was supposed to render unnecessary. Polish deputy defence minister Cezary Tomczyk said this month his country is "preparing for war, hoping it never happens." That is not the language of a reassured ally.

There is also something telling in Trump's flat, almost bored denials. A president who wanted to maximise deterrence would not publicly say there is "nothing unusual" about a CIA director's emergency flight to Moscow. The very casualness of the denial may be the most dangerous signal Washington is sending. Deterrence is psychological; it depends on adversaries believing that the defender means what it says. When the defender publicly minimises his own warning, he partially cancels it. Trump may be doing this to manage a domestic audience; the problem is that the audience in Moscow is also watching.

This is not to say war is imminent or inevitable. Russia's military is overstretched, its officer corps thinned by attrition, its logistics creaking under sanctions-driven parts shortages. But "not imminent" and "not being tested" are different assessments. The pattern of Russian hybrid provocations across Europe — arson at a German munitions warehouse, suspected drones over British military sites, systematic cyber intrusions — looks less like independent incidents and more like a deliberate probing of response thresholds. History's lesson is that this kind of probing is most dangerous when the prober believes the defender's red lines are ambiguous.

## What to watch

Watch Friday for any NATO emergency consultations or public statements from Baltic defence ministers following the Ratcliffe trip — silence will be as revealing as alarm. Track Patriot interceptor deliveries from the US domestic stockpile to European theatre: if the Pentagon cannot or will not resupply before autumn, the deterrence gap becomes measurable. Watch whether the Leipzig drone is officially attributed to Russia — formal attribution would trigger a different escalatory ladder than treating it as deniable. And watch the Warsh speech at Jackson Hole for any signal that US bond markets are beginning to price a European-security premium into US debt; that would be the economic system's own warning light.
