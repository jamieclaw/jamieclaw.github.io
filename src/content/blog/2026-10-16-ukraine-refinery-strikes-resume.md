Ukraine resumed long-range drone and missile strikes against Russian oil refinery facilities on October 15, according to [Ukrainian and US officials](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/g-s1-148194/russia-ukraine-war), ending a brief informal pause in attacks on energy infrastructure. Russia simultaneously accused Ukraine of striking a refinery in Saratov and confirmed resumed Ukrainian offensive operations. The renewed strikes come as Russia and Ukraine have been trading blows on multiple fronts, with a [BBC report noting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqwyrnwd9dw1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that Moscow has accused Kyiv of attacking an oil refinery even as Zelensky publicly announced that energy-sector strikes would resume. The development ends a fragile interlude in the war over economic infrastructure and raises the stakes for any future ceasefire framework that attempts to ring-fence civilian energy systems from military targeting.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of Ukraine's resumed refinery strikes is sympathetic bordering on celebratory: Kyiv is exercising its sovereign right to degrade Russian war-making capacity, striking the logistical arteries that fund and fuel Putin's army. Oil revenue is, after all, what sustains Russia's defence budget. Western commentators have broadly applauded Ukraine's long-range drone programme as a cost-effective equaliser against Russian conventional superiority. The ceasefire pause, in this telling, was a gesture of restraint that Russia immediately squandered by continuing its own bombardment of Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure. The logic is simple: if Moscow will not reciprocate, why should Kyiv hold back? This position has the advantage of moral clarity and strategic coherence — it is hard to argue that Ukraine must unilaterally spare Russian refineries while Russian glide bombs continue to fall on Zaporizhzhia. The liberal internationalist consensus holds that hitting Russian oil production is both legally permissible under the laws of armed conflict and strategically rational.

## A different read

The problem with the "hit the refineries" framework is not that it is wrong about Russia's bad faith — Moscow has indeed continued attacking Ukrainian civilian infrastructure throughout any informal pause — but that it mistakes tactical logic for strategic wisdom. Energy infrastructure strikes create path dependencies that are extraordinarily difficult to reverse.

Consider the historical precedent. The Allied strategic bombing campaigns of World War Two targeted German synthetic oil production with enormous effect: by mid-1944, Luftwaffe pilot training had been curtailed because of aviation fuel shortages. But the lesson drawn from Ploesti and Leuna was also that bombing alone rarely compels a regime to sue for peace when existential stakes are involved. Russia's oil economy is vast, its refinery network distributed, and its capacity for improvised repair repeatedly underestimated by Western analysts. [NPR's coverage](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/g-s1-148194/russia-ukraine-war) notes that Ukraine resumed attacks "after a brief truce," implying the truce was itself something more than a unilateral pause — a signal, however fragile, that both sides recognised the civilian cost of energy warfare.

The deeper strategic question is what end state these strikes are optimising for. If the goal is a negotiated settlement — which every serious analyst acknowledges must eventually occur — then destroying Russian refinery capacity creates a Russian incentive to negotiate from desperation rather than calculation, which historically produces worse, not better, peace terms. Germany in November 1918 is instructive: a collapse of the home front produced an armistice, but also a stab-in-the-back mythology that poisoned Weimar politics for fifteen years. An agreement extracted from a Russia whose economy has been systematically degraded may prove less durable than one built on mutual strategic exhaustion.

There is also the question of precedent and escalation norms. Ukraine's drone programme has been remarkably sophisticated, striking targets hundreds of kilometres inside Russian territory. [The BBC's reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqwyrnwd9dw1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) frames Russian accusations of refinery strikes as tactical information in a war of narratives. But each expansion of the targeting envelope — from frontline military positions to logistics hubs to oil refineries to, eventually, what? — chips away at the informal norms that have so far prevented direct NATO-Russia confrontation. The West's stated position remains that it does not seek regime change in Moscow and does not want escalation to nuclear use. Systematically targeting the economic foundations of the Russian state strains the credibility of that position.

None of this is an argument for Ukrainian restraint as a matter of moral principle. It is an argument that the strategic case for refinery strikes, compelling as it sounds at the level of quarterly earnings reports and fuel supply chains, has not been adequately stress-tested against the question of what kind of Russia emerges from this war, and whether the West has thought carefully about living with that Russia for the next half-century.

## What to watch

- Whether Russia retaliates with a matching expansion of targeting against Ukrainian civilian infrastructure — particularly electrical grid assets ahead of winter — as a direct response to the refinery strikes resuming.
- The pace of Western weapons deliveries, specifically long-range systems: if Ukraine is to sustain the refinery campaign, it needs munitions, and resupply politics in Washington and Berlin remain volatile.
- Any signal from Washington that it is quietly pressuring Kyiv to pause strikes as a diplomatic sweetener in back-channel conversations with Moscow — the Trump administration's incentive structure on this point is opaque.
- European energy prices: Russian refinery disruptions ripple through global oil markets, and a spike in heating fuel costs this winter would have direct political consequences for European governments supporting Ukraine.
