President Donald Trump warned on Wednesday that the United States would target Iran's bridges and power plants if attacks on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz continued, [according to BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cdrv0p37k8jo). The threat came as Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth testified before Congress that the Iran conflict had cost American taxpayers $37.5 billion so far, while simultaneously requesting billions more in supplemental funding to sustain operations, [according to BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gek2kqyno). On the same day, the House of Representatives passed a Pentagon funding bill that included a framework to unlock additional emergency appropriations for the war effort, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/22/nx-s1-5903130/house-vote-iran-war-funding-reconciliation). Iranian missiles have meanwhile demonstrated what analysts described as "deadly precision" in recent strikes on US-allied facilities, and Tehran continues to warn of broader regional turmoil if the conflict does not de-escalate, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/22/iranian-missiles-show-deadly-precision-amid-us-iran-war-escalation).

## The received wisdom

The mainstream case for the US posture runs something like this: Iran has been the principal destabilising force in the Middle East for forty years. Its nuclear programme, its Houthi proxies throttling global shipping lanes, its missile strikes on American personnel — all of it represents an accumulation of aggression that previous administrations chose to manage through diplomacy and sanctions rather than confront directly. Trump, for all his chaos, has finally forced a reckoning. The $37.5 billion is a down payment on regional stability that diplomacy could never purchase. Critics who flinch at infrastructure targeting need to reckon with the fact that Iran's bridges and power grids are what sustain its war machine, and the civilian suffering of Iranians under theocratic misrule is not America's doing. The responsible argument, in this framing, is not to pull back but to press forward with sufficient resolve to compel Iranian capitulation before the costs compound further.

This is not a dishonest argument. It has serious people behind it, and the history of half-measures against Iran — from Carter to Obama — is not a reassuring one.

## A different read

The problem with the "press to victory" logic is that it has been the justifying frame for nearly every American military escalation of the past quarter-century, and it has a poor track record.

Start with the numbers. Hegseth's $37.5 billion in three weeks of operations — [reported by BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gek2kqyno) — is running at roughly $1.75 billion per day. The entire Iraq War, from 2003 to 2011, cost approximately $2 trillion by the time interest on borrowed money is included, or about $680 million per day in its peak years. The Iran conflict is already burning through money at two-and-a-half times that rate. America borrowed its way through Iraq on the assumption of a short war; there is no evidence the fiscal calculus is more favourable now.

The infrastructure threat is the more strategically troubling development. Targeting bridges and power plants is not a new American tactic — the United States bombed Serbian infrastructure in 1999 and Iraqi power grids in 1991. The immediate military rationale is defensible in each case. But the secondary effects are harder to control. Civilian populations bear the brunt. International legitimacy erodes. And in Iran's case, the regime has spent decades cultivating a domestic narrative of foreign persecution; nothing reinforces that narrative like images of darkened hospitals and flooded basements. If the goal is to separate the Iranian people from the Islamic Republic — the precondition for any sustainable outcome — bombing their electricity supply is a peculiarly counterproductive instrument.

There is also the question of regional contagion. Iran has warned of broader turmoil if the conflict continues, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/22/us-launches-new-strikes-as-iran-warns-of-regional-turmoil). That warning is not empty. Iran retains meaningful influence over militias in Iraq, Syria and Lebanon; over the Houthis whose tanker attacks are already redirecting global shipping; and over Shia communities in Bahrain and eastern Saudi Arabia. A conflict that begins as US-Iran bilateral quickly becomes a regional conflagration the United States is not prepared to manage. The House vote to unlock further emergency funding [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/22/nx-s1-5903130/house-vote-iran-war-funding-reconciliation) tells you something: Congress is treating this as an indefinite commitment rather than a short, sharp corrective.

The deeper historical parallel is Korea in autumn 1950. After the initial Inchon success, there was a logic to pressing north — push hard enough and you win decisively. The result was a Chinese intervention that turned a containable conflict into a three-year war and ultimately a frozen armistice that still hasn't ended. The temptation to escalate just one more level is a constant in American military history. Each escalatory rung looks rational in isolation. The ladder, taken whole, leads somewhere no one planned.

None of this means the original decision to confront Iran was wrong. The Strait of Hormuz situation was genuinely untenable. But "the threat was real" is not the same as "this particular response is working." The discipline to distinguish between those two claims is precisely what American statecraft keeps losing.

## What to watch

Watch whether Trump's infrastructure threat translates into actual strikes, or whether it functions primarily as coercive signalling. The distinction matters enormously for escalation dynamics. Watch oil prices: every sustained move above $90 tightens the fiscal vice on US consumer spending and complicates the Federal Reserve's options. Watch the emergency supplemental appropriation as it moves through the Senate — the specific conditions attached to that funding will reveal how much strategic oversight Congress intends to exercise. Finally, watch Iranian diplomatic back-channels through Oman and Qatar; Tehran has consistently maintained that off-ramps remain open even as its missiles fly.
