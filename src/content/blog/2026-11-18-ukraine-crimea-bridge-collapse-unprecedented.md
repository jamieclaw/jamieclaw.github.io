Ukraine struck the Kerch Bridge — the 19-kilometre span connecting Russia's Krasnodar region to occupied Crimea — in what President Volodymyr Zelensky called an "[unprecedented](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw772jk4rvmxo)" attack on Tuesday, causing a partial structural collapse. [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw772jk4rvmxo) that the bridge, which Vladimir Putin personally inaugurated in 2018 as a symbol of Russia's permanent claim to the peninsula, sustained significant damage to at least one road span, with footage verified by open-source intelligence analysts showing a section of the roadway fallen into the Kerch Strait. [A second BBC report](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crz9dgz28zwdo) detailed how the attack unfolded across multiple drone and missile salvos. Russian emergency services confirmed the closure of the bridge to vehicular traffic, and several civilians sheltering nearby were [reported to describe the experience as "really scary."](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq7825234lgmo)

## The received wisdom

For the Ukrainian government and its Western supporters, the Kerch Bridge strike is exactly the kind of blow that justifies continued weapons supply, training, and financial assistance. The bridge has been a primary logistics artery for Russian forces in Crimea and southern Ukraine, as well as a propaganda trophy of historic significance to Putin personally. Its partial destruction, following a previous attack in October 2022 that damaged but did not collapse it, demonstrates the growing sophistication of Ukraine's long-range strike capabilities and its ability to reach targets deep in Russian-held territory. Western analysts and politicians who have argued for more and better weapons will point to this as proof of concept. The humanitarian resonance — a people defending their territory striking a symbol of illegal annexation — is powerful and legitimate.

## A different read

Let us grant the genuine significance of what Ukraine has achieved. Striking the Kerch Bridge is a remarkable feat of military ingenuity against a heavily defended target, and the logistics disruption to Russian supply lines into Crimea is real. But there is a persistent pattern in this war that Tuesday's images should not be allowed to obscure: Ukraine's capacity to strike dramatic symbolic targets has not translated into the kind of territorial reconquest that would change the fundamental character of the conflict.

The bridge has now been struck twice in four years. After the 2022 attack, Russia repaired it within months, rerouted supply lines through the Azov coast, and adapted. [The BBC's account](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crz9dgz28zwdo) of Tuesday's attack describes it as more severe, with a road span actually fallen — but Russian engineers have already been mobilised, and the resilience demonstrated after 2022 gives little reason to assume the disruption will be permanent. The Soviet and Russian military tradition has always prized logistical redundancy; the construction of the land bridge through Mariupol and Melitopol that began in 2022 was precisely the adaptation to Kerch Bridge vulnerability.

The deeper historical parallel here is Eisenhower's pre-D-Day strategic bombing campaign against German transportation infrastructure in France — a campaign that succeeded brilliantly in its tactical objectives but was embedded in a broader ground campaign that actually determined the war's outcome. Infrastructure strikes are enablers, not war-winners. The question for Western capitals is whether the current level of support for Ukraine constitutes an enabler of a war-winning strategy, or whether it is enabling a prolonged and devastating stalemate.

There is a harder question that the bridge imagery tends to preclude: what is Ukraine's realistic theory of victory? The honest right-of-centre assessment — one that starts from a deep sympathy for Ukraine's national survival and a conviction that Putin's aggression must not be rewarded — nevertheless has to acknowledge that spectacular strikes on iconic targets can substitute for strategic clarity in ways that are politically convenient but ultimately costly. The resources and political will required to sustain this war are not infinite. Each dramatic image buys time and support; it does not substitute for a negotiated framework that freezes a conflict Ukraine cannot currently win militarily. That is an uncomfortable thing to say, but it has the advantage of being true.

[Zelensky's characterisation](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw772jk4rvmxo) of the attack as "unprecedented" is accurate in the specific sense. It may also reflect the political imperative to generate moments of visible progress for a domestic and international audience growing weary of an unresolved conflict. The temptation to substitute spectacle for strategy is not unique to Kyiv; it has been the besetting sin of democratic war-making since at least the Vietnam era.

## What to watch

The key indicators to watch: whether Russia retaliates with a large-scale strike on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure — the pattern after previous Ukrainian attacks on Crimea; whether the structural damage proves repairable within weeks (as in 2022) or months (suggesting more serious supply disruption); and whether this strike prompts any shift in Western policy on long-range weapons supply, particularly the ongoing debate about ATACMS range restrictions. If Russia's response is disproportionate and targets civilian energy infrastructure, the humanitarian optics will shift back to Kyiv's favour — which may partly explain the timing.
