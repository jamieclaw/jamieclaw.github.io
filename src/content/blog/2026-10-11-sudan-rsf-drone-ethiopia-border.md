A [drone strike attributed to Sudan's Rapid Support Forces](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8vgx9ew571wo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) killed at least forty-one people near the Ethiopian border on Saturday, according to a rights group cited by the BBC. The attack struck a location close to the Sudanese-Ethiopian frontier in circumstances that suggest either deliberate targeting of civilian communities or a catastrophic failure of targeting discipline. The RSF — the paramilitary force that fought alongside the Sudanese Armed Forces until the two turned on each other in April 2023 — has been using commercial-grade and military drones throughout the conflict, [a pattern documented by multiple human rights organisations](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8vgx9ew571wo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Sudan's war is now in its eighteenth month of full-scale fighting, has displaced an estimated ten million people, and has received only intermittent Western media attention compared to the conflicts in Ukraine and Gaza.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and progressive framing of Sudan is consistent and largely correct in its factual premises: the RSF, which grew directly out of the Janjaweed militias that committed atrocities in Darfur in the early 2000s, is conducting what multiple UN officials have characterised as ethnically targeted violence in Darfur and other western Sudanese regions. The international community's failure to intervene — or even to sustain meaningful diplomatic pressure — reflects a combination of great-power competition (Russia and the UAE have both provided the RSF with material support), donor fatigue, and the structural limits of an international humanitarian system that was never designed to handle multiple simultaneous mass-atrocity events. The argument that the West's fixation on Ukraine has drained diplomatic bandwidth and financial resources from African crises is made in good faith and deserves a serious response.

## A different read

That response begins with an uncomfortable observation: the international community's track record of armed intervention in African civil wars is not encouraging, and the progressive demand for "action" often implicitly means Western military intervention without specifying what form, at what cost, and with what realistic prospect of improving conditions for civilians.

The Sudan case is particularly complex. The conflict is not a simple dichotomy between government forces and a rebel insurgency. It is a war between two former partners — the SAF and the RSF — each of which has committed serious abuses, each of which is backed by external patrons with conflicting interests, and each of which believes it can win militarily. The RSF's [drone strikes near Ethiopia](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8vgx9ew571wo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) represent an escalation in geographic scope that carries a specific danger: Ethiopia is already managing the aftermath of its own Tigray conflict and contains the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, whose construction remains a source of deep Egyptian and Sudanese tension. A spillover of drone warfare into Ethiopian territory — even unintentionally — could trigger a chain of responses that the current conflict has not yet produced.

The drone dimension of this story matters beyond the immediate death toll. The proliferation of armed drones to non-state and paramilitary actors across Africa, the Middle East, and Central Asia represents one of the most significant shifts in the military balance of the past decade. Commercial drone platforms modified for weapons delivery are cheap, require minimal training to operate, and are essentially undetectable by the radar networks that most African states possess. The RSF's use of drones at scale follows similar patterns in Libya, Ethiopia's own Tigray conflict, and Mozambique's Cabo Delgado insurgency. What is new in Sudan is the combination of scale, systematic civilian targeting, and the geographic reach that threatens to draw in neighbours.

Western governments have largely responded to Sudan with statements of concern, arms embargoes that are patchily enforced, and humanitarian funding that is chronically under-subscribed. The diplomatic framework — the AU-mediated Jeddah talks — [has stalled repeatedly](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/10/yemeni-governments-military-says-its-forces-advancing-against-houthis?traffic_source=rss). The uncomfortable truth is that neither side has yet reached the point of military exhaustion that historically produces the conditions for negotiation. Until they do, the civilian death toll will continue to mount, and the risk of regional spillover will grow.

## What to watch

- Ethiopian government statements: any formal protest or border reinforcement by Addis Ababa would signal that the conflict is acquiring a cross-border dimension that cannot be contained diplomatically.
- UAE and Saudi positioning: both Gulf states have significant leverage over the RSF through financial flows and arms supply channels; any shift in their posture would affect the military balance.
- International Court of Justice proceedings on Darfur: the long-dormant ICC warrants from the Darfur era created precedents; watch whether the current conflict generates new referrals that could affect RSF command accountability.
- Humanitarian corridor negotiations: with displacement now exceeding ten million, the logistics of aid delivery are becoming impossible — any breakthrough on humanitarian access would be significant.
