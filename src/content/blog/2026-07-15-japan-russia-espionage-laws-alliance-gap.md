Japan has acknowledged the need to strengthen its counterintelligence framework after a [New York Times investigation revealed that Russia had turned the country into a regional espionage hub](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/russia-japan-den-spies-nyt-investigation), exploiting Tokyo's comparatively lenient espionage laws to conduct intelligence operations targeting the West and to procure dual-use technology for the Ukraine war. The reporting describes Russian intelligence using Japanese-resident operatives and front companies to acquire electronics components, semiconductor equipment, and other materials subject to export controls — routing them through a country with no general espionage statute and limited prosecution history for foreign intelligence activity on its soil. Japan's government has confirmed the basic outline of the findings and indicated that legislative remedies are under consideration, though no timetable has been offered. The revelations come as Japan has simultaneously moved to increase its defence budget toward 2% of GDP and deepen security cooperation with NATO partners — a jarring contrast between strategic intent and legal infrastructure.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and realist critique of Japan's espionage laws has long held that Tokyo's reluctance to criminalise foreign intelligence collection reflects a healthy sensitivity to its own wartime history of political police abuse and the postwar constitutional commitment to civil liberties. Under Article 21 of the Japanese constitution, freedom of speech and association receive strong protections, and successive governments have been extremely cautious about any legislation that might be used to criminalise journalism, political dissent, or ethnic-minority communities. The 2017 "Conspiracy Law" — which criminalised planning of serious crimes including some terrorism offences — was massively controversial for exactly this reason, with critics arguing it gave the state overly broad surveillance powers. The argument, then, is that Japan's legal "weakness" on espionage is not an oversight but a considered democratic choice rooted in legitimate historical trauma; and that strengthening it risks creating the kind of security state architecture that can be turned inward.

## A different read

The civil liberties concern is real and should be taken seriously in any legislative drafting. But the current situation — in which Japan, a formal US treaty ally, is being used as a procurement and intelligence hub for an adversary actively fighting a war against a country the G7 has collectively committed to supporting — is not a minor inconvenience. It is a serious strategic liability that calls for a calibrated response, not a shrug.

The legal gap is genuine. [Japan has no general espionage statute](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/russia-japan-den-spies-nyt-investigation) in the manner of the UK's Official Secrets Act, the US Espionage Act, or Germany's §99 StGB (foreign intelligence agent offence). Foreign nationals conducting intelligence operations for hostile states on Japanese soil can, in many circumstances, only be charged with immigration violations, fraud, or specific export control breaches — charges that carry minimal sentences and do not reflect the actual gravity of the offence. This has been known to Japanese intelligence professionals for decades; it was known to allied services as well. The question is why nothing was done before a New York Times exposé forced the issue onto the public agenda.

Part of the answer lies in postwar Japan's institutional DNA. [The 1945–1952 occupation deliberately dismantled state security infrastructure](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world/asia-pacific/japan-postwar-intelligence-history) and shaped a political culture in which intelligence agencies are kept deliberately weak and underfunded — a feature of the constitutional order that served US strategic interests during the Cold War (when Washington wanted Japanese strategic dependence, not strategic autonomy) but has become a bug now that the alliance is under stress. Japan's own security bureaucracy has internalised this weakness as normal; the Kantei has historically relied on the US for intelligence product rather than generating its own.

The dual-use technology procurement angle is the most immediately actionable concern. Semiconductors, machine tools, and electronics manufactured in Japan or transiting through it are subject to Japanese export control law, but enforcement has historically been patchy — [the same problem Germany faced with covert Soviet procurement in the 1970s and 1980s](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/russia-japan-den-spies-nyt-investigation). Germany eventually tightened its regime under pressure from the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls; Japan is likely to face similar pressure now from the US and UK. The question is whether Tokyo will accept G7-coordinated enforcement obligations or defend its commercial-first approach to export regulation.

The broader lesson is one about alliance architecture: a chain of democratic security relationships is only as strong as its weakest legal link, and the weakest links are almost always countries with strong civil liberties traditions and weak enforcement cultures — Japan, Luxembourg, Cyprus, historically the Netherlands. Adversaries have understood this for decades; it is the democratic alliance that is still learning.

There is also a domestic accountability story worth noting: the fact that this came out via a US newspaper, rather than a Japanese Diet investigation or government disclosure, raises questions about parliamentary oversight of the security agencies. The absence of a robust committee structure for intelligence oversight in Japan means there is no institutional mechanism to surface and act on exactly this kind of systemic vulnerability before it reaches crisis level.

## What to watch

Watch the **Diet's legislative calendar this autumn**: whether the Kishida government successor introduces a general espionage statute or limits itself to export control tightening will determine whether Japan is genuinely addressing the structural problem or offering symbolic gestures. Watch **US pressure in alliance forums**: if Washington begins conditioning intelligence-sharing or joint exercise access on Japanese legislative reform, the timeline accelerates dramatically. Watch **arrests or prosecutions under existing law**: if Japanese authorities begin using immigration and export control charges more aggressively against known Russian-linked actors, it signals an enforcement-first approach while legislation is pending. And watch **China's reaction**: Beijing has its own well-developed Japan-based intelligence apparatus and will be watching Tokyo's legal response with considerable interest.
