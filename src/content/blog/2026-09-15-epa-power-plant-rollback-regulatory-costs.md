The Trump administration on Monday [repealed federal limits on greenhouse gas emissions from fossil fuel power plants](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/14/nx-s1-5968785/epa-repeals-pollution-limits-on-power-plants), eliminating rules that had required power generators to cut carbon dioxide emissions in line with a decarbonisation trajectory. Separately, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/14/us-repeals-rules-limiting-greenhouse-gas-emissions-by-power-plants) that the repeal affects plants responsible for roughly a quarter of US electricity generation. The rules being struck were themselves replacements for earlier Obama-era regulations that the Supreme Court had previously curtailed in *West Virginia v. EPA*. The EPA, under the current administrator, has characterised the rules as examples of regulatory overreach that impose costs on the electricity grid without proportionate benefit to consumers — a framing that picks up the *West Virginia* majority's concern about agencies claiming major policy authority without explicit congressional authorisation.

## The received wisdom

The environmental and centre-left response is unambiguous: this is a catastrophic step backward, a gift to fossil fuel producers at the expense of the climate, public health, and the credibility of American commitments under the Paris Agreement framework. The economic case is presented as settled: renewables are already cheaper than fossil fuels at the margin, so preserving coal and gas generation is not even a good deal for consumers — it is a subsidy to incumbent polluters dressed up as regulatory relief. The rollback will have measurable consequences in air quality and in carbon emissions accounting, and it further undermines whatever residual trust non-American parties have in US climate commitments surviving changes in administration. For the climate movement, this is not merely bad policy; it is bad faith.

## A different read

The climate movement's frustration is legitimate, but the EPA rollback is more instructive than simply regrettable — and the right's defence of it reveals a set of genuine governance trade-offs that the mainstream commentary routinely elides.

Start with the constitutional question, because it matters. The *West Virginia v. EPA* decision was not a pro-fossil-fuel ruling in disguise; it was a ruling about administrative law and the separation of powers. The Supreme Court said, in effect, that the Environmental Protection Agency — an executive agency with no direct electoral accountability — cannot single-handedly rewrite the energy economy through regulatory interpretation. If Congress wants to mandate a specific decarbonisation trajectory for power plants, Congress should pass a law saying so. The fact that Congress has repeatedly failed to do that is not a reason for the EPA to do it anyway; it is a reason to fix Congress. That distinction matters enormously for how democratic accountability functions. When agencies make major policy choices that elected legislatures have refused to make, they are substituting administrative judgment for political will — and they are doing so in ways that are vulnerable to reversal by the next administration, as Monday's events illustrate perfectly.

This is the central structural problem with the strategy the Obama and Biden administrations pursued on climate: they chose executive action over durable legislation because durable legislation was politically hard, and executive action was immediately available. The result is a climate policy architecture built on sand — defensible in the courts only until it isn't, and permanently vulnerable to rollback. [The Atlantic's climate coverage](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/14/nx-s1-5968785/epa-repeals-pollution-limits-on-power-plants) has noted this dynamic for years without producing the obvious conclusion: the progressive approach to climate regulation has consistently prioritised short-term wins over durable institutional change, and Monday's repeal is the predictable result.

There is also a cost question that the climate community handles badly. Power plant emissions rules impose real costs on the electricity grid — particularly in regions where gas generation is the marginal source of power. Those costs are borne disproportionately by lower-income households, which spend a higher proportion of their income on energy. When the EPA issued these rules, it produced benefit-cost analyses that showed net positive outcomes — but those analyses used a social cost of carbon figure that embeds significant distributional and intergenerational assumptions that are contested. A conservative analysis that discounts future generations more heavily and weights present consumer energy costs more heavily will produce a very different number. Neither analysis is objectively correct; both reflect value judgments. The current administration has made its value judgments explicit; the previous one pretended it hadn't.

None of this means the rollback is good policy. The climate science is clear that unabated fossil fuel combustion at scale creates severe long-term risks, and the United States' failure to enact durable carbon pricing or a credible clean energy standard is a genuine governance failure. But the right way to fix that failure is through legislation that embeds the political trade-offs in statute — not through agency action that is both constitutionally dubious and politically fragile. Monday's repeal is the predictable end of a strategy that prioritised regulatory cleverness over political legitimacy.

## What to watch

- Whether a coalition of Democratic state attorneys general challenges the repeal in federal court, and which circuit they draw
- The response from US trading partners — particularly the EU, which has a carbon border adjustment mechanism that will now apply to US exports in covered sectors
- Whether any Republican senators in swing states signal concern about the rollback, which could create an opening for bipartisan clean energy legislation
- The electricity pricing effects over winter — if gas costs rise (as they have with Middle East tensions), the administration will face a domestic political test of its claim that deregulation benefits consumers
