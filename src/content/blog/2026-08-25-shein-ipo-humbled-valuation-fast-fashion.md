Shein, the fast-fashion giant that built an empire on astonishingly cheap garments sold directly from Chinese factories to global consumers, announced Monday that it would list on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange on 1 September at a valuation of approximately [$27 billion](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/24/fashion-giant-shein-27bn-valuation-hong-kong-market-debut) — a figure that would have seemed impossibly modest just four years ago, when the company commanded a $100 billion private market valuation. The offering will raise roughly £1.3 billion at the top of the price range. Shein swung to a net loss of $99 million in the first quarter of this year, compared with net income of $395 million in the same period last year, after the United States removed the import duty exemption on small packages that had been a structural subsidy for the de minimis business model the company built. Plans to list in New York were previously blocked by regulators over forced labour concerns; a proposed £50 billion London float was abandoned after similar questions about supply chain transparency.

## The received wisdom

The progressive critique of Shein is by now familiar and largely accurate on its face: the company grew by exploiting regulatory arbitrage (the de minimis threshold), offloading environmental costs onto the atmosphere and ultimately the public, and refusing — even in front of [British MPs](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/24/fashion-giant-shein-27bn-valuation-hong-kong-market-debut) — to confirm that its cotton was not produced with Uyghur forced labour in Xinjiang. The humbled IPO is, in this framing, a small and partial reckoning: the market is finally pricing in costs that were always real but were previously externalised onto workers, ecosystems and the rule of law.

There is not much to argue with here factually. The de minimis regime was a structural subsidy for direct-from-China shipping that domestic retailers could not access. The Xinjiang cotton question remains unanswered. The environmental footprint of producing thousands of new SKUs weekly, many of which end up in landfill after minimal use, is not a contested claim.

## A different read

What deserves complicating is the smugness with which some Western commentators have greeted Shein's difficulties — a smugness that contains its own set of concealed assumptions worth examining.

Shein grew to 156 million average monthly European users and became one of the continent's largest e-commerce platforms alongside Amazon because a very large number of people, many of them with limited incomes, found that it offered them access to fashion they could not otherwise afford. The moral case against cheap clothing is easiest to make from a position of financial comfort. The consumers who lined up for Shein's Paris opening and the protesters who gathered outside represent two real constituencies; the protesters are not more representative of ordinary European life than the shoppers.

The deeper question raised by the [IPO's collapse in valuation](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/24/fashion-giant-shein-27bn-valuation-hong-kong-market-debut) from $100 billion to $27 billion is what changed. Three things happened simultaneously: the US removed the de minimis exemption, meaning Shein's business model in its most important market became structurally more expensive; the Iran war disrupted delivery logistics in key markets and reduced consumer demand generally; and investors began demanding that the forced-labour supply chain questions actually be answered rather than deferred.

The first factor is the most important. The de minimis threshold — under which small packages valued below a set threshold entered the US duty-free — was a regulatory artifact that was never designed to subsidise mass commercial retail at scale. [The EU introduced a €3 customs charge on small parcels](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/24/fashion-giant-shein-27bn-valuation-hong-kong-market-debut) in a similar move to curb cheap Chinese imports. These regulatory corrections are legitimate — they simply restore the competitive conditions that were already supposed to exist.

This is the right-of-centre argument against Shein that gets less attention than it deserves: not that cheap fashion is morally wrong, but that Shein's model was built on regulatory subsidies that distorted competition and allowed it to avoid the supply chain compliance costs that European and American brands are legally required to meet. When the regulatory playing field is levelled — even partially — the "miracle" valuation disappears because it was partly a function of rule arbitrage.

There is also a Hong Kong angle worth noting. Shein chose Hong Kong for its listing after being blocked in New York and retreating from London. Hong Kong's stock exchange, battered since 2019 by political turbulence and the exodus of international institutional capital, is accepting a listing that Western exchanges declined — a reminder that the financial ecosystem that once made Hong Kong the premier gateway to Asian capital markets has fundamentally changed character.

For investors, the question now is whether $27 billion accurately prices a business whose structural advantages have been legislated away, or whether there is a real underlying company — 156 million European users, genuine logistics capability, strong brand recognition among younger consumers — that is worth owning at a lower multiple. The market will decide on 1 September.

## What to watch

- The actual share price on 1 September: if Shein prices at the bottom of its range or below, it will signal that institutional demand is weak even at the humbled valuation.
- Whether the Uyghur cotton question is formally answered in the IPO prospectus — the [regulatory filings](https://www1.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2026/0824/2026082400009.pdf) are public and the supply chain language will be scrutinised.
- EU enforcement of the small-parcel customs charge: partial implementation would restore competitive distortion; full enforcement would further compress Shein's European margins.
- Whether any major institutional investor takes a significant position — the absence of prominent names in the book would be its own signal about the quality of the asset.
