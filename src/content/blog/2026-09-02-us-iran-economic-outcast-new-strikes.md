The United States launched [new airstrikes on Iranian targets](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/01/us-iran-airstrikes-launch) on September 1st, dashing hopes that last weekend's exchange of fire might prove a contained episode rather than the opening of a wider renewal of hostilities. The strikes came days after Washington said it had observed Iran preparing to deploy fresh mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and follow an earlier American assault on Larak Island to which Iran [responded by targeting US bases in Jordan](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/aug/31/us-iran-israel-hormuz-oil-donald-trump-larak-kharg-island-uae-jordan-latest-news-updates). Simultaneously, the Trump administration has unveiled what it calls [Operation Economic Outcast](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/31/iran-economy-crisis-usa-launches-sanctions-inflation-trump), a sweeping new sanctions campaign designed to push Iran's already fragile economy further into crisis. Food inflation in Iran has soared, and analysts warn the operation is expected to push many Iranians into deeper poverty. The dual-track approach — military pressure plus economic strangulation — is now Washington's declared strategy.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream case for the administration's approach is not without merit. Iran has spent years enriching uranium beyond any plausible civilian justification, [funding proxy militias across the region](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/1/us-launches-new-airstrikes-on-iranian-targets-as-hostilities-flare), and — most provocatively — threatening to mine the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil supply transits. The argument that Tehran only responds to coercion has a long history, and the Obama-era nuclear deal, which relaxed sanctions in exchange for uranium limits, was arguably undone not by American hawkishness but by Iran's continued regional adventurism. Trump's hawks would say that the current pressure campaign is simply applying a lesson the Obama and Biden administrations learned too slowly: that Iran's regime treats diplomatic goodwill as a resource to be exploited rather than a foundation to build on. From that vantage point, the combination of military strikes and economic sanctions is the only language the Islamic Republic reliably understands.

## A different read

There is something strategically incoherent about the dual approach that deserves more scrutiny than it is currently receiving. Military coercion and maximum economic pressure have now been running simultaneously for the better part of two years, and the Islamic Republic is still standing. The question that Washington's hawks consistently fail to answer is: what comes after? Sanctions that push ordinary Iranians into poverty have a poor record of toppling authoritarian regimes; if anything, economic immiseration tends to consolidate the security state's grip while eliminating the middle-class constituencies most likely to push for liberalisation. Iraq in the 1990s is the cautionary tale — a decade of sanctions impoverished the population while Saddam Hussein remained in power.

[Operation Economic Outcast](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/31/iran-economy-crisis-usa-launches-sanctions-inflation-trump) is a vivid name, but naming a sanctions regime after social exclusion signals something revealing about the administration's thinking: it is designed for domestic political consumption as much as strategic effect. The Trump administration needs Iran to remain a visible enemy. An Iran that caves diplomatically or collapses economically enough to negotiate would, paradoxically, be a political problem — it would require the administration to accept a deal that critics on the right would inevitably label insufficient.

The military dimension is equally fraught. The initial American strike on Larak Island, justified by Iran's alleged minelaying preparations, crossed a threshold that the two countries had carefully avoided for decades: direct strikes on Iranian territory rather than proxies. Iran's retaliatory strikes on US bases in Jordan moved the conflict further along the escalatory ladder. [Each exchange of fire](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/31/trump-threatens-further-action-us-iran-exchange-fire) now creates facts that constrain future American and Iranian decision-makers. Tehran cannot afford to appear domestically as though it absorbed American strikes without response; Washington cannot appear to have fired shots without consequence. These are exactly the dynamics that produce wars neither side fully intended.

Historical precedent offers a sobering guide. The 1980–88 Iran-Iraq War — which killed an estimated half a million people and resolved nothing — began as a series of escalatory miscalculations, each side reading the other's restraint as weakness and each side's shows of force as opportunities for the opponent to claim victimhood. The current sequence shares uncomfortable structural similarities. American planners presumably believe they can calibrate the level of strikes precisely enough to deter without triggering full-scale war. That confidence in calibration has historically been misplaced: the fog of war, domestic political pressures on both sides, and the involvement of proxies operating with partial autonomy all introduce variables that render "limited" military campaigns far less controllable than the slide decks in the Situation Room suggest.

There is a harder right-of-centre critique here too. Consistent with a genuine national-interest framework, one would ask: what is the concrete American objective? Regime change is not a declared goal and likely not achievable by these means. Preventing Iranian nuclear weapons is a goal, but the current military-economic pressure may actually accelerate Tehran's nuclear calculus rather than deter it — a regime under existential economic pressure has more incentive, not less, to acquire a deterrent that makes invasion prohibitively costly.

## What to watch

Watch whether China and Russia move to provide Iran with economic lifelines that blunt the sanctions regime — [both Beijing and Moscow have strategic incentives](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3v4pgk7gweo) to ensure that the American coercive playbook fails. Watch for any sign of internal Iranian fracturing — if the economic pressure produces elite defections or significant unrest, the strategic calculus changes. Watch the Strait of Hormuz: any actual mining would trigger an immediate oil price spike and a near-automatic American military response, making it the single most dangerous flashpoint. And watch what, if anything, back-channel diplomacy is doing that the public posturing is designed to obscure — because the only exit from this spiral that does not involve catastrophe is a negotiated one.
