A [BBC World Service investigation published this week](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c54g1kxw4nvvo) has found that Sudan's Rapid Support Forces have been deliberately targeting hospitals across the country's civil war zones. The report draws on satellite imagery, survivor testimony, and medical documentation to argue that attacks on healthcare infrastructure are not collateral but systematic. Sudan's civil war, now in its fourth year, has displaced over 11 million people — the largest displacement crisis on earth — and killed an estimated 150,000. The RSF, a paramilitary force that emerged from the Janjaweed militias responsible for the Darfur genocide, is fighting the Sudanese Armed Forces for control of the state. Hospitals in Khartoum, El-Fasher, and Wad Madani have all reportedly been hit. International humanitarian organisations have largely been expelled or have self-evacuated.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing is that Sudan represents a catastrophic failure of global attention — a war that is "too complicated" and too far from strategic Western interests to command sustained coverage or diplomatic pressure. The progressive humanitarian consensus holds that wealthy nations, particularly the United States, the United Kingdom, and Gulf states, bear moral responsibility for the RSF's empowerment: the UAE in particular is accused of [funnelling weapons to the RSF](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/22/hundreds-of-ethiopians-cross-into-kenya-to-flee-regional-fighting), while Western governments are said to have prioritised counterterrorism partnerships over human rights conditionality. In this reading, the solution involves greater multilateral action — more UN Security Council pressure, stronger ICC referrals, and emergency humanitarian corridors. The war is framed as a consequence of post-colonial neglect compounded by cynical great-power competition.

## A different read

The humanitarian framing is not wrong — it is simply incomplete, and its proposed remedies have a dismal track record. Let us be precise about what the BBC investigation actually documents: not random brutality but a deliberate military doctrine of destroying healthcare capacity to accelerate civilian submission. This is not the fog of war. It is a recognisable strategy, one [employed by Assad in Syria](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c54g1kxw4nvvo), by Russian forces in Ukraine, and by the Islamic State in Iraq — and it tends to emerge where perpetrators calculate, accurately, that the international community will not pay a serious price for it.

The International Criminal Court has been sanctioned by the United States under the current administration and is structurally incapable of enforcing its own warrants in active conflict zones. The UN Security Council is paralysed by Russian and Chinese vetoes on any measure with enforcement teeth. And the UAE, Sudan's most consequential external actor, is simultaneously a strategic Western partner in Gulf security, a major investor across sub-Saharan Africa, and a key node in global energy markets. This is not an excuse. It is a diagnosis.

Here is where the right-of-centre analysis diverges from the progressive one: the answer to this paralysis is not more multilateral institutions or expanded international mandates. It is the reassertion of targeted bilateral leverage, the kind that requires naming states — specifically, Abu Dhabi — and attaching costs to their conduct. The UK, which has substantial financial and diplomatic ties to the UAE, could do this. So far it has not. Instead, successive British governments have issued anodyne statements about "deep concern" while the arms and money flow.

History offers instructive parallels. The Rwandan genocide in 1994 was preceded by similar warnings from the UN's own force commander, Roméo Dallaire, which were [systematically ignored](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c54g1kxw4nvvo) by the Security Council. The lesson drawn from Rwanda was, ostensibly, the "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine — yet R2P has been invoked selectively and has never generated the automatic response its architects promised. Sudan is the latest proof that international norms without enforcement mechanisms are aspirations dressed up as law.

There is also a more uncomfortable domestic dimension. Britain has, over the past decade, dramatically reduced its international development budget — from 0.7% of gross national income to 0.5%, with further cuts floated. The foreign aid cuts were sold politically as a corrective to waste and dependency, and there is genuine merit in that critique. But the Sudan crisis illustrates the second-order cost: when development spending collapses, so does the network of civil society organisations, local health systems, and early-warning infrastructure that might otherwise slow a conflict's humanitarian spiral. Fiscal prudence is a virtue; false economy is not. The cost of not acting in Sudan will eventually be paid in refugee flows, regional destabilisation, and the further erosion of the international order Western nations claim to be defending.

The RSF's hospital campaign is not anomalous. It is a rational, if monstrous, application of lessons learned from other impunity-permitting conflicts. Until the calculus changes — until perpetrators and their sponsors face something harder than a UN statement — the lesson will keep being learned.

## What to watch

- Whether the BBC investigation prompts any formal UK government response, particularly regarding UAE arms flows, before the next parliamentary recess
- The trajectory of the siege of El-Fasher in North Darfur, which humanitarian agencies have described as the last major civilian refuge in the region
- Any ICC action following the investigation, and whether the Trump administration's sanctions on the court complicate European members' ability to respond
- Sudan's absence from the G20 agenda in November — or any signal that host nation South Africa will raise it formally
