China's monthly car exports [topped one million units for the first time in June 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/china-car-exports-record-one-million-monthly), while overall exports [rose 27% year-on-year](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/china-exports-record-trade-surplus-2026), putting Beijing on track to [match or exceed last year's record $1 trillion annual trade surplus](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/china-trade-surplus-trillion-dollar-record). These numbers land at a moment of maximum political sensitivity: the US has already imposed sweeping tariffs on Chinese goods, the EU has added levies specifically on electric vehicles, and the broader G7 debate about "de-risking" from Chinese supply chains has been running for three years without producing a convincing alternative. The numbers also arrive against the backdrop of active US-Iran conflict that is driving up energy prices globally — a dynamic that, ironically, benefits Chinese industry, which imports the bulk of its oil at discounted rates from sanctioned producers while its Western competitors absorb the Hormuz premium. China is not the story of the week in most Western newsrooms, but it may be the story of the decade.

## The received wisdom

The dominant Western reading of China's trade data alternates between alarm and fatalism. The alarm camp — represented by trade hawks in Washington, Brussels, and London — argues that China's export surge is the product of deliberate state subsidy, dumping, and market-access asymmetry: Chinese firms sell freely into Western markets while Western firms face regulatory barriers, forced technology transfer, and opaque procurement rules in China. The fatalist camp — more common among economists and in boardrooms with China exposure — argues that trying to counter Chinese manufacturing competitiveness through tariffs is like trying to hold back a tide with a strainer: Chinese productivity gains and scale advantages are now structural, not merely subsidised, and the pain of decoupling falls disproportionately on Western consumers and downstream manufacturers. The received wisdom, in both variants, tends toward a resigned acknowledgement that the West needs to "do something" about China's industrial rise without a clear consensus on what that something actually is.

## A different read

Both camps are identifying real phenomena while missing the deeper structural problem: the West did not lose the manufacturing competition in the last five years; it conceded it over the previous thirty, and the bill is now coming due.

The car export figure is the most legible symbol of this. A decade ago, Chinese electric vehicles were a punchline — heavily subsidised, technically mediocre, dismissed by Western automakers who were themselves slow-rolling EV investment to protect internal combustion margins. BYD, CATL, and their peers used that window to achieve genuine cost and technical leadership in battery chemistry and software integration. [Volkswagen's announcement of 100,000 global job cuts](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/business/2026/07/13/volkswagen-jobs-cuts-europe-industry) — reported just days before China's export milestone — is the most visible symptom of what happens when a legacy industry optimises for yesterday's product while an adversary optimises for tomorrow's. The causal chain from Chinese EV competitiveness to German industrial contraction is not speculative; it is playing out in real time.

The tariff response, on both sides of the Atlantic, is best understood as a political analgesic rather than a structural cure. US tariffs on Chinese goods have not rebuilt American manufacturing capacity in the affected categories; they have largely shifted production to Vietnam, Mexico, and other third parties who often use Chinese inputs anyway, raised prices for American consumers, and generated a paper "decoupling" that conceals continued deep interdependence. The EU's EV-specific levies — [challenged by China at the WTO](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/china-eu-ev-tariffs-wto-challenge) and fiercely resisted by European automakers who fear Chinese retaliation against their profitable China operations — are a more targeted instrument but face the same fundamental limitation: they protect existing players without creating conditions for new competitive entrants.

The genuinely hard question — which neither free-traders nor protectionists in the West have answered convincingly — is what a legitimate, WTO-compatible industrial policy actually looks like when your adversary is operating a state-capitalist model that plays by different rules. The US CHIPS Act and the EU's strategic autonomy agenda represent serious attempts to answer this question in semiconductors; they have had partial success in accelerating domestic fabrication investment. But the lessons learned in chips have not been applied systematically to batteries, solar panels, shipbuilding, or the dozens of other sectors where Chinese state-directed investment has achieved market dominance.

[China's own risks are real but underweighted in most Western analysis](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/07/15/china-trade-surplus-trillion-dollar-record): a property sector still working through the consequences of Evergrande-era excess, youth unemployment that official figures systematically understate, and a demographic structure that will produce labour force contraction through the 2030s. An export-led growth model is also inherently vulnerable to the kind of demand compression that Western tariffs and slowdowns impose. The trillion-dollar surplus is not a sign of invincibility; it is partly a sign of domestic consumption weakness — money that Chinese households and firms are not spending at home is being parked in export earnings instead. That is a structural problem, not a strategic triumph.

The irony of the current moment is that the US-Iran conflict — by driving up energy costs — is simultaneously inflating the price of the green transition that was supposed to reduce Western dependence on fossil fuels, while benefiting the Chinese firms positioned to supply the solar panels, batteries, and EVs that the transition requires. The Hormuz premium is, in effect, a subsidy to Chinese clean energy exports.

## What to watch

Watch **whether the EU's automotive tariff regime survives its first serious political test**: German carmakers facing factory closures will lobby hard for relief, and the Franco-German alignment that has historically driven EU trade policy is under strain. Watch **China's WTO challenge trajectory**: if the panel finds against EU EV tariffs, it forces a choice between WTO compliance and industrial protection that Brussels has so far avoided. Watch **the Hormuz conflict's duration**: every week of elevated oil prices compounds the Chinese competitive advantage in electrification. And watch **whether any G7 country produces a credible sectoral industrial strategy** that goes beyond tariffs to actual competitive capacity building — the rare event that would represent a genuine strategic shift.
