Student protesters [attacked government buildings in Tirana](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq80zk5k2ep4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) this week in demonstrations that began as a reaction to campus conditions and escalated into broader anti-government unrest. The Guardian reports that [protests turned violent as demonstrators clashed with police](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/14/student-protests-tirana-albania-police-clashes) in scenes that recall earlier waves of post-communist protest across the Balkans. Albania is an EU candidate country — it has been in accession negotiations since 2014 — governed by Prime Minister Edi Rama's Socialist Party, which has held power since 2013. The protests follow years of accumulating grievances: corruption allegations against the government, a justice system that Albanians regard with deep scepticism despite internationally supervised reforms, youth unemployment that drives emigration at rates that have significantly depopulated the country, and a university system whose conditions have for years lagged European standards. What began as a student movement has drawn in broader opposition groups, raising questions about the stability of a government that has dominated Albanian politics for over a decade.

## The received wisdom

The European mainstream reading of Balkan instability tends to default to a single explanatory frame: these countries need more Europe. More accession pressure, more conditionality, more alignment with EU norms, more Brussels-supervised institution-building. Corruption is a problem of insufficient European integration; violence is a symptom of the democratic immaturity that membership will eventually cure. This is not an entirely cynical position — the EU's transformative power in Central and Eastern Europe after 1989 was real and significant, and the prospect of accession has repeatedly served as a forcing function for judicial reform in aspirant states. The problem is that Albania has been hearing this diagnosis for twenty years. It has implemented EU-mandated vetting mechanisms for judges and prosecutors. It has signed cooperation agreements, adopted acquis legislation, and submitted to peer reviews. The corruption has persisted; the emigration has continued; the streets are now burning.

## A different read

The Albanian protests invite a question that the Brussels consensus is poorly equipped to answer: what happens when institutional reform proceeds on paper but fails in practice, and the population — particularly its educated young — runs out of patience waiting for convergence to arrive?

Albania's emigration crisis is the canary in the coal mine. The country has lost, by various estimates, somewhere between a quarter and a third of its population since 1990 — one of the highest emigration rates in the world relative to population size. This is not merely a development problem; it is a democratic problem. The people most likely to organise, to vote, to staff civil society organisations, to sustain the kind of patient civic engagement that democratic consolidation requires — are the people most likely to leave. What remains is an electorate that is older, more rural, more economically dependent on state employment, and thus more susceptible to clientelistic politics. Rama's government has been accused of precisely this kind of clientelism: using state contracts, public employment, and permit systems to maintain a coalition of beneficiaries who vote predictably.

The EU's accession framework was designed in the 1990s for a specific moment: the transition of post-communist states with functioning administrative traditions, educated populations, and neighbour countries already inside the club. Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic had these advantages. The Western Balkans — fragmented by war, hollowed by emigration, corrupted by post-conflict political economies — present a structurally different challenge, and applying the same framework to a different problem is producing different results.

There is also something to be said about the limits of technocratic patience as a substitute for political representation. The vetting mechanism that Albania adopted — internationally supervised review of judges and prosecutors — was celebrated in Brussels as a model of externally anchored reform. It has been running for nearly a decade. Public trust in the judiciary, by opinion surveys, has barely moved. The mechanism may have removed some corrupt officials; it has not rebuilt the *legitimacy* of legal institutions in the eyes of citizens who have watched justice be routinely denied. Legitimacy cannot be imported from Brussels; it has to be earned domestically, through visible accountability and consistent rule of law. When that fails, students fill the streets.

The right-of-center concern here is not that reform is wrong but that the managerial confidence of external reformers — their faith that if you get the institutions formally right, political reality will follow — is repeatedly confounded by the stubbornness of culture, history, and economic incentive. Sustainable democratic order is, in Burkean terms, a slow inheritance from the past, not a product that can be assembled from OECD blueprints.

## What to watch

- Whether Rama's government makes meaningful concessions to student demands, or whether the crackdown escalates into a cycle of repression and broadened protest.
- EU reaction: whether Brussels uses the protests as leverage to sharpen accession conditionality, or treats them primarily as a stability problem to be managed.
- The Albanian emigration pipeline: whether violence accelerates the departure of the educated young who might otherwise have remained to build civic capacity.
- Comparison with Georgia's recent democratic backsliding: whether Western Balkan candidate states are beginning to see EU conditionality as a burden rather than a benefit.
