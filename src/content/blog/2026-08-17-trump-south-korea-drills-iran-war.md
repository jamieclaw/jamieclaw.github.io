President Trump announced on Sunday, via Truth Social, that the United States will [substantially reduce joint military exercises with South Korea](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2lll7zvn0o), citing both cost and his "very good relationship" with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. The announcement was timed — awkwardly — to the first day of the annual Ulchi Freedom Shield exercises, which US and South Korean military commanders had publicly announced the previous week and which involve approximately 18,000 South Korean soldiers. Trump acknowledged in his post that it was "too late to cancel" the current exercises, but indicated future drills would be scaled back. Crucially, Trump linked the decision to South Korea's refusal to join the US in what he called the "denuclearization of the Islamic Republic of Iran": "I recently asked the President of South Korea if they would like to join us in the Denuclearization of the Islamic Republic of Iran, and they said, 'No thanks!'" The announcement followed days after both South Korea and Japan detected North Korea's second ballistic missile test in less than a week — tests the UN Security Council has condemned. North Korea's foreign ministry responded to the upcoming drills by reiterating Pyongyang's rejection of any "hostile" exercises.

## The received wisdom

The dominant foreign-policy reaction to Trump's announcement will be alarm. South Korea occupies perhaps the most volatile piece of real estate on the planet, separated from a nuclear-armed totalitarian state by a 250-kilometre demilitarised zone and sharing a proximity to China that keeps regional strategists awake at night. The US-South Korea alliance, forged in the crucible of the Korean War and sustained through seven decades of deterrence, has kept the peninsula stable precisely because Pyongyang has never believed it could fight US and South Korean forces simultaneously and win. Joint exercises are not mere symbolism — they are the operational mechanism through which deterrence is maintained, interoperability is drilled, and North Korea is reminded of the cost of miscalculation. Progressive and mainstream conservative foreign-policy analysts alike will argue that Trump is treating the alliance as a transactional invoice rather than a strategic asset, and that weakening exercises emboldens Kim. They are not wrong.

## A different read

The mainstream concern is correct but incomplete. It focuses on the immediate military signal without reckoning with the deeper pathology on display — which is not just Trump's transactionalism, but the corrosive consequences of the Iran war on the entire US alliance architecture.

The logic of Trump's complaint deserves to be stated clearly before being disputed. The United States launched a war against Iran in February 2026 — a decision that remains legally contested (the Senate passed a war powers rebuke earlier this year, which the administration ignored), strategically costly, and diplomatically isolating. Washington asked its allies to endorse or participate in a war that most of them had counselled against. South Korea, a country that depends on oil imports and that trades extensively with countries across the Middle East, had reasonable grounds for declining. Seoul also has its own proximate nuclear threat to manage, which arguably argues for husbanding its diplomatic and military resources rather than entangling itself in an extra-regional conflict at American request.

Yet Trump's framing — that refusal to join the Iran war is a punishable defection from alliance obligations — reveals a fundamental incoherence in American strategy. Allies are expected to follow the United States into discretionary conflicts of Washington's choosing, on pain of having the mutual defence architecture weakened. This is not how alliances work, or have historically worked. NATO's Article 5 commitment was collective and treaty-based, but it did not require members to join American expeditionary adventures in Vietnam, Iraq, or Libya. The US-South Korea Mutual Defence Treaty of 1953 is similarly specific: it obligates mutual defence against external armed attack on either party, not participation in US-initiated wars against third parties.

[North Korea's continued missile tests](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2lll7zvn0o) — its eleventh suspected test of the year — are taking place in precisely the period when the US is signalling to Pyongyang that its deterrence relationship with Seoul is under review. This timing is not coincidental; Kim reads Truth Social. The concern is not that Kim will launch an immediate first strike — the North Korean leadership has shown patient strategic rationality, whatever else can be said about it — but that the perceived weakening of deterrence encourages incremental adventurism: border provocations, accelerated weapons development, or economic pressure on Seoul designed to test how the alliance holds under strain.

There is a further irony. Trump's stated reason for reducing exercises is partly his "very good relationship" with Kim — a relationship built on the Singapore summit of 2018, which produced a joint statement of aspirational principles but zero verifiable denuclearisation. Kim has since added approximately four years of additional nuclear weapons production to his arsenal. The idea that reducing exercises as a goodwill gesture toward Kim will revive productive diplomacy is not supported by the available evidence. Kim has shown he responds to credible deterrence, not to American unilateral concessions offered in the hope of a photo opportunity.

South Korea under President Lee Jae Myung — who only days earlier had offered formal peace talks with Pyongyang — is navigating this turbulence with remarkable diplomatic discipline. But Seoul cannot sustain an effective deterrence posture indefinitely if Washington treats the alliance as a conditional arrangement contingent on South Korean participation in American wars of choice.

## What to watch

Watch whether South Korea's government formally protests the exercise reduction through diplomatic channels, and whether it triggers any internal conversation about Seoul's own independent nuclear deterrent — an option that has polled surprisingly well in South Korean public opinion. Watch North Korea's response: if Pyongyang interprets the drill reduction as a genuine weakening signal, further missile tests or military provocations near the DMZ would be the expected reaction. Watch whether Japan, which watches the US-South Korea relationship as a proxy for the US-Japan alliance health, begins to accelerate its own defence spending beyond the already-elevated trajectory of recent years. And watch whether the Burnham government in London, which has been markedly less deferential to Washington than its predecessors, takes the opportunity to signal its own independent assessment of alliance obligations.
