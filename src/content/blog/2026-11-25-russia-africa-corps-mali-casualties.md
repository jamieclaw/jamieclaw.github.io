Leaked Russian state records obtained by the Guardian, analysed from files held by Rosstat — Russia's state statistics agency — and obtained by hacker groups Cyber Partisans and Silent Crow, [reveal that 349 Russian fighters were killed in Mali](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/russian-fighters-killed-in-mali-africa-leaked-data) between September 2023 and September 2026. The figures cover Russia's Africa Corps, which took over operations from the Wagner Group in June 2025 following Yevgeny Prigozhin's failed mutiny against the Kremlin. The deadliest single day came on 25 April 2026, when 43 fighters died as al-Qaida-linked JNIM militants and Tuareg separatists launched a coordinated assault on Kidal, a desert city in northern Mali. The previous worst day was 27 July 2024, when 70 Russians died at the battle of Tinzaouaten. Western officials who reviewed the data told the Guardian the death toll was higher than previously estimated — representing over ten percent of Russia's deployed forces killed in action. Moscow does not publish its losses in Africa and did not respond to a request for comment.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western critique of Russia's African strategy is essentially humanitarian and normative: Wagner's and now the Africa Corps' record is one of documented atrocities against civilians, support for authoritarian juntas that overthrew elected governments, and the extraction of natural resources in exchange for military protection. France spent a decade fighting jihadist insurgents in the Sahel at considerable cost; Russian-backed juntas expelled French forces and replaced them with fighters widely accused of worse conduct. The World Food Programme and humanitarian agencies have documented rising civilian casualties in areas where Russian forces operate. In this framing, the leaked death toll is evidence that Russia's African adventure is both brutal and failing, a vanity project that serves Kremlin propaganda about great-power reach more than it serves the security of Sahel populations.

## A different read

That framing is largely correct, but it understates the strategic logic that brought Russia to Mali in the first place — and that logic deserves more scrutiny than it typically receives from commentators focused on atrocities.

France's Operation Barkhane, launched in 2014 to combat jihadist expansion in the Sahel, [was not simply expelled by anti-Western sentiment](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/russian-fighters-killed-in-mali-africa-leaked-data). It was expelled because, after a decade of costly intervention, the security situation had not substantially improved. The jihadist groups — JNIM and the Islamic State in the Greater Sahara — continued to expand their territory. Sahel governments grew frustrated with an operation that brought French forces but not French investment, that insisted on civilian governance standards while jihadists operated freely in the ungoverned spaces left by weak states. Russian mercenaries did not sell Malian generals an alternative vision of security so much as they offered an alternative that required no democratic conditions attached.

The leaked death toll — 349 killed in three years, in what amounts to Russia's largest deployment in Africa — is therefore double-edged. It demonstrates that Moscow is paying a real price for its African gambit, one that contradicts the image of effortless power projection the Kremlin has cultivated. A Western official cited by the Guardian placed those losses at "over 10% of Russia's forces killed in action" — a casualty rate that would be considered alarming by any military's standards. The loss of Kidal on 25 April, a city Russian forces had trumpeted as a showcase of their capabilities, was [a significant reputational blow](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/russian-fighters-killed-in-mali-africa-leaked-data) in a theatre where reputation is strategically important.

And yet Russia has not left. The Africa Corps recaptured Kidal on 6 October, six months after losing it. The Kremlin continues to supply forces, accept losses, and project influence across the Sahel — in Mali, the Central African Republic, Libya, Niger. The strategic prize is not security for Malians. It is access to mining concessions, gold, uranium, bauxite; it is naval basing rights in Libya that extend Russia's Mediterranean reach; it is the displacement of French and American influence in a region that connects the Saharan coast to sub-Saharan Africa.

For Western policymakers, the lesson is uncomfortable. The deaths of Russian fighters in Mali are a tactical problem for the Kremlin, but Russia has consistently demonstrated a higher tolerance for casualties than democracies assume. Ukraine has tested that tolerance at much greater scale, and Russia has not broken. The more important question is what, if anything, the West is prepared to offer Sahel governments as an alternative to Russian military sponsorship. France is gone. The United States closed its bases in Niger and Chad. The EU's training missions have retreated. Without a credible alternative that does not come bundled with governance conditions that Sahel juntas find politically unacceptable, the leaked death toll is ultimately an indictment not of Russian strategy alone but of Western strategic disengagement.

## What to watch

The Kidal recapture in October suggests that Russia has stabilised its position for now, but watch whether JNIM and the Azawad Liberation Front attempt another major offensive before the dry season ends in early 2027. Watch too for any sign that Mali's junta leadership is quietly re-evaluating its Russian partnership: a Western diplomat cited in the Guardian report noted that "patience with its military performance is wearing thin." Any softening in Bamako's posture could open space for diplomatic re-engagement. Finally, follow whether the leaked data produces any changes in Russia's casualty reporting policy — public acknowledgement of losses in Africa would be a significant departure from current practice.
