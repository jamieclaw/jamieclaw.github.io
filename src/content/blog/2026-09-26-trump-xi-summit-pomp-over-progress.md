The state dinner is over, the pandas are in their enclosures, and the joint readout is carefully bland. Xi Jinping's visit to Washington produced what [BBC World describes](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr93e4x7kdjjo) as a warm but ultimately thin outcome: Trump and Xi "got Trump's red carpet welcome — but not everything he wanted." [NPR's analysis](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/25/nx-s1-5981265/takeaways-trump-xi-state-visit-pageantry-pandas) frames the summit as "pageantry and pandas," while [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/25/donald-trump-xi-jinping-china-president-us-visit-red) concludes flatly that "pomp over progress in Xi's US summit with Trump is a win for China." Trade tariffs remain largely in place. Taiwan was not resolved. Fentanyl precursor flows were acknowledged but not arrested. Technology export controls were discussed but not modified. Two leaders who had spent months signalling the importance of this meeting left Washington with atmospherics and photo opportunities rather than frameworks.

## The received wisdom

The optimistic case for summitry without deliverables is that the relationship itself is the deliverable. After years of near-zero top-level contact, restoring a direct channel between the two leaders prevents miscalculation — particularly important given the proximity of US and Chinese naval assets in the South China Sea and the risk of accidental escalation over Taiwan. On this reading, the absence of a specific agreement on semiconductors or tariffs is not a failure but a sign of appropriate caution: both sides chose not to lock in positions that domestic politics would subsequently force them to repudiate. Trade negotiations are multi-year undertakings. Military-to-military communication lines, restored, matter more than any specific communiqué. The Guardian notes that Xi laid out "terms to avoid US-China military conflict" — and even that level of strategic communication has value.

## A different read

The history of summit diplomacy is littered with warm meetings that papered over structural tensions until they could no longer be papered over. Nixon and Brezhnev held four summits between 1972 and 1974; détente collapsed within a decade. The logic of summit pageantry is seductive precisely because it flatters both leaders' self-images as men who can move history — which is exactly why it tends to substitute for the harder, slower, less photogenic work of negotiating binding frameworks.

The specific problem with this summit is that the gap between ceremony and substance is not evenly distributed. [BBC Business reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cxq63dqp93n1o) that the two leaders exchanged "warm words at state dinner but little progress on key issues," which is a polite way of saying that Xi got the legitimising optics of a White House welcome while conceding very little of the structural substance Washington wanted. China's leadership can point domestically to the red carpet, the 21-gun salute, and the bilateral photo opportunities as evidence that the United States treats Beijing as a peer power — strengthening Xi's domestic position — while the strategic competition continues unchanged in semiconductors, military posture, and trade.

Trump's instinct toward personal diplomacy — the transactional belief that two strong men in a room can settle what bureaucracies cannot — has produced this outcome before. The Kim Jong-un summits of 2018-2019 generated extraordinary global attention and zero verifiable denuclearisation. The difference with China is that the stakes are incomparably higher and the structural competition is baked into the economic and military architecture of both countries in ways that no personal rapport can dissolve.

There is also something revealing in the specific things that were not discussed — or discussed without resolution. Taiwan remains the central strategic tripwire. Fentanyl precursor export controls from Chinese manufacturers remain inadequate. The technology decoupling that Washington has been pursuing through semiconductor export controls and the Entity List continues to generate Chinese counter-pressure. These are not peripheral issues; they are the heart of the strategic competition. The fact that a state visit with full ceremony addressed none of them suggests that both sides came to perform, not to negotiate.

The Guardian's framing — "pomp over progress is a win for China" — may slightly overstate it, but the underlying point holds. China's longer-term strategic approach does not require bilateral breakthroughs; it requires preventing decisive American action while Chinese technological and military capabilities continue to develop. A summit that consumes diplomatic bandwidth without producing constraints on Chinese behaviour is, in that narrow sense, a favourable outcome for Beijing.

## What to watch

- Whether any specific working-group mechanisms were established, and whether they convene within 90 days — the typical test of whether a summit produced real follow-through.
- Congressional reaction: if Republican hawks or Democratic China hawks treat the summit as cover for policy drift, expect legislation on Taiwan or semiconductors to accelerate rather than slow.
- Chinese state media framing over the next week: how Beijing characterises the outcome for a domestic audience will signal what it thinks it achieved.
- The next flashpoint — most likely a Taiwan Strait transit by a US Navy vessel — and whether the newly restored communication channels actually function under pressure.
