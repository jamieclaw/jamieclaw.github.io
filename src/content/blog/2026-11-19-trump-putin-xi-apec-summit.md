On the margins of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, [Donald Trump shook hands with both Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck9d75x5wwwwo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) on Wednesday — the first time the three leaders have stood together since Trump's return to the White House. A bilateral Trump–Putin session is confirmed for Thursday. Russian state media captured [Putin's nuclear command briefcase](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/11/18/putins-nuclear-briefcase-makes-surprise-appearance-at-china-summit?traffic_source=rss) being carried openly into the venue, an image choreographed, as these things always are, for maximum psychological effect. Xi, as host in a city chosen to showcase China's technological rise, watched proceedings with the quiet satisfaction of a man who has arranged the furniture to his own advantage. The diplomatic triangle that many strategists long argued was structurally impossible — a Washington-Moscow rapprochement brokered on Chinese soil — is, improbably, taking shape.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western liberal narrative runs something like this: Trump, intellectually susceptible to strongmen and allergic to the multilateral architecture his predecessors painstakingly built, is legitimising authoritarian revanchism. By meeting Putin without demanding a credible ceasefire framework first, he hands the Kremlin a propaganda victory and signals to Kyiv that American staying power is finite. Meeting Xi in Shenzhen — not in a neutral venue, not on Washington's terms — compounds the symbolism. The Atlantic alliance, already rattled by years of American ambivalence, watches anxiously as its patron cosy up to the two powers most committed to its erosion. For this school of thought, the photograph of three leaders shaking hands is the postcard from the funeral of the post-1945 liberal order.

There is something to this. Any serious analyst should concede that summitry without preconditions creates perverse incentives, and that Putin has historically treated American eagerness to negotiate as confirmation that pressure is working.

## A different read

And yet the received wisdom deserves a harder examination, because it encodes several assumptions that do not survive contact with the historical record.

The first is that isolation is a coherent strategy for managing great-power rivalry. The reflex to shun adversaries has a poor track record. Richard Nixon's opening to China in 1972 was condemned at the time as moral capitulation; it proved to be one of the most consequential acts of strategic triangulation in modern history, driving a wedge between Beijing and Moscow that defined Cold War geometry for two decades. The [Shenzhen summit](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/17/hi-tech-shenzhen-chinamaxxing-xi-apec-summit) is, structurally, the inverse problem: how do you prevent China and Russia from fusing into a consolidated Eurasian bloc? The answer is probably not to refuse to attend.

The second assumption is that the liberal order was functioning adequately before Trump arrived to disrupt it. It was not. Ukraine had been fighting a grinding, attritional war for over four years at enormous human cost with no plausible termination strategy on offer from either NATO or the EU. The [underground tunnel warfare in the Kharkiv region](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c32l507ev4v4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) reported this week — Ukrainian forces fighting room-to-room inside Soviet-era infrastructure to stay out of drone range — illustrates what "managed deterrence without endgame" looks like on the ground. If Trump can extract a durable ceasefire, the procedural offensiveness of shaking Putin's hand fades into irrelevance against the humanitarian arithmetic.

The third assumption is that Xi is a passive beneficiary of this meeting rather than its principal architect. That framing is almost certainly wrong. Xi chose Shenzhen deliberately — a city that functions as a living argument for Chinese-model techno-authoritarianism — and [signalled a "Chinamaxxing" message](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/17/hi-tech-shenzhen-chinamaxxing-xi-apec-summit) to the world. Having both Trump and Putin attend on his turf is a form of soft-power theatre that Beijing has been building toward for years. The question Western analysts should be asking is not whether Trump should have attended, but whether he — or anyone in his administration — has the strategic patience to use this forum to separate Russian and Chinese interests rather than allow Xi to weld them tighter. Historical precedent is not encouraging: Nixon succeeded because Kissinger spent months laying groundwork; there is scant evidence of equivalent preparation here.

The nuclear briefcase, meanwhile, deserves its own paragraph. Putin's decision to have it visibly displayed is not an accident. It is a reminder that Russia's leverage in any negotiation is ultimately coercive — and that any agreement will be only as durable as the credible threat structure underpinning it. This is precisely why the procedural details of a potential Ukraine settlement matter so much: security guarantees without enforcement mechanisms are theatre. The Obama administration offered Ukraine an association agreement without Article 5 coverage in 2013. We know how that ended.

The right-of-centre case for engaging with this summit is therefore conditional, not enthusiastic. Diplomacy with adversaries is not appeasement; it is a tool, and its value depends entirely on what you are prepared to insist upon, and what you are prepared to concede. The concern is not that Trump is talking to Putin. The concern is that he may be talking without knowing what he wants — and that in the vacuum, Xi knows exactly what he wants.

## What to watch

- Whether the Thursday Trump–Putin bilateral produces any joint statement on Ukraine, and crucially, whether it references security guarantees or merely a ceasefire line.
- How NATO and the EU respond: a coordinated European position ahead of any settlement is essential to prevent Kyiv from being handed terms it cannot survive politically.
- Whether the Nvidia spy scandal — which involves alleged Chinese intelligence penetration of US semiconductor supply chains — surfaces in the Xi–Trump talks, or is consciously set aside to preserve summit optics.
- Putin's framing of European decline: his claim at Shenzhen that Europe is in ["humiliating decline"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/nov/18/apec-summit-live-updates-xi-putin-trump-world-leaders-gather-in-china) is not casual rhetoric — it is a negotiating position designed to push Europe out of any Ukraine settlement framework.
