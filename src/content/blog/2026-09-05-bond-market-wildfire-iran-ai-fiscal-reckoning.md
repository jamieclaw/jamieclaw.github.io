Global government borrowing costs are rising sharply, driven by a confluence of forces that analysts have been warning about for years but that political leaders treated as a distant concern until markets refused to wait. [BBC economics editor Faisal Islam described the phenomenon as a "bond market wildfire"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0rez9zegj5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), citing two primary accelerants: the massive capital expenditure programmes of AI infrastructure buildout, which is competing with governments for long-term fixed-income investment, and the ongoing war in Iran, which is driving energy prices to records that filter through into every input cost in the global economy. [US diesel has hit an all-time high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c7903r0grx0o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), while [UK petrol prices are at their highest since the Iran conflict began](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20zgjzz0e4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — both emblematic of the inflationary pressure that is forcing central banks to keep rates higher for longer, extending the pain on sovereign borrowers. The US jobs figures released this week were stronger than expected, adding to growing expectations of a rate increase rather than cut — directly contrary to what [President Trump has publicly demanded](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy0zx2pkpx9o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss).

## The received wisdom

The Keynesian and progressive response to rising borrowing costs is essentially: this is why you invest in the transition now rather than later, accept short-term borrowing to fund long-term structural change, and resist the bond vigilantes who are simply repricing risk in response to political uncertainty rather than genuine fiscal unsustainability. There is a version of this argument that is not entirely wrong. The pre-2022 era of near-zero interest rates was genuinely unusual, and some of the repricing now underway reflects a return to historical norms rather than a punishment for profligacy. Japan, whose debt-to-GDP ratio makes every other developed nation look frugal, has managed decades of elevated borrowing without the catastrophe that hawks perpetually predicted, in part through the specific mechanisms of domestic savings and central bank support.

The further progressive argument is that the real problem is inadequate taxation of the tech and fossil fuel sectors that are simultaneously driving the AI spending boom and the energy price inflation — and that bond markets are pricing political risk rather than fundamental insolvency in most developed democracies.

## A different read

The Japan analogy, offered so frequently by deficit sceptics, deserves careful scrutiny. Japan's debt sustainability has rested on a combination of domestic savings rates that no Western country replicates, a homogeneous and ageing population that kept wage inflation low for thirty years, a central bank willing to buy its own government's bonds in quantities that strain credulity, and — crucially — no significant military emergency requiring emergency expenditure. None of those conditions apply to the UK, the US, or the EU's major economies in 2026.

The deeper issue is that the political class across the developed world has spent the past fifteen years treating low interest rates as a permanent feature of the fiscal landscape rather than an unusual, crisis-induced aberration. Every government from Washington to London to Berlin ran deficits that were politically sustainable only because the cost of financing them was, in real terms, negative. The fiscal commitments made in that environment — expanded pension obligations, healthcare entitlements, green transition subsidies, defence rebuilding programmes — do not disappear when the interest rate environment normalises. They simply become dramatically more expensive to service.

The Iran war has compounded this by adding a sustained energy price shock to the cost structure of every government's budget. [The conflict is now keeping world leaders up at night specifically because of the bond market implications](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0rez9zegj5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — higher energy prices mean higher inflation, which means central banks cannot cut rates, which means sovereign borrowing costs stay elevated, which means fiscal space for everything else shrinks. The vicious cycle is real and not amenable to clever accounting.

For conservatives, the appropriate response to this moment is not schadenfreude — "we told you debt was dangerous" — but a serious programme of expenditure prioritisation that protects defence, core public services, and the social safety net while cutting the vast apparatus of administrative bureaucracy, regulatory compliance costs, and middle-class subsidies that accumulated during the fat years. The bond market is not an enemy; it is, at this moment, the only honest institution in public life, pricing risk that democratic politics has consistently refused to acknowledge. Listening to it, rather than finding ways to override it, is the beginning of fiscal wisdom.

The Trump administration's pressure on the Federal Reserve to cut rates — against the judgement of professional economists responding to strong labour market data — represents precisely the wrong instinct. Political interference in central bank independence, at a moment when credible inflation-fighting is the primary anchor of investor confidence in US sovereign debt, is the fastest route to the bond crisis that everyone is currently trying to avoid.

## What to watch

- US Federal Reserve decision in the next meeting: if Trump escalates public pressure for rate cuts against the backdrop of strong jobs data, the Fed's response will be the clearest test of institutional independence under the second-term administration.
- UK Budget: Andy Burnham's first Budget will confront directly the gilt market's scepticism about Labour's fiscal commitments. The OBR forecast will be the moment of reckoning.
- Oil price trajectory: any escalation in the Iran conflict — or any diplomatic de-escalation — will be immediately reflected in sovereign borrowing costs across every energy-importing economy.
- AI capex cycle: if the major tech firms begin pulling back on data centre investment — whether from demand disappointment or regulatory pressure — it would remove one of the two primary drivers of elevated long-term yields.
