The United States and Japan confirmed this week that they jointly intervened in currency markets last week to halt a slide in the yen that had pushed it to a 40-year low against the dollar. [Japan's Ministry of Finance and US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cglj1pr0wjwo) both confirmed the action and said they would not hesitate to repeat it. Bank of Japan data indicated that Tokyo may have sold nearly $59 billion in US dollars to buy yen on Thursday alone, before Friday's confirmed joint intervention with Washington. A Reuters photograph of a notepad visible in front of Bessent at a cabinet meeting on Friday read: "To Do: Buy Japanese Yen $5-10 bil." The dollar fell to 157.07 yen after President Trump told reporters on Sunday that Japan "wanted a little bit of help. And we're always there for Japan" — the yen's strongest point in three months, having recently hit 164 against the dollar. The intervention is the first coordinated US-Japan currency action since 2011, when both countries moved in concert after the devastating Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami.

The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 1 percent in June — its highest level since September 1995 — but that rate remains dramatically below the US Federal Reserve's range of 3.50 to 3.75 percent, leaving the structural interest-rate differential that has weakened the yen largely intact.

## The received wisdom

The standard market-liberal reading holds that currency interventions of this kind are at best temporary fixes and at worst counterproductive. Markets, on this view, are reflecting underlying economic fundamentals: Japan has structurally lower rates, an ageing population that shrinks the working-age base, low productivity growth, and heavy dependence on dollar-priced energy imports. The yen is weak because Japan's economy is structurally weak, and no amount of central-bank dollar-selling changes that. Interventions are expensive signals that eventually get overwhelmed by the market; the Bank of Japan spent approximately $60 billion defending the yen in 2022, and the currency continued to depreciate in subsequent years. The progressive critique adds that yen weakness disproportionately harms ordinary Japanese consumers paying higher prices for food and energy imports, and that the real solution is structural reform, higher wages, and genuine monetary normalisation — all of which the Bank of Japan has moved too slowly to accomplish.

These arguments deserve serious weight. Japan's demographic trajectory is one of the most severe of any large economy. And the interest-rate gap between the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan will not be closed by intervention alone.

## A different read

But the market-purist framing misses two important dimensions: why the US has chosen to participate, and what the broader strategic context is.

On the first question: the US has not historically been enthusiastic about interventions that strengthen the yen, because a stronger yen tends to reduce Japanese export competitiveness and, by implication, benefits American manufacturers. The fact that Scott Bessent — a former hedge-fund macro trader who is well acquainted with the mechanics of currency markets — agreed to joint intervention signals that Washington's calculus has shifted. The Oxford Economics explanation offered by Shigeto Nagai is instructive: ["The United States agreed to participate in the coordinated intervention because it serves its national interests by offering the prospect of significant benefits at a low cost."](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cglj1pr0wjwo) What are those benefits? A stable yen reduces capital outflows from Japan into US Treasury markets. A yen in freefall encourages Japanese institutional investors — the largest holders of US government debt outside the Federal Reserve — to repatriate capital to cover domestic losses, which would push up US borrowing costs at a moment when the federal deficit is already under strain from Iran war spending.

This is the buried logic of the intervention: the US is not helping Japan out of alliance sentiment alone. It is protecting its own borrowing costs. The fact that Bessent's notepad specified a $5–10 billion purchase range — modest compared to Japan's own $59 billion outlay — suggests Washington's primary contribution was political legitimacy and deterrence rather than raw firepower. As Nagai noted, "the prolonged sense of vigilance regarding intervention will be effective in deterring speculators" even if the actual sums are not large.

The historical parallel worth invoking is not 2011 but the Plaza Accord of 1985, when the Reagan administration convened the G5 finance ministers at New York's Plaza Hotel to engineer a deliberate depreciation of the dollar against the yen and Deutsche mark. Plaza succeeded not because of the size of the initial intervention but because it signalled unambiguous political will. The 2026 joint action is a structurally similar exercise: markets now know that Washington is willing to act, which changes the risk calculus for speculative short-yen positions.

The wider implication for currency diplomacy is significant. For four decades, the post-Plaza norm was that the US would tolerate exchange-rate diplomacy only in extremis, and that bilateral currency discussions happened quietly through the IMF's Article IV consultations. The current administration has broken that norm repeatedly — through tariff threats that are explicitly linked to exchange-rate demands, through direct presidential commentary on the dollar, and now through a publicly confirmed bilateral intervention. [Whether this produces durable yen stability or simply encourages markets to test the next round of intervention remains to be seen](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/03/yen-trump-currency-us-japan), but it has already altered what currency policy looks like in a world of US-allied great-power competition.

The irony is that the same administration that ran on a strong-dollar, pro-manufacturer platform is now spending US resources to prop up the yen — a measure that weakens the relative value of US exports. Electoral politics and geopolitical alliance management have, once again, overridden the economist's preferred equilibrium.

## What to watch

- **Bank of Japan rate path**: Whether the BoJ accelerates its normalisation timeline to narrow the interest-rate differential — the only durable solution to yen weakness.
- **Speculative positioning**: Whether hedge funds test the intervention by rebuilding short-yen positions, forcing a second round of coordinated purchases.
- **Japanese bond market**: The joint intervention is partly about protecting the JGB market; any spike in Japanese 10-year yields would signal that investors are not convinced.
- **Dollar-yen at year-end**: If the pair drifts back above 160, the intervention will be judged a temporary reprieve rather than a turning point.
