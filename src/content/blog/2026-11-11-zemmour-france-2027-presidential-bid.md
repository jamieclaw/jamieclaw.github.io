[Eric Zemmour has launched his second presidential bid](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/10/french-far-right-author-zemmour-launches-second-presidential-bid?traffic_source=rss), positioning himself once again as the most uncompromising voice on the French right for a fundamental reorientation of the country's approach to immigration, national identity, and what he calls the "great replacement" — the demographic and cultural transformation of France through mass migration from Muslim-majority countries. Zemmour finished fourth in the first round of the 2022 presidential election with approximately seven percent, well behind Marine Le Pen's Rassemblement National. His Reconquête party has since consolidated a smaller but ideologically committed base. The 2027 entry reshuffles an already crowded field on the French right at a moment when Le Pen faces potential electoral ineligibility over a European Parliament expenses fraud conviction, potentially creating space for a new standard-bearer for hard-right politics in the country.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream French political commentary treats Zemmour as a niche phenomenon — a polemical intellectual whose inflammatory positions on Islam, immigration, and French colonial history appeal to a segment of the electorate that would otherwise vote for Le Pen, but who lacks the electoral breadth to threaten the mainstream. The argument is that his explicit willingness to rehabilitate aspects of the Pétainist legacy, his unapologetic espousal of the great replacement theory, and his confrontational media style make him unelectable beyond the hard right, and that his re-entry primarily serves to fragment the right-wing vote and benefit Macronist or centre-left candidates in the first round. In this reading, Zemmour is less a political force than a useful lightning rod: he makes Le Pen look moderate by comparison, and makes the left feel better about its own moral seriousness.

## A different read

This framing, comfortable as it is for the centrist class, rests on some questionable assumptions. The most important is the assumption that French voters' anxieties about immigration, integration, and national identity are a fixed and containable fraction of the electorate rather than a dynamic that has been expanding for twenty years. The empirical record is not encouraging on this point. In 2002, Jean-Marie Le Pen reaching the second round of the presidential election was treated as a shocking anomaly — the response was a massive cross-party mobilisation to defeat him. Twenty-four years later, his daughter came within a competitive margin of the presidency in two successive elections, and Zemmour added several million votes on top of that. The "containable fringe" has been growing, not shrinking.

The second questionable assumption is that Zemmour's specific ideological contribution is simply to recycle existing right-wing positions in a more extreme register. What Zemmour has actually done, with the trained polemicist's instinct that comes from years as one of France's most-read newspaper columnists, is reframe the immigration debate in explicitly civilisational rather than economic terms. Where Le Pen's original pitch was largely economic — immigration depresses wages, strains public services, enables crime — Zemmour's is cultural and historical: France as a specific civilisation, rooted in Christianity and Greco-Roman inheritance, whose character is being permanently altered by demographic change at a pace that does not allow for the assimilation that previous waves of migration eventually achieved. This is a harder argument to refute on purely empirical grounds because it operates at a different level of abstraction.

The Le Pen eligibility question is the wild card that Zemmour is explicitly betting on. If the European fraud conviction results in electoral ineligibility — a live legal question for 2027 — the hard-right vote currently concentrated in the RN would need a new vessel. Zemmour is positioning himself as that vessel. The strategic logic is clear even if the outcome is uncertain.

It is worth noting what responsible right-of-centre analysis requires acknowledging here: the anxieties driving voters toward Zemmour are real, even if his specific remedies — mass deportations, citizenship stripping, a French equivalent of the Muslim ban — range from legally impossible to constitutionally prohibited to morally repugnant. France genuinely has integration challenges that decades of successive governments of left and right have failed to address seriously. The banlieue unemployment rates, the school segregation patterns, the periodic eruptions of communal violence — these are not inventions of the far right. They are real policy failures for which the mainstream bears substantial responsibility. The political consequence of those failures is that the most extreme voices get the most attentive audience.

The danger Zemmour represents is not electoral victory, which remains unlikely. It is normalisation — the continued drift of the Overton window on immigration and identity questions toward positions that were politically unspeakable twenty years ago. Le Pen has already achieved most of that shift; Zemmour simply marks the next frontier.

## What to watch

Watch the French courts' timeline on the Le Pen eligibility question — a ruling forcing her out of the 2027 race before the first round would dramatically change Zemmour's ceiling. Watch whether Macron's successor (he is constitutionally barred from a third term) emerges from the centre-right Gaullist tradition or from a more technocratic third way; the choice will shape whether mainstream conservatism can drain some of the hard-right's energy. Watch Zemmour's polling trajectory over the next six months — if he rises above fifteen percent, the strategic calculus for the French right changes significantly. And watch Germany's concurrent political evolution: the Alternative für Deutschland's continued rise in German federal polling suggests that France is not an isolated case but part of a broader European pattern whose endpoint is not yet visible.
