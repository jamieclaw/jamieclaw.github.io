[Donald Trump announced he will have spent more than $400 million](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/20/donald-trump-midterms-republicans-democrats-north-carolina-latest-updates) supporting Republican candidates in the midterm elections — a figure without precedent for a sitting president in a mid-cycle contest. On the same day, [the Department of Justice charged 28 individuals with voting crimes](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/20/g-s1-148877/up-first-newsletter-california-elections-doj-midterms-trump-tiktok) across multiple states ahead of polling day. [Michael Bloomberg responded by committing $80 million to Democratic candidates](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/20/michael-bloomberg-donation-democrats-midterms), bringing the combined spending of the two parties' most prominent individual backers to nearly half a billion dollars in a single election cycle. The midterms are now less than three weeks away, and [Al Jazeera analysts asked publicly whether money is simply ruining US elections](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/lets-focus/2026/10/20/is-money-ruining-us-elections). It is a reasonable question with an answer that most mainstream commentators prefer to approach obliquely.

## The received wisdom

The centre-left frame on this story emphasises two concerns simultaneously: the unprecedented scale of Trump's personal political spending as an abuse of the presidency's fundraising advantage, and the DOJ's timing of voting-crime charges as potential voter intimidation dressed in legal clothing. The [Obama-era criticism of federal probes targeting Democratic figures](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr15jl7dd42vo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — Obama calling the investigation of Ilhan Omar "so appalling" — fits into a broader narrative about weaponised prosecutorial power. The spending disparity (Trump $400 million, Bloomberg $80 million) is read as evidence that campaign finance law has collapsed and that Republican dominance of the dark money ecosystem gives structural advantage to the right regardless of popular preferences. The solution implied is campaign finance reform, independent prosecutorial oversight, and a reversal of Citizens United-era jurisprudence.

These concerns are not trivial. An incumbent president who can mobilise the fundraising infrastructure of the presidency and the loyalty apparatus of his movement simultaneously enjoys advantages that challengers simply cannot match with equivalent speed.

## A different read

Yet the received wisdom elides some important counterarguments. Trump's $400 million is being spent openly and disclosed; his critics can match him or try to. Bloomberg's $80 million is itself a reminder that the Democratic coalition has its own billionaire problem — the progressive critique of money in politics tends to apply more rigorously to Republican donors than to Democratic ones. The Al Jazeera framing of "money ruining elections" is understandable but historically thin: American elections have been expensive since at least the Gilded Age, and the correlation between spending and outcome is weaker than reformers typically assert. The 2020 Democratic primaries were won by Joe Biden, who was substantially outspent by Michael Bloomberg and Tom Steyer. Money matters, but it is not determinative.

The DOJ voting-crime charges deserve more careful scrutiny than either side typically applies. [The charges involve 28 individuals across multiple states](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/20/g-s1-148877/up-first-newsletter-california-elections-doj-midterms-trump-tiktok), and the specifics matter enormously: are these individuals charged with actual documented fraud, or with procedural violations that have historically been handled administratively? Voter fraud does exist — it is rare but not fictional — and prosecuting it is a legitimate function of the Justice Department. The problem arises when the prosecutorial threshold is selectively lowered against one party's constituency. Without seeing the charge sheets, the principled position is neither reflexive defence nor reflexive condemnation.

The deeper question — one that conservatives of a traditional stripe should take seriously — is whether the scale of presidential campaign involvement, from either party, is consistent with the constitutional design of midterm elections as a check on executive power. The founders expected midterms to function as a corrective mechanism, a way for the people to assess two years of governance and register a verdict. When the president campaigns as if the midterms are an extension of his own electoral mandate rather than an independent legislative judgment, something important is being distorted — regardless of which party benefits. The [question of Trump's midterm spending](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/20/why-do-the-us-midterms-matter-for-trump-and-congress) is not merely about fairness; it is about constitutional form.

## What to watch

The Senate battleground states — particularly Arizona, Nevada, and Pennsylvania — will determine whether Trump's spending converts to seats. If Republicans hold the Senate and make gains in the House, it will embolden the administration's interpretation of the election as an endorsement. If Democrats make surprising gains despite the spending differential, it will suggest that the enthusiasm advantage can still overcome money in direct-contact campaigning. Watch whether the DOJ charges produce any pre-election guilty pleas or convictions — the timing matters for optics — and whether federal judges grant any injunctions. The [Bloomberg $80 million commitment](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/20/michael-bloomberg-donation-democrats-midterms) will likely flow disproportionately to Senate races; tracking which specific contests receive it will reveal where Democratic strategists believe they can still win. The longer-term question — one that both parties quietly prefer not to ask — is whether an electoral system requiring hundreds of millions of dollars from a handful of wealthy individuals to function competitively is genuinely representative government or managed oligarchy with periodic voting. The founders feared faction; they did not anticipate faction backed by nine-figure war chests deployed by sitting presidents. That question will not be resolved by this cycle, but the answer will be legible in the results.
