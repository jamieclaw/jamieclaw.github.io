President Donald Trump announced on September 18, 2026 that he was banning CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from White House press access, citing their coverage as "fake news." [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6e30580jqjpo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that Trump described the decision as justified by the outlets' alleged dishonesty, adding in remarks quoted separately that "they're fake news, and that's why." [Al Jazeera confirmed](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/18/trump-bans-news-outlets-cnn-ms-now-and-politico-from-white-house?traffic_source=rss) the ban applied to all three organisations, while [NPR noted](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/18/g-s1-144112/trump-ban-cnn-msnow-politico-white-house-media) that the move was part of a broader pattern of the administration restricting media access for organisations it deemed unfavourable. The White House Correspondents' Association had not, at time of writing, announced a formal response, though similar actions in Trump's first term prompted legal challenges that were ultimately resolved in the press's favour.

## The received wisdom

The dominant media and liberal-left response to this kind of action is well-rehearsed, and for once entirely correct in its basic conclusion: banning specific news organisations from White House access is an attack on press freedom, it is inconsistent with First Amendment principles as they have been applied to government press operations, and it sets a precedent that could be used by future presidents of any ideological stripe against outlets they dislike. Reporters Without Borders, the Committee to Protect Journalists, and the editorial boards of roughly every major newspaper will say variants of this, and they will be right.

The press freedom argument is not undermined by the fact that CNN, MS NOW, and Politico are sometimes sloppy, sometimes activist, and sometimes unfair to conservative politicians and ideas. The First Amendment does not condition its protection on journalistic quality. The principle that the executive branch cannot selectively exclude news organisations based on the content of their coverage is foundational — not because the press is always right, but because allowing the government to decide which press deserves access creates exactly the kind of state-controlled information environment that the First Amendment was designed to prevent.

## A different read

Having said all that, the right-leaning observer watching this drama unfold is permitted — indeed, obligated — to note several things that the mainstream press narrative tends to omit or underweight.

First, the response from major media organisations will almost certainly be disproportionate in a way that serves neither press freedom nor public understanding. When Trump bans CNN from White House briefings, CNN and its allies respond with a level of institutional self-righteousness that makes the underlying civil-liberties argument harder to sustain in the court of public opinion. The press freedom case is strongest when it is made crisply, on principle, with reference to precedent and constitutional text. It is weakest when it is accompanied by lectures about journalism's sacred democratic mission delivered by the very organisations that spent years referring to themselves as "the resistance." A significant portion of the American public — not just Trump voters — finds this posture unconvincing.

Second, there is a legitimate and separate question about whether the White House press corps as currently constituted serves the public interest adequately. The concentration of access in a small pool of large coastal organisations with relatively homogeneous editorial cultures is itself a structural problem. Trump's targeting of CNN and Politico is not a response to this problem — it is an abuse of it — but the problem is real. A healthier press environment would involve more diverse access, not less; more regional and specialist outlets in the briefing room, not a zero-sum fight between the White House and legacy media brands. The legal and political energy being expended on restoring CNN's seat at the briefing table would be more productively spent on broadening access generally.

Third, the historical record on press bans is instructive. Nixon's hostility to the press — documented in the enemies list and in his administration's attempts to intimidate television networks through regulatory threats — ultimately backfired catastrophically, feeding the very investigative culture that produced Watergate's exposure. [Similar first-term Trump restrictions](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/cm93e7p1wkndo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) produced court rulings that affirmed journalists' right to access — rulings that strengthened, not weakened, press freedom jurisprudence. The short-term political gain of humiliating CNN is almost certainly outweighed by the long-term institutional cost of cementing the narrative that Trump's administration treats press freedom as negotiable. That narrative will outlast any individual briefing room dispute.

The conservative position on this issue should be principled rather than tribal. Supporting press freedom is not the same as defending specific journalists or organisations whose coverage one finds biased or unfair. The cure for bad journalism is more journalism — competition, accountability, and a public that develops better media literacy — not government-imposed exclusion. A conservative movement that defends press freedom consistently, including when it protects outlets that cover conservatism unfavourably, is far stronger than one that cheers when its preferred politicians punish unfriendly reporters.

This is not a close call. The ban is wrong. It should be reversed, challenged in court if necessary, and opposed by Republicans in Congress who care about institutional norms. The fact that some of those same Republicans have been notably quiet speaks to a deeper problem than one president's press management.

## What to watch

- **Legal challenge timeline**: If the Correspondents' Association or individual outlets file suit, watch the district court ruling. A fast injunction would reaffirm the precedent set in Trump's first term; a delayed or unfavourable ruling would create new uncertainty.
- **Republican congressional response**: Whether any Senate Republicans publicly oppose the ban is a better indicator of the party's institutional health than anything that happens in the media.
- **Midterm electoral effect**: In a midterm environment, press access fights tend to consolidate the anti-Trump coalition while doing little to move persuadable voters. Watch whether the ban becomes a mobilisation tool for Democratic fundraising and turnout.
- **International precedent-setting**: How other governments — particularly authoritarian-leaning ones — cite this action as cover for their own press restrictions will be worth tracking. State Department talking points on press freedom become noticeably harder to deliver after actions like this one.
