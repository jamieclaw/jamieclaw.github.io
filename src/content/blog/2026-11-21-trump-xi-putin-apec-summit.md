For two days in Shenzhen, the leaders of the three nuclear superpowers shared the same stage at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit — an arrangement that would have seemed fantastical a decade ago. Donald Trump met separately with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin before all three endorsed a joint communiqué on Thursday. [The declaration](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/19/china-russia-us-agree-peace-statement-no-mention-ukraine-iran-wars), as reported by the Guardian, outlined priorities including reform of the United Nations, boosting global oil supplies, and the protection of diplomatic immunity. What it did not mention was rather more conspicuous: the ongoing war in Ukraine, which Russia is prosecuting, or the US-led war against Iran. Trump, at a White House press conference the following day, [offered little in the way of concrete results](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/20/nx-s1-6047594/trump-asia-trip-xi-putin-press-conference), saying he had "fantastic relationships" with both leaders while deflecting on specifics.

## The received wisdom

The standard liberal interpretation is that this summit represents a dangerous normalisation of authoritarian power. By treating Putin as a legitimate peer statesman rather than a war criminal, the argument goes, Trump has legitimised the invasion of Ukraine and undermined the rules-based international order that the West spent eighty years constructing. The declaration's silence on Ukraine is taken as proof that Trump traded away Ukrainian interests for the optics of a historic photo opportunity. Progressive commentators also note the irony of a "peace declaration" issued by the two countries most actively involved in hot wars — Russia in Ukraine, the US in Iran — making no mention of either conflict. The summit, in this reading, is theatre designed to ratify a new great-power spheres-of-influence arrangement at the expense of smaller nations.

## A different read

There is something to that critique, but it mistakes the weather for the climate. The more historically literate framing is that great-power communication has always been preferable to great-power silence, and that the alternative to this summit was not a principled rules-based order but a continuing slide toward direct superpower confrontation. Henry Kissinger spent his career arguing — not wrongly — that stable international systems require the major powers to have working channels, however distasteful their interlocutors. The mistake would be to confuse a talking shop with a peace settlement.

The specific content of the declaration is worth parsing more carefully than the headlines suggest. The references to UN reform and diplomatic immunity are, beneath the boilerplate, pointed: they reflect a shared interest among all three powers in constraining the International Court of Justice and the International Criminal Court, both of which have recently issued rulings uncomfortable for all three governments. [The ICJ's unprecedented genocide ruling against Myanmar this week](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/19/genocide-rohingya-international-court-of-justice-ruling-myanmar) — and its implications for future cases involving Israel and potentially the Iran war — provides direct context for why Washington, Beijing, and Moscow would collectively want to signal scepticism toward expansive international judicial reach.

The oil-supply language is similarly functional rather than decorative. All three economies benefit from price stability, and the implicit signal to OPEC+ nations is that the great powers are aligned on preventing a supply shock that could derail their own fiscal positions. This is old-fashioned realpolitik, not ideological solidarity.

What troubles the right-of-centre analyst is not that Trump talked to Putin — talking to adversaries is not capitulation — but what appears to have been given in return for the atmospherics. [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/20/nx-s1-6047594/trump-asia-trip-xi-putin-press-conference) that Trump offered little in actual results beyond the joint statement. At the same press conference, Trump defended his relationship with Putin while threatening new tariffs on American tech firms building data centres abroad — an unrelated populist intervention that suggests the foreign policy coherence of the trip is thin. Historic summits require historic deliverables. The Nixon opening to China, the Reagan-Gorbachev Reykjavik meeting, even the imperfect Helsinki Accords — all produced documents that, whatever their limitations, defined the terms of subsequent engagement. A joint statement that omits the two most active wars on Earth is not architecture; it is a family photograph.

There is also the European dimension. [BBC Politics reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckzd9nln5e6go) that European leaders, meeting in Brussels on the same day as the APEC summit, declared themselves ready for talks with Russia to end the war in Ukraine — a move that reflects growing European exhaustion with a conflict that, with American attention now focused on the Iran theatre, may be entering a new and more unstable phase. The divergence between Washington's embrace of the summit's optics and Europe's anxious watch from the sidelines is the structural fault line the Shenzhen communiqué has exposed rather than resolved.

The historical parallel that keeps occurring to this reader is not Nixon-Mao but the 1938 Munich Conference — not because Trump is Chamberlain, a comparison that is both lazy and unfair, but because of the structural temptation of great-power convergence: the desire to mistake the absence of immediate conflict for a durable settlement. What looks like statesmanship in the short run can look like recklessness in the long run, depending entirely on whether the underlying territorial and ideological disputes are genuinely managed or merely postponed.

## What to watch

Watch whether the APEC declaration produces any follow-on diplomatic mechanism: a trilateral hotline, a working group on the wars, or any structured process. If it doesn't — if the summit produces no institutional residue — it will confirm the sceptic's view that this was spectacle. Watch the European response: if Burnham's newly reset UK-EU relationship produces a joint statement on Ukraine that explicitly contradicts the APEC communiqué's silence, that transatlantic divergence becomes the dominant story of the winter. Watch Congress, where Democrats are already seeking to block Iran war funding — a leverage point that could force Trump to show his hand on what he actually agreed to in Shenzhen. And watch oil futures: if prices move sharply in the week following the summit, it will tell you that the market read the oil-supply language as a concrete signal rather than decoration.
