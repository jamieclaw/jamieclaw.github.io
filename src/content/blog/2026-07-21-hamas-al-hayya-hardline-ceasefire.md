Hamas has elected Khalil al-Hayya as its new overall leader, [according to a statement from the organisation](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx25nzkyj50o). Al-Hayya, 65, who already headed Hamas in Gaza from exile, replaces Yahya Sinwar, killed by Israeli forces in October 2024. The election was contested: the first round was inconclusive, with former political bureau chief Khaled Meshaal narrowly winning more votes but failing to reach the required threshold of 36 of 69 electoral college votes. A second-round run-off on a first-past-the-post basis produced al-Hayya's victory. The result reflects a strategic divide within Hamas: Meshaal's supporters argued for drawing lessons from the war, moving away from armed confrontation, and adopting a more pragmatic political approach. Al-Hayya's faction — drawing its authority from the Gaza leadership that bore the war's full brunt — successfully argued that Gaza should lead the movement and that major concessions, particularly on disarmament, were unacceptable without a guaranteed political path to Palestinian statehood. Al-Hayya survived an Israeli air strike in Doha in September 2025 in which five Hamas members and a Qatari security guard were killed; three of his sons have been killed in various Israeli strikes. More than 73,280 Palestinians have been killed in the conflict, including 1,158 since the ceasefire began, according to the Hamas-run health ministry.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream analysis of this election will emphasise the humanity of the choice: a man who has lost multiple sons, who represents a community that has suffered catastrophic destruction, who was the chief negotiator through the most difficult ceasefire talks in the conflict's history. Al-Hayya's election, on this reading, reflects the legitimate grievances of a people who have watched their territory reduced to rubble and who understandably reject the demand that they disarm before any political path to self-determination is even defined. The US-led Board of Peace plan, which prioritises immediate demilitarisation over reconstruction — and which Hamas has rejected — can reasonably be criticised as asking Palestinians to surrender their only remaining leverage before any of their core demands are addressed. A pragmatist like Meshaal, who spent his career in comfortable exile while Gaza burned, was always a problematic figure to lead the post-war movement. The argument that Gaza's surviving leadership deserves to set the political direction is not without moral force.

## A different read

There is a distinction between understanding why al-Hayya won and being sanguine about what his victory means.

Al-Hayya is, on the available evidence, a maximalist. His political career is defined by his proximity to Sinwar's vision — a vision that produced the October 7 attack and the subsequent war. He has explicitly stated that Hamas will not give up its weapons without a guaranteed political path to statehood. That is not merely a negotiating position; it is a structural veto on any ceasefire framework that requires prior demilitarisation as a condition of reconstruction. The US-led Board of Peace plan may be imperfect, but its sequencing — security first, then political process — reflects a logic that has some historical warrant. The Palestinian Authority in the West Bank maintains a degree of political legitimacy precisely because it is, however imperfectly, a non-armed political actor. Hamas's insistence on retaining weapons while demanding political recognition puts it in a category that no mediating party — not Egypt, not Qatar, not the US — has been able to move beyond.

The strategic divide al-Hayya's election resolves within Hamas is precisely the divide that outside mediators were counting on to create space for a deal. Meshaal's faction represented a possible off-ramp: a Hamas leadership that could credibly claim it was responding to the war's lessons and moving toward a political, rather than military, model. That off-ramp is now closed, or at least much more difficult to access. This matters not because the outside world should determine Hamas's internal political direction, but because the practical consequence of al-Hayya's leadership is that the ceasefire framework is less likely to evolve into a durable political arrangement and more likely to remain a frozen conflict — permanent enough that Gaza's reconstruction cannot begin, temporary enough that the violence can resume at any moment.

There is a historical parallel worth noting. After the 1993 Oslo Accords, the Palestinian political process was repeatedly undermined by internal divisions between those who accepted the framework and those who rejected it through violence. The pattern — a pragmatist faction capable of political accommodation undercut by a hardline faction with military capacity — is a recurring one in national liberation movements. The IRA's evolution in the 1990s required both Sinn Féin's willingness to accept a political path and the IRA's eventual, grudging agreement to decommission. What distinguished Northern Ireland from the Gaza situation is that the IRA's political wing — Sinn Féin — eventually gained sufficient democratic legitimacy that it could constrain the military wing's veto power. Hamas has no equivalent separation.

Israel's response to al-Hayya's election will be significant. If Netanyahu treats it as grounds for resuming major military operations rather than as an internal organisational matter, the ceasefire will end. If the US and mediating parties treat it as a reason to recalibrate the ceasefire framework to address Hamas's stated concerns about sequencing, it might hold. Neither of those outcomes is straightforward. [Trump has already rejected New York Mayor Mamdani's threat to arrest Netanyahu for war crimes](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/20/trump-rejects-mamdani-threat-to-arrest-), which signals that the US is not currently in a posture of pressuring Israel for restraint.

## What to watch

- **Hamas's response to disarmament proposals**: Whether al-Hayya's first public statements on the Board of Peace plan harden Hamas's negotiating position or merely maintain it.
- **Mediators' response**: Egypt and Qatar have been the principal ceasefire brokers. Whether they continue to engage al-Hayya with the same intensity as they engaged his predecessor will indicate whether the back-channel is still functional.
- **The civilian death toll since ceasefire**: 1,158 killed since the ceasefire began is a figure that, if it continues to rise, will make the political environment for any new framework significantly more hostile.
- **West Bank dynamics**: Al-Hayya's Gaza-centric authority base may create complications for any Hamas-Fatah reconciliation process that would be necessary for a unified Palestinian political actor capable of negotiating statehood.
