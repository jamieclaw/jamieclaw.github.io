Five European Union member states — reportedly including Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Austria, and Denmark — have announced plans to establish joint migrant deportation and processing centres in Rwanda, following a framework similar to the now-defunct UK-Rwanda scheme. The announcement came on the margins of a summit in Brussels and marks the first coordinated multilateral effort by EU governments to offshore asylum processing to an African third country. The scheme would involve deporting irregular migrants who cannot demonstrate refugee status to Rwandan-run facilities, where their cases would be adjudicated outside EU territory. Rwanda has agreed in principle; details of the financial arrangement have not been disclosed. The move follows years of surging irregular arrivals across the Mediterranean and the English Channel and comes amid record political pressure from right-of-centre parties across the continent.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive critique of offshore processing schemes will be familiar: they are expensive, legally dubious, morally tainted by association with authoritarian recipient states, and do little to address the root causes of migration. When Britain tried a comparable arrangement with Rwanda under Rishi Sunak, it was [blocked by the UK Supreme Court](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw987343352go) on human rights grounds before a single flight departed, and the scheme was eventually scrapped by the incoming Labour government. Critics will argue that the EU version faces the same legal obstacles under the European Convention on Human Rights and the EU's own Charter of Fundamental Rights. Refugee advocates point out that Rwanda — however stable it may be — is an authoritarian state with a poor record of political pluralism and press freedom, and that sending vulnerable people there for processing is itself a human rights violation. The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees has consistently opposed offshoring as incompatible with the 1951 Refugee Convention.

There is also the practical objection: the UK spent hundreds of millions of pounds on its Rwanda scheme and never deported a single person. What makes anyone think five EU governments, with their more complex legal environment, will do better?

## A different read

These objections are not without force, but they miss something important: the political logic driving this policy is not irrational, and dismissing it as moral panic is itself a form of analytical failure.

Europe has spent three decades oscillating between open-border idealism and enforcement paralysis. The result is a system in which borders exist on paper but not in practice, where the gap between stated policy and lived reality has become so wide that it fuels precisely the populist movements that liberals most fear. The five governments announcing this Rwanda scheme are not fringe actors — they include Germany under a centre-right coalition and the Netherlands under a government shaped by [Geert Wilders's Freedom Party](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/16/eu-states-immigration-return-hubs-rwanda). They are responding to electoral mandates that are, by any reasonable measure, genuine expressions of democratic will.

The deeper question the Rwanda model raises is not whether it is morally pristine — it manifestly is not — but whether the alternative is better. The current system, in which the [EU's Dublin Regulation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/16/eu-states-immigration-return-hubs-rwanda) assigns asylum responsibility to the country of first arrival (usually Italy, Greece, or Spain) while richer northern states evade their obligations through procedural delay, is also morally problematic. It concentrates suffering at the periphery, overwhelms the reception systems of Mediterranean countries, and incentivises dangerous sea crossings by signalling that arrival equals eventual settlement. The body count in the Mediterranean is not a consequence of tough deterrence policies; it is partly a consequence of the implicit promise that the journey will ultimately pay off.

Historically, deterrence works at the margins in ways that liberal analysts underweight. Australia's offshore processing arrangements in Nauru and Papua New Guinea — [however ethically contested](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/16/eu-states-immigration-return-hubs-rwanda) — did result in a dramatic collapse in boat arrivals. Denmark, which has pushed further on deterrence than any other EU state, has seen irregular arrivals decline even as neighbouring countries' numbers rose. These are not coincidences. The signal matters, even if the operational capacity to enforce it remains limited.

There is also a question of institutional legitimacy. The European Court of Human Rights and the Court of Justice of the EU are genuinely powerful bodies, but their legitimacy depends partly on their operating within some democratic envelope. When courts consistently strike down policies that command majority support across multiple member states, the long-run risk is not that the courts win but that they lose — that governments begin structuring workarounds, as the UK's last Conservative government attempted with its Safety of Rwanda Act, or that Euroscepticism deepens precisely because the EU's legal architecture is perceived as an obstacle to democratic self-governance.

The more intelligent critique of the Rwanda model is not that it's wrong to try to control borders but that this particular scheme is likely to be costly, slow, and legally entangled enough to fail in practice while succeeding symbolically as a political gesture. That may be true. But if it is, the burden falls on the scheme's critics to propose something that actually works at scale — not merely to invoke international law as a conversation-stopper while boat crossings continue and far-right parties consolidate.

## What to watch

Watch whether the European Court of Human Rights issues an emergency injunction the moment the first deportation flight is scheduled — that legal confrontation, not the politics, will define the scheme's fate. Watch also whether the financial terms of the Rwanda deal become public; if the per-deportee cost approaches what the UK spent, the scheme may collapse under fiscal rather than legal pressure. And watch Germany most closely: [Berlin's willingness to participate](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw987343352go) signals that the mainstream European centre-right has decisively shifted, with implications for the EU's internal negotiating balance on migration for years to come.
