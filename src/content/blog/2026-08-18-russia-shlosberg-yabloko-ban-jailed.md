On Monday, a court in the Russian city of Pskov sentenced Lev Shlosberg, 63, the deputy leader of the liberal Yabloko party, to eleven years and one month in a penal colony for "discrediting" Russia's armed forces and spreading "false information" about them. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj4kjnle2neo) that the verdict was delivered minutes before Russia's Supreme Court separately rejected Yabloko's appeal against a ban on it standing in September's parliamentary elections. Yabloko was the only registered political party in Russia to oppose the full-scale invasion of Ukraine launched in February 2022. [Al Jazeera's coverage of the dual rulings](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/17/russia-maintains-election-ban-on-opposition-yabloko-jails-deputy-head) noted that dozens of the party's supporters and journalists were detained near the Pskov courthouse as the verdict was read. Police said the detentions were for public order offences. Shlosberg, from the dock, called the war a "catastrophe for Russia" and predicted that "the entire nation will shudder" when the full death toll is eventually published.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of this story treats Shlosberg's imprisonment and Yabloko's electoral erasure as sad but predictable data points in an already well-documented arc: Putin consolidating power, wartime censorship laws weaponised against critics, and Russia's democratic façade stripped away layer by layer. On this reading, the story confirms what most Western analysts already believe — that Russia has been an authoritarian state in practice for at least fifteen years, and that the invasion of Ukraine merely accelerated a process of closure that began with the jailing of Mikhail Khodorkovsky in 2003 and the effective destruction of independent media in the mid-2000s. The lesson, from this perspective, is that Western engagement with Russian civil society was always somewhat naïve, that sanctions have not reversed the political direction of travel, and that the story is primarily one of democratic backsliding now reaching its logical terminus. The appropriate response, this reading suggests, is continued support for Ukraine, renewed attention to information warfare, and patience until the Putin system eventually exhausts itself.

## A different read

This framing is not wrong, but it underweights what is actually new about Monday's events and what those events reveal about Russia's immediate political calculus ahead of September's elections. The timing is not incidental. Putin's United Russia party is polling at 32.1 percent — the lowest figure since May, according to Russia's state pollster VCIOM. That is still the plurality, and the Kremlin retains the apparatus to deliver whatever outcome it requires. But the decision to both ban Yabloko from the ballot and simultaneously jail its deputy leader on the same day — a simultaneity so choreographed it reads as deliberate messaging — suggests a level of institutional anxiety that goes beyond routine authoritarian housekeeping.

Historically, mature authoritarian regimes do not expend this much energy eliminating parties that are, by any reasonable measure, already powerless. The Soviet Communist Party did not bother jailing dissident philosophers when its polling numbers were comfortable. The fact that the Kremlin found it necessary to ban a party polling in the low single digits, and to jail its 63-year-old deputy leader on the day of the party's appeal, suggests that the symbolic stakes of even minimal visible dissent have become too high to tolerate. This is the behaviour of a regime that feels more brittle than its surface appearance of control suggests. As the historian Richard Pipes observed in his analysis of late-imperial Russia, systems that cannot tolerate symbolic opposition are often closer to rupture than they appear from the outside — not because dissidents are powerful, but because the repression itself reveals the ruling system's lack of confidence.

The Shlosberg prosecution also matters as a precedent for what Kremlin-style legal architecture looks like in practice. The charges — "discrediting" the armed forces and spreading "false information" — are drawn from laws passed after February 2022 that carry penalties of up to fifteen years. These laws were explicitly designed to criminalise political speech, not operational military intelligence. Using them against a 63-year-old regional politician who made statements on social media represents a deliberate escalation of their application into mainstream electoral politics. It establishes that any Russian citizen with a public profile who questions the war can be convicted under the same framework. That is not merely repression of a party; it is the permanent juridification of political loyalty.

There is also a European dimension that deserves more attention than it typically receives. [Finland on Monday halted the deportation of a Russian family](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/17/finland-halts-deportation-russian-family-opposed-ukraine-invasion) — Olga and Nikita Belov — who had fled Russia after opposing the invasion and faced asylum rejection. Their case is one of hundreds; European states are simultaneously providing military and financial support to Ukraine while deporting Russian anti-war refugees back to the country that is prosecuting people like Shlosberg. That contradiction is morally uncomfortable and strategically self-defeating. Russians willing to publicly oppose the war are exactly the kind of civil society actors whose preservation matters for any eventual post-Putin transition. Treating them as a migration problem rather than a political asset is a failure of strategic imagination.

## What to watch

- September's Russian parliamentary elections: whether United Russia's vote share falls below 30 percent and how the Kremlin manages any shortfall.
- The treatment of Shlosberg in custody: the deaths of Navalny and others in Russian detention have established that political prisoners face extraordinary physical risk; any deterioration in his condition will be a significant international signal.
- Whether other Yabloko figures face similar prosecutions — the party has hundreds of local-level elected officials whose future is now legally precarious.
- European refugee policy toward anti-war Russians: the Finnish reversal is a small positive sign, but there is no EU-wide framework for treating political opposition to the Russian government as grounds for protection.
