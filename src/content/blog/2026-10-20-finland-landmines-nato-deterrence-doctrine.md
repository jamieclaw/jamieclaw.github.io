Finland is seeking to acquire approximately one million land mines as part of a major expansion of its territorial defence posture, the Finnish defence ministry [announced this week](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/19/finland-seeking-one-million-land-mines-as-it-eyes-russian-threat), citing the ongoing threat from Russia following Moscow's invasion of Ukraine. The announcement comes despite the Ottawa Treaty on anti-personnel mines, from which Finland is not a signatory — unlike most NATO members — having retained the right to produce and stockpile mines as a core element of its defence doctrine since the treaty's adoption in 1999. Finland shares an 1,340-kilometre border with Russia, making it NATO's longest land frontier with Moscow since the country joined the alliance in April 2023. The mine acquisition would significantly increase Finland's capacity to slow a Russian mechanised advance along multiple axes. The announcement has drawn quiet attention from NATO planners and rather louder controversy from international humanitarian organisations, who argue that mines are inherently indiscriminate weapons regardless of the operational doctrine under which they are deployed.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian framing — that land mines are categorically unacceptable regardless of strategic context — has been the dominant Western position since Princess Diana championed the Ottawa process in 1997, and it retains considerable moral weight. Human Rights Watch and the International Campaign to Ban Landmines have documented the long-term civilian toll of mine contamination in former conflict zones from Angola to Cambodia to Bosnia, where poorly mapped minefields have continued to kill and maim civilians for decades after the fighting ended. The argument is not merely aesthetic: mines are weapons that cannot distinguish between soldiers and children once the tactical situation changes, and a country that deploys them is making a choice to impose costs on future populations who will have to live with the terrain. The mainstream position treats Finland's announcement as a troubling signal that NATO's expansion is pulling the alliance away from the humanitarian norms that distinguish it from its adversaries.

## A different read

The humanitarian argument is serious, but it is being applied in a context for which it was not principally designed. The Ottawa Treaty emerged from the experience of post-conflict mine contamination in countries like Cambodia and Mozambique — cases where mines were deployed chaotically, often by non-state actors or collapsing armies, without systematic mapping or demining plans, in terrain that would subsequently be occupied by civilian populations. Finland's doctrine is structurally different. It involves a country with an exceptionally well-trained reserve army, a detailed territorial defence plan for its own national territory, and a military culture that has maintained the most sophisticated anti-armour mine warfare doctrine in Western Europe precisely because Finnish geography — forests, lakes, and narrow corridors — makes mines uniquely effective as a force multiplier against a mechanised invader.

The historical context is essential. Finland fought two wars against the Soviet Union between 1939 and 1944 — the Winter War and the Continuation War — in which a country of four million people held off an adversary with an army of several million through exactly this combination of terrain exploitation, defensive fortification, and asymmetric attrition. Finnish military doctrine has never forgotten that experience. The ["Motti" tactics](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/19/finland-seeking-one-million-land-mines-as-it-eyes-russian-threat) of encircling and destroying Soviet columns in forested terrain — named for a unit of firewood — relied on channelling enemy forces into kill zones where firepower advantage was neutralised. Land mines are a natural extension of that doctrine into a modern context.

The broader point is about the credibility gap at the heart of European defence. [NATO's own assessments](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/19/finland-seeking-one-million-land-mines-as-it-eyes-russian-threat) since Russia's full-scale invasion have repeatedly identified a shortage of ammunition stocks, defensive fortifications, and the kind of attritional warfare materiel — shells, mines, field fortifications — that is less photogenic than F-35s but more immediately relevant to the scenario Russia presents. European governments have spent thirty years harvesting the "peace dividend" of the post-Cold War order, reducing conscript armies, selling off stockpiles, and treating defence as a budget line that could be deferred in favour of social programmes. Finland is one of the few countries that never fully joined this consensus — it maintained conscription, kept its reserve forces in training, and refused to reduce its defence spending below two percent of GDP. Its decision to acquire land mines is consistent with a strategic culture that has always priced deterrence credibility above diplomatic optics.

The Ottawa Treaty signatories who are now criticising Finland face an uncomfortable question: what do they propose Finland do instead? The realistic alternative is not a mine-free border defended by goodwill and condemnation. It is a much larger, more expensive standing army with greater conventional firepower — which European governments have been consistently unwilling to fund. If the choice is between land mines that can slow a Russian advance at relatively low ongoing cost, and the much larger fiscal commitment of maintaining a conventional deterrence that European publics have repeatedly refused to fund adequately, Finland's choice looks less like a humanitarian regression and more like a sensible response to the constraints it actually faces.

There is also a signal value to this announcement that transcends the immediate tactical question. Finland's willingness to absorb international criticism and pursue the deterrence posture it believes is necessary is the kind of strategic seriousness that the Western alliance has been lacking. Germany has spent three years announcing Zeitenwende — the turn toward genuine defence investment — while repeatedly failing to translate the announcement into procurement decisions and budget commitments. Finland's mine programme is not headline-grabbing, but it is concrete. In deterrence, concrete is what counts.

## What to watch

- Whether other NATO members with long Russian borders — Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania — follow Finland's lead and revise their positions on defensive mine use. A coalition of Nordic-Baltic states pursuing a common border fortification doctrine would change the political calculus significantly.
- The reaction from Ottawa Treaty signatories, particularly Canada and the UK: formal diplomatic protests would signal that the humanitarian norm still has institutional force; silence would signal that strategic pragmatism is reasserting itself.
- Russian response: Moscow has used previous NATO deterrence investments as propaganda fodder for domestic consumption, framing them as evidence of Western aggression. Finland's mine programme will almost certainly receive this treatment.
- Whether the European Commission's defence industrial proposals include provisions for mine production capacity. The EU's reluctance to engage with this category of weapon will be tested if Finland's position gains broader support within the alliance.
