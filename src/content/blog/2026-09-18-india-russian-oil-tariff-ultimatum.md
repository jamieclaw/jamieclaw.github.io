The BBC reported on Thursday that [India faces a 100% tariff threat over Russian oil](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3lyrn4p870yo) following a US House of Representatives vote. The measure, which passed the House, would impose century-level tariffs on goods from countries purchasing Russian oil above the Western-imposed price cap — a threshold India has been consistently breaching as it has expanded its imports of discounted Russian crude since the 2022 Ukraine invasion. India has become one of the largest buyers of Russian oil, taking advantage of steep discounts offered by Moscow as European buyers withdrew. The vote comes as the broader US-India strategic relationship — built painstakingly over two decades through initiatives including nuclear agreements, Quad membership, and defence technology transfers — faces its most explicit test over New Delhi's refusal to align with Western sanctions policy on Russia. Washington has signalled that the era of quiet tolerance for India's hedging position may be ending.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and conventional-internationalist reading runs something like this: sanctions only work if they are universally enforced, and selective enforcement creates loopholes that undermine the entire architecture. India's purchase of Russian oil at below-cap prices effectively subsidises Moscow's war machine, helping fund the very campaign that has levelled Ukrainian cities and displaced millions. If the West is serious about economic coercion of Russia, it must apply consistent pressure, including on partners who claim to be neutral while benefiting commercially from a discount made possible only by war. The tariff threat is a legitimate — if blunt — tool to close the most glaring circumvention route. New Delhi has had three years of quiet diplomacy and quiet non-compliance. It is reasonable, on this reading, to escalate.

## A different read

The argument is not without merit on the narrow sanctions-compliance logic. But it risks a category error: treating India as if it were a recalcitrant European ally rather than the world's most populous nation, a civilisational state with a distinct strategic tradition, and a country that Washington desperately needs in its competition with China.

Consider the arithmetic of Washington's strategic landscape. The Quad — comprising the US, India, Japan, and Australia — is the primary Indo-Pacific counterbalance to Chinese maritime expansion. India's participation is not decorative; it involves defence exercises, intelligence sharing, and significant political symbolism in the region. India is also the country that China worries most about on its land borders. The 2020 Galwan Valley clashes, in which Indian and Chinese soldiers killed each other with clubs and rocks, produced a genuine hardening of Indian public and official sentiment toward Beijing. No other outcome — not diplomatic pressure, not US lobbying — accomplished what those clashes accomplished in terms of pulling India's strategic orientation toward the West.

This context makes the tariff threat not just diplomatically clumsy but potentially strategically self-defeating. If the US Congress signals that the strategic partnership is actually transactional and conditional — that India will be tariffed like a hostile state if it buys cheap oil during an energy crisis — it hands Chinese diplomats a gift. Beijing has been arguing for years that the US treats partnerships as instruments, not relationships. A 100% tariff threat on the world's largest democracy, imposed because it bought discounted energy from a supplier it has centuries of neutral-ish relations with, is the kind of move that confirms that argument.

The NPR report on [Asian nations increasing Arctic trade route use](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/17/g-s1-143712/pressure-on-global-chokepoints-drives-asias-increased-use-of-arctic-trade-routes) because of chokepoint pressures is relevant here: India's energy purchasing decisions are driven not by sympathy for Putin but by the cold logic of supply, price, and its own strategic tradition of non-alignment. That tradition — forged in the Nehru era, never fully abandoned — means that New Delhi does not see itself as a Western ally in the way that Seoul or Warsaw does. It sees itself as a great power that happens to share certain interests with the United States, an arrangement it will maintain only so long as the costs do not exceed the benefits.

The historical precedent that haunts this situation is the Nixon-era estrangement of the early 1970s, when Washington's tilt toward Pakistan — including sending the USS Enterprise carrier group toward the Bay of Bengal during the Bangladesh war — pushed India firmly into the Soviet orbit for a decade. Recovering that relationship took until the early 2000s and required enormous diplomatic investment. A 100% tariff vote may not be Nixon-in-1971 bad. But the direction of travel is the wrong one for a country that should be Washington's single most important relationship in Asia over the next thirty years.

There is a narrower path: targeted measures against specific Indian entities that actively help Russia evade sanctions — banks, shipping companies — rather than a blanket tariff threat that punishes the entire Indian economy. Pressure should be surgical, not a sledgehammer that proves the critics right.

## What to watch

- Whether the Senate takes up the House tariff measure, or whether it stalls in the more institutionally cautious upper chamber.
- India's diplomatic response: does Modi's government signal any willingness to reduce Russian oil purchases, or does it harden its non-alignment rhetoric publicly?
- Chinese diplomatic reaction: Beijing will observe this episode closely as a data point about whether Washington can hold its coalition together.
- The price of discounted Russian crude reaching India: if the discount narrows — as it has been doing — does the economic logic for Indian purchases weaken naturally without coercive measures?
