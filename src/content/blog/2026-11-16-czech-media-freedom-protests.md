Hundreds of thousands of Czechs gathered in Prague's Wenceslas Square and surrounding streets on Saturday in what local organizers described as the [largest demonstration since the Velvet Revolution](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/15/czechia-protest-prague-media-freedom) of 1989. The trigger was a government plan — backed by Prime Minister Andrej Babiš's ANO party and its coalition partners — to fundamentally alter the funding model of Czech public television and radio, shifting from a household licence fee to direct state budget subsidy. Critics, including journalist unions, university academics, and opposition parties spanning both left and right, argue the change would give the governing coalition direct leverage over editorial decisions. The BBC reported that [Czechs rallied against the plan to switch funding of public media](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm8e0knr7604o), noting the size of the crowd and the resonance of comparisons with Viktor Orbán's systematic capture of Hungarian media after 2010. Al Jazeera [also covered the rallies](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/15/thousands-rally-in-czech-republic-against-state-media-funding-changes), emphasising the cross-partisan character of the opposition.

## The received wisdom

Progressive commentary in Brussels and among Western media organisations will frame this as a straightforward story of civic virtue triumphing over authoritarian encroachment: brave Czechs protecting Enlightenment institutions from populist vandals. That framing has the considerable merit of being substantially accurate. Babiš, the billionaire former prime minister who returned to power after a stint as defendant in a European Union subsidy fraud case, has long displayed Orbánite ambitions without quite achieving Orbánite results. Czech civil society is resilient, the Constitutional Court has teeth, and the country's history — 1968, 1989 — gives its citizenry an unusually high sensitivity to state information control. The protests represent exactly the kind of popular mobilisation that democratic theory says should happen when institutions are threatened.

## A different read

That said, the neat narrative of liberal democracy under siege from populism misses some important texture.

First, it is worth acknowledging what the protesters are defending and what they are not. Czech public television and radio are genuinely independent institutions by regional standards — but "independent" is not the same as "balanced." An honest account of the Czech media landscape would note that Czech Television (ČT) and Czech Radio have, over the past decade, developed their own ideological tendencies, particularly on EU policy, immigration, and climate, that reflect the priorities of the educated urban class that most closely identifies with public broadcasting. The licence-fee model does not produce ideological neutrality; it produces a particular kind of ideological tilt that has simply been normalised as the default. The right-of-centre case for the reforms — rarely made seriously but not entirely frivolous — is that public broadcasters accountable to no specific political actor are in practice accountable to their own professional culture, which is not automatically representative of the broader public.

Second, the Orbán comparison deserves scrutiny. Hungary's media capture happened through a combination of regulatory capture, ownership change, and legal intimidation applied over many years. The Czech proposal, as it currently stands, changes the funding mechanism but does not, on its face, give the government power to appoint editors or direct coverage. The gap between "this could lead to Orbánisation" and "this is Orbánisation" matters enormously for how the Constitutional Court should evaluate the law, and how European institutions should respond. Treating every reform to public media as equivalent to Orbán's assault risks degrading the category and making it harder to defend when genuine capture occurs.

Third, there is a broader question about the licence-fee model that is rarely asked in coverage like this. The BBC, funded by licence fee, is in existential financial difficulty. Germany's ARD is under severe political pressure from multiple directions simultaneously. Denmark's DR has undergone substantial restructuring. The licence fee is not an eternal and self-evidently correct funding mechanism; it is a post-war settlement that is visibly straining across European democracies. The question of how to fund public media in the digital age — when households increasingly lack television sets and younger viewers don't watch linear broadcast — is a genuine policy problem, and the fact that Babiš is raising it in bad faith does not mean it cannot be raised in good faith elsewhere.

The Prague protesters deserve support. But the strongest case for independent public media is one that also honestly confronts the weaknesses of existing institutions, rather than treating any challenge to them as an existential threat to democracy itself. Poland rebuilt its public broadcaster after PiS-era capture precisely by being clear-eyed about what had gone wrong and what a genuinely independent institution should look like. That is the model Central Europe needs — not reflexive defense of the status quo, but honest reckoning with the limits of institutions that were never quite as neutral as their defenders claim.

## What to watch

The Czech Constitutional Court is expected to receive a challenge to the legislation within weeks of passage. Watch whether the Chamber of Deputies amends the bill in response to protest pressure, or whether Babiš's coalition holds firm and forces the Constitutional Court to rule. Also track the European Commission's response: Brussels has been reluctant to invoke Article 7 proceedings against member states except in extreme cases, and the Czech situation will test whether the threshold has effectively dropped. If Czech Television's editorial coverage of the government visibly changes within a year of the funding shift, that will be the most damning evidence for protesters' concerns.
