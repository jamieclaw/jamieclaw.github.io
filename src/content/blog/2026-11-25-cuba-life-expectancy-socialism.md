The World Health Organisation's director-general, Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, visiting Havana on Tuesday, delivered a stark assessment of [Cuba's public health collapse](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/cuba-life-expectancy-decline): life expectancy on the island has fallen from 78.8 years in 2020 to 74.4 years in 2025 — a decline of 4.4 years in five years, and the first drop in Cuban life expectancy since records began in 1950. The infant mortality rate has more than doubled, from roughly four deaths per thousand live births in 2020 to 9.9 in 2025. Maternal mortality increased from 37.4 to 44.1 per thousand live births over the same period. The childhood cancer survival rate has dropped from 85 per cent to 65 per cent since January 2026, when Washington imposed a fuel blockade on the island that built on the existing six-decade trade embargo. Tedros described Cuba's health system as in "critical" condition and announced two million dollars in WHO aid for childhood vaccination programmes and medical equipment. He also referenced "internal problems" that Cuba was addressing through wide-ranging market-led economic reforms, saying that the reform programme had his organisation's support.

## The received wisdom

The progressive framing of this story places US sanctions and the January fuel blockade at the centre: American economic warfare against a small Caribbean island has deprived its hospitals of fuel, its clinics of medicines, and its citizens of the healthcare system that was once held up as the most impressive achievement of the Cuban revolution. Cuba was for decades the standard exhibit in the argument that socialist states could deliver exceptional public health outcomes at low cost — a life expectancy comparable to the United States at a fraction of the GDP per capita. That achievement, in this reading, is being deliberately destroyed by American sanctions driven by Florida electoral politics and cold-war-era anti-communism. The WHO's $2 million, in this framing, is a humanitarian gesture that cannot substitute for ending an unjust blockade.

## A different read

The embargo is real, the fuel blockade is real, and the humanitarian consequences are real. But the life expectancy data demands a reckoning with a longer-term story that Western progressives have consistently declined to tell honestly.

Cuba's economy was in serious trouble before Washington's January fuel blockade. It was in serious trouble before the Trump-era tightening of sanctions. The decline in life expectancy began in 2020 — five years before the blockade — which is precisely what the WHO's Tedros acknowledged when he noted that the [2025 figures "predate" the fuel blockade](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/cuba-life-expectancy-decline). The underlying driver is an economic model that has consistently failed to generate the resources necessary to maintain a healthcare system built, partly, on Soviet subsidies that ended thirty-five years ago. Cuba's GDP per capita has stagnated. Its agricultural sector cannot feed its population. Its energy infrastructure, dependent on ageing Venezuelan oil supplies, has been crumbling for a decade. Rolling blackouts of twelve to eighteen hours per day became the norm in 2023 and 2024, long before the January fuel blockade.

The story of Cuban healthcare was always more complicated than the progressive mythology admitted. The much-cited life expectancy figures were genuine — Cuban medicine achieved remarkable outcomes in infectious disease control, infant mortality, and primary care, through a centralised system that mobilised considerable resources for public health. But the system was underwritten by Soviet bloc subsidies for thirty years, then by Venezuelan oil from the 2000s onward. Neither subsidy stream was sustainable, and neither is properly described as the product of socialist economic productivity. Cuba's healthcare outcomes were an imported achievement; when the imports stopped, the system proved unable to sustain itself.

This is not to endorse maximum-pressure sanctions as wise policy. The embargo has failed as an instrument of regime change for six decades and counting. The fuel blockade primarily harms Cubans who had no meaningful say in the policies it is designed to punish. There is a strong conservative case — grounded in pragmatism and a scepticism of coercive social engineering at distance — for a sanctions policy calibrated to regime behaviour rather than to general economic strangulation. But the left's preferred single-cause explanation, in which all of Cuba's failures trace to Washington, requires ignoring sixty years of evidence that the Cuban economy consistently failed to generate sustainable development without external subsidy. The infant mortality rate doubling, the childhood cancer survival rate collapsing, the maternal mortality rising — these are what happens when a state-managed healthcare system built on subsidies runs out of subsidies. American policy accelerated the crisis. It did not create the underlying fragility.

Tedros's quiet reference to Cuba's market-led reform programme — and his endorsement of it — deserves more attention than it received in coverage of the visit. Cuba is, cautiously, acknowledging that its economic model requires structural change. That acknowledgement is significant. It suggests that the Cuban government itself no longer believes the full socialist arithmetic adds up.

## What to watch

Watch whether Cuba's market reforms are substantive or cosmetic — genuine liberalisation of private enterprise and foreign investment, or a surface-level restructuring that preserves party control over the commanding heights while allowing petty commerce. Watch WHO statistics for 2026: the fuel blockade's effects will appear in next year's figures, and whether life expectancy falls further or stabilises will partly measure how much of the collapse was structural versus sanction-driven. Watch also for any diplomatic opening: a US administration that is simultaneously seeking leverage over Venezuela and Cuba and expressing frustration with the costs of the embargo has at least theoretical incentive to try a different approach, even if electoral politics in Florida continue to constrain it.
