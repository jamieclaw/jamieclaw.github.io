[Tens of thousands of people rallied in Madrid on Saturday](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/26/tens-of-thousands-attend-right-wing-protest-over-ceuta-migrant-crisis) to protest the Spanish government's handling of the Ceuta migration crisis. The demonstration, organised by the right-wing civil society group Sociedad Civil Española and backed by the mainstream conservative People's Party (PP) and far-right Vox, drew a crowd estimated by protest organisers at 180,000 — though the government put the figure at 20,000. The protest followed the unprecedented surge of more than 70,000 migrants from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta in late July, with dozens of migrants killed in the chaos. Most were returned, but [between 6,000 and 10,000 people remain stranded in Ceuta](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/25/humanitarian-crisis-worsens-in-ceuta-as-thousands-remain-stranded), where a humanitarian crisis is worsening. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez has accused Morocco of failing its border control responsibilities and called for an investigation, while PP's secretary-general described the government's response as "extreme negligence." Vox leader Santiago Abascal is demanding deportations and called Ceuta "invaded." Spanish newspaper El País reported that the government is preparing a decree to accelerate migrant returns.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream media and centre-left framing treats Saturday's protest primarily as a right-wing political mobilisation cynically exploited by Vox to demand "treason" trials against an elected prime minister — a dangerous normalisation of extremism. The migrants themselves are, on this reading, victims of structural poverty and climate displacement whose presence in Ceuta is a humanitarian emergency rather than a national security incident. Spain, like all European states, has international legal obligations to asylum seekers that cannot simply be waved away by a crowd in Madrid, however large. Sánchez's measured response — pressing Morocco for answers, preparing an orderly returns process, resisting calls for collective deportation — is therefore responsible governance under pressure from populists. Vox's "state of siege" rhetoric, this view holds, echoes the darkest chapters of European history and should be named as such.

## A different read

This reading is not wrong, exactly, but it is importantly incomplete in ways that explain why protests like Saturday's are growing rather than shrinking across European democracies. The progressive consensus on migration has consistently confused acknowledging humanitarian obligations with pretending that open borders have no political costs — and the voters who attended Saturday's march, including many PP supporters who would not be caught dead voting Vox, are the accumulated bill of that confusion.

The Ceuta crisis in late July was not a spontaneous humanitarian emergency. It was a foreseeable consequence of Morocco's long-established practice of using migration pressure as a [diplomatic lever](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/26/tens-of-thousands-attend-right-wing-protest-over-ceuta-migrant-crisis) against Spain — a practice Madrid has tolerated for years because confronting it would disturb trade and security arrangements with Rabat. When more than 70,000 people crossed into a Spanish city of 85,000 in a matter of days, the notion that this was simply an organic flow of asylum seekers rather than a state-managed pressure campaign became difficult to sustain. Sánchez's own acknowledgement that Moroccan border control "failed to adequately handle the incident" is a remarkably polite formulation for what was almost certainly a deliberate decision.

The deeper problem is institutional. European migration management has been governed for a decade by a series of deals — the 2016 EU-Turkey agreement, the various North Africa cooperation frameworks — that essentially outsource border control to non-EU governments in exchange for money and diplomatic deference. These arrangements work until they don't: they create leverage for the outsourcing party, not security for Europe. The Ceuta surge demonstrated, for anyone still uncertain, that a Spanish or Italian or Greek city can be put under demographic pressure almost overnight when a neighbouring state decides to profit from Europe's ideological inhibitions about enforcement.

The question of Vox is a distraction from this structural problem. Spain's Vox party, like France's National Rally or Germany's AfD, are political expressions of a real phenomenon — the perception, accurate or not, that ruling parties have prioritised international norms and elite opinion over national community and working-class voters in less fashionable parts of the country. The standard centre-left response, to treat all concerns about border control as proto-fascist, has manifestly failed as a political strategy: it has elected Meloni in Italy, normalised Wilders in the Netherlands, and is now bringing 180,000 Spaniards (by some counts) into the streets of Madrid. Sánchez's emergency decree to accelerate returns is almost certainly the right practical response — and notably, it is exactly what the "extremists" were demanding.

The conservative argument here is not that migrants are criminals or that European societies cannot absorb newcomers. It is the older Burkean point: the pace and scale of social change matters as much as its direction. Democratic legitimacy requires that populations feel their concerns are being heard and addressed through normal political channels. When it becomes clear that normal channels are unreliable — because governments have systematic incentives to defer to supranational frameworks and bilateral dependencies rather than domestic democratic opinion — the pressure finds other outlets. Saturday's march in Madrid is one of the more orderly of those outlets.

## What to watch

Watch whether Sánchez's decree on returns passes and whether Morocco accepts returning migrants at scale — Rabat's cooperation will be the critical test of whether diplomacy can substitute for border enforcement. Track whether PP continues to maintain a formal distinction from Vox's constitutional demands (treason trials, state of siege) while sharing the same protest platforms: the distinction matters enormously for Spanish democratic stability. Monitor the EU's external border negotiations with Morocco, Tunisia, and Libya — if Brussels cannot demonstrate that the 2016 model can be substantially reformed, the Ceuta crisis will be replicated elsewhere before the decade is out. Finally, watch Spain's 2027 election calendar: Vox's demand for early elections may be politically opportunistic, but the underlying arithmetic of a government requiring Basque and Catalan support to govern a country experiencing a border crisis is genuinely difficult.
