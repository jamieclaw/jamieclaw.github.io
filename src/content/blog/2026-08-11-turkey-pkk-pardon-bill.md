The Turkish parliament voted on Monday to approve a conditional pardon for thousands of members of the Kurdistan Workers' Party, known by the initials PKK. [The bill passed 468 to 88 with six abstentions](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/10/turkiye-approves-pardon-like-bill-for-thousands-of-pkk-fighters), backed by President Recep Tayyip Erdogan's ruling AK Party and the pro-Kurdish DEM Party. The legislation suspends prosecutions and prison sentences for some offences for between five and ten years. In an initial phase, around 3,500 PKK-linked detainees are expected to be released — roughly a third of those held in Turkish prisons on related charges. The bill does not constitute a full amnesty; it envisages a conditional return to civilian life for fighters who lay down their weapons. The legislation represents Türkiye's first formal legislative step toward ending a conflict that began in 1984, has killed more than 40,000 people, and has imposed enormous economic and social costs on the country's predominantly Kurdish southeast. The move follows PKK founder Abdullah Ocalan's call, issued from prison 18 months ago, for his followers to disarm.

## The received wisdom

The dominant reading — shared across the European liberal press, human rights organisations, and Kurdish diaspora groups with varying emphases — is that this is a long-overdue step toward ending one of the world's most protracted internal conflicts. The PKK insurgency has been catastrophic not only in lives lost but in what it has done to Kurdish communities in southeastern Turkey, where decades of counter-insurgency operations destroyed villages, displaced populations, and suppressed political expression. Any move toward political reconciliation deserves qualified welcome.

The progressive framing adds a regional dimension: a Turkey that has resolved its Kurdish question internally is a more stable partner in a region under extraordinary strain. Kurdish groups in Syria and Iraq have been central actors in the post-ISIS landscape. A normalised relationship between Ankara and Kurdish political movements could, in theory, reduce proxy conflicts across borders. The DEM Party's support for the bill is significant precisely because it represents Kurdish political participation within Turkish constitutional structures — the kind of integration that peaceful conflict resolution theorists view as the only durable solution.

## A different read

The bill's passage is genuinely important. But the architecture of the deal, and the political environment in which it is being constructed, contains several features that deserve scrutiny before the celebration becomes too general.

Start with the conditionality. The bill suspends rather than eliminates prosecution; the suspension runs for five to ten years. This is not a truth-and-reconciliation framework with institutional foundations. It is, structurally, a political arrangement — one whose sustainability depends on the continued commitment of political actors who have historically been antagonists. The AK Party and the DEM Party have managed an extraordinary cooperation to pass this legislation, but the interests that motivated each side are not necessarily aligned on what comes next.

Erdogan's motivation is legible: a resolution of the Kurdish conflict would represent a historic legacy achievement, improve Turkey's economic development in the southeast, and reduce security costs that have burdened the military for decades. It would also — and this is the political arithmetic that matters — allow the AK Party to consolidate an electoral coalition that includes portions of the Kurdish vote, potentially insulating Erdogan against future electoral challenges from a united opposition.

But the AK Party's nationalist base, represented by the MHP, which has been in coalition with the AK Party, is deeply uncomfortable with the deal. The MHP's opposition could fracture the government's parliamentary arithmetic in future votes. The 88 votes against were not negligible; they represent a real constituency for whom any accommodation with the PKK is capitulation rather than reconciliation. Managing this tension — giving the AK Party's nationalist wing enough of something to sustain their participation while delivering on the peace process's obligations to the Kurdish side — is the central challenge of implementation.

The PKK's side of the equation is also genuinely uncertain. [Ocalan's disarmament call came 18 months ago](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/10/turkiye-approves-pardon-like-bill-for-thousands-of-pkk-fighters). PKK fighters in the Qandil mountains of northern Iraq remain an autonomous force with their own command structures and relationships with regional actors, including, until recently, the United States. The question of whether those fighters — who have lived their adult lives in armed resistance — will accept conditional civilian integration is one that cannot be answered by a parliamentary vote in Ankara. Some will. Some will not. The spoiler problem in peace processes is well documented: it takes only a small faction committed to violence to derail an agreement whose benefits are diffuse and whose costs are concentrated.

There is also the Turkish state's own accountability. The conflict involved documented cases of forced disappearances, extrajudicial killings, and village destructions on the Turkish military's side. The conditional pardon addresses the PKK's criminal liability. Whether Turkish security forces face equivalent accountability is a question the bill does not address, and which Erdogan's government has no political interest in addressing. Durable peace processes — from the Good Friday Agreement to the Colombian peace process — have required mutual accountability to stick. The absence of symmetry is not automatically fatal, but it is a fragility.

Against all this, the scale of the opportunity should not be understated. Forty years of conflict, 40,000 dead, a generation of Kurdish children raised under militarised conditions — ending this would be among the most significant peacetime achievements of any Turkish government. The historical parallel that comes to mind is the 1998 Good Friday Agreement in Northern Ireland, which was similarly reached between parties who had recently been killing each other, and which survived — imperfectly, with repeated crises — because the cost of reverting to violence eventually became unacceptable to everyone involved.

## What to watch

- The pace of detainee releases and whether the first phase — roughly 3,500 people — proceeds without significant judicial obstruction or MHP-linked political resistance.
- PKK commanders' responses from Qandil: public statements from senior figures still in the mountains will signal whether the military wing accepts civilian leadership's direction.
- Whether Erdogan's government attempts to convert the peace process into electoral capital in a way that causes the DEM Party to publicly distance itself — preserving the DEM's credibility with Kurdish voters is essential to the deal's legitimacy.
- Violence incidents: even one significant attack attributed to a PKK splinter could provide the political cover needed for hardliners to demand the legislation be suspended.
