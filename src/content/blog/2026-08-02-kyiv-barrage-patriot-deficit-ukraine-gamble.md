Before dawn on Saturday, 1 August, [Russian missiles tore into five districts of Kyiv](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/01/nx-s1-5916393/overnight-russia-kyiv-ukraine-strikes), killing nine people and wounding more than thirty. The attack comprised 35 ballistic missiles and 185 attack drones. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy reported that only one of 27 ballistic missiles was intercepted — a consequence, he said, of a dwindling supply of Patriot air-defence interceptors. The bombardment set fires across residential apartment blocks; rescue crews worked through the night. Lithuania's embassy in Kyiv was also damaged. The same day, Zelenskyy announced that Ukrainian drones had struck three Russian oil refineries in Bashkortostan, roughly a thousand miles from the front, and confirmed the sinking of a sanctioned Russian container ship in the Black Sea.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive-internationalist framing presents this as a straightforward case for more Western arms transfers and more diplomatic pressure. The argument runs: Russia is a revisionist aggressor violating the UN Charter; Ukraine is a victim exercising the right of self-defence under international law; and the moral and strategic duty of democratic states is to provide Ukraine with every means to resist. The recent wavering by the Trump administration — walking back, at a Camp David cabinet meeting, an earlier signal that Ukraine would be licensed to manufacture its own Patriot systems — is in this view pure folly: emboldening Moscow, betraying an ally, and undermining the deterrence architecture that has kept Europe's borders largely stable since 1945. The plea from Zelenskyy — "every package of ballistic missile interceptors saves the lives of our people" — captures the humanitarian urgency of that framing, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

## A different read

The humanitarian framing is real, but it sits uncomfortably alongside a strategic picture that the Trump administration has, despite its characteristic clumsiness, at least partially grasped. The core problem is not American ill will toward Ukraine but a structural imbalance that Western governments have been reluctant to name plainly: [the United States has supplied Ukraine with enough support to survive but not enough to win](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/24/g-s1-135405/rubio-ukraine-russia-war), a posture that maximises the duration of the war and the accumulation of Ukrainian civilian casualties without producing a decisive outcome.

The Patriot interceptor shortfall is a product of that posture. Patriot batteries are expensive, their interceptor missiles are manufactured slowly, and the United States has finite stocks. Each interceptor costs in the range of $3 million to $6 million; Russia, for its part, manufactures ballistic missiles and drones at industrial scale, partly thanks to [sanctions-busting arms flows from partners](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cgk4rdley0ko) including, as the BBC reported this week, Russian ships defying sanctions to deliver military vehicles to Mali — a sign that the broader sanctions regime is porous. This is the arithmetic of attrition, and Ukraine is on the wrong side of it.

The historical parallel worth reaching for is not Munich 1938 — the analogy Western hawks habitually invoke — but rather the Korean War of 1950–53, where the United States committed enough force to prevent a communist victory but not enough to reunify the peninsula, ultimately freezing the conflict along the 38th parallel at a cost of roughly 36,000 American and hundreds of thousands of Korean lives. The armistice was widely condemned at the time as a betrayal. With hindsight, it was an unglamorous but workable stabilisation. Whether such an outcome is achievable or desirable in Ukraine is genuinely uncertain; but the question itself is one that responsible policymakers must at least ask, rather than treating indefinite escalatory support as the only morally permissible position.

The Trump administration's communication failures — Trump saying at Camp David that the US "has not agreed" to Patriot manufacturing licences, days after suggesting it had — are real and damaging, not because the underlying scepticism is necessarily wrong but because uncertainty about American commitments is precisely what invites Russian opportunism. Zelenskyy spoke by phone with Vice President JD Vance on Friday, reportedly reiterating the topics from Tuesday's White House meeting. That meeting produced language about "progress"; this week's strike killed nine Kyiv residents. Vagueness is not a strategy.

What a coherent conservative foreign policy would look like in this context is roughly as follows: clear public commitments on the specific capabilities the US will and will not supply, accompanied by a serious diplomatic track — not a unilateral capitulation — aimed at a durable ceasefire. The current approach combines the costs of escalation with the costs of appeasement while reaping the benefits of neither. Moscow in the meantime can read the chaos in Washington as clearly as anyone.

There is also a separate thread here that receives less attention: the Moscow cafe bombing on the same day, [in which a woman detonated a homemade device outside a restaurant near Kudrinskaya Square](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c86n4ljxp63o), killing three people including a security guard. Russian state media attributed the attack to a woman who was stopped at the door. Russia has blamed Ukrainian-linked networks for similar attacks in the past; no group has yet claimed this. What it illustrates is that the war is increasingly generating blowback inside Russian territory — a development with unpredictable escalatory potential. Whether Moscow uses this domestically to justify further intensification of its Ukrainian air campaign is a signal worth watching.

## What to watch

Four signals in the coming days. First, whether the Trump administration clarifies its position on Patriot manufacturing licences before next week's congressional recess — ambiguity now has measurable human costs. Second, whether Zelenskyy's continued appeals to Vance produce any concrete supply commitments, or whether the White House continues to manage this issue with rhetoric rather than hardware. Third, whether the Moscow cafe bombing generates a credible attribution — if Kyiv-linked networks are identified, expect Russia to escalate further and for that to be used as domestic justification. Fourth, watch the Black Sea: Ukraine's sinking of the sanctioned Russian container vessel represents a new front, and Russia's response — if it comes — will tell us something about how far Moscow believes it can escalate without American red lines.
