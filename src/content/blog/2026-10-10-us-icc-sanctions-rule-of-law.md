The United States unveiled sweeping sanctions against the International Criminal Court on Friday, targeting the institution's judges, prosecutors, and administrative staff in an action the Court itself described as an "assault on the rule of law." [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj20vkkx3rdvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the White House framed the sanctions as a defence of American sovereignty against a body that lacks US consent to exercise jurisdiction over American citizens. [Al Jazeera confirmed](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/9/european-states-canada-japan-and-un-back-icc-against-us-sanctions) that European states, Canada, Japan, and the United Nations immediately backed the ICC against Washington, issuing a joint statement condemning the sanctions as an unprecedented assault on an institution that has been central to post-conflict accountability since its founding in 2002. The ICC's current docket includes investigation of alleged Russian war crimes in Ukraine — an investigation that Washington had previously encouraged.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-internationalist response is both predictable and, in this instance, largely correct in its moral orientation. The International Criminal Court exists precisely because the alternative — leaving war crimes to be judged solely by the perpetrators' own courts — is no alternative at all. Its indictments of Russian commanders and its investigation into alleged atrocities in Gaza represent the slow, imperfect machinery of accountability grinding forward against the wishes of the powerful. To sanction the judges and staff of the ICC is to place the United States in the same company as Russia and China, who oppose the Court's jurisdiction for obvious reasons. The European coalition's response underscores how isolated this action makes Washington. Scholars of international law will note that the US position is not merely hypocritical but strategically self-defeating: if the ICC cannot function, the American interest in prosecuting Russian war crimes — which Washington has vocally championed — is itself undermined. The progressive argument here is that universal institutions require universal participation to function, and that America's selective engagement with international law destroys its credibility as a rules-based-order advocate.

## A different read

And yet the question of ICC jurisdiction is genuinely thornier than its defenders allow. The United States is not a signatory to the Rome Statute — it signed but never ratified under Clinton, and the Bush administration formally "unsigned" in 2002. The principle that a court can exercise jurisdiction over citizens of a state that has explicitly refused to submit to it is not self-evidently compatible with the Westphalian system that underpins most of international law. Every conservative critique of the ICC is not mere cynicism: there is a real tension between universal-jurisdiction theories and the democratic accountability of domestic legal systems to their own electorates.

But the Trump administration's move is different in kind from principled legal objection to ICC jurisdiction. It is punitive action against individual judges and administrators — people carrying out the mandates of a treaty institution — using the full weight of American financial power. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj20vkkx3rdvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the Court condemned the move as an "attack against sovereign states" that have consented to its jurisdiction. This is an important point: sanctioning the ICC does not merely protect Americans from prosecution; it directly undermines the legal process for every state that has ratified the Rome Statute and sought the Court's assistance.

The historical parallel is instructive. When the League of Nations was gutted by American non-participation in the 1920s and 1930s, the proximate cause was a principled senatorial rejection of entangling commitments. But the practical consequence was the removal of the only multilateral brake on revisionist states — Germany, Italy, Japan — that was available at the time. The lesson subsequent American statesmen drew was that international institutions, however imperfect, serve American interests by creating normative environments where American power does not have to be exercised bilaterally in every crisis. The ICC is part of that environment. Sanctioning it does not strengthen American sovereignty; it weakens the normative fabric that American power has historically helped maintain.

There is also a self-defeating quality specific to this moment. The ICC's most prominent current cases involve Russia. European allies have cited the Court's work as a core element of the accountability architecture for Ukraine. By sanctioning the ICC now, Washington is handing Moscow a diplomatic gift of incalculable value: the opportunity to argue that American and Russian approaches to international law are morally equivalent. Putin's propagandists are already making that case. The administration has, in one executive action, done more to rehabilitate Russia's international standing than any number of Russian influence operations could have achieved.

## What to watch

Watch whether Congress exercises any restraint on the sanctions — some Republican senators with internationalist instincts have already expressed reservations, though they have been a shrinking constituency. Watch how the European coalition responds in practice: will they impose countermeasures, or merely issue statements? Watch whether the ICC prosecutors in The Hague proceed regardless, and whether European states fill the enforcement gap left by American defection. Most importantly, watch the Russian reaction: if Moscow interprets American ICC sanctions as tacit permission to escalate its own disregard for international humanitarian law, the consequences will be measured in Ukrainian civilian casualties, not diplomatic communiqués.
