At least 72 civilians who were prospecting for gold in Sudan's River Nile state were killed in air strikes on Saturday morning, according to Emergency Lawyers, a rights group based in the Sudanese capital Khartoum. The organization said jets targeted artisanal mining camps at approximately 06:00 local time, firing more than five missiles directly at miners' camps and into excavation pits where workers were present. The [Sudan Tribune](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqy43vrk8wv8o), citing survivor testimony, reported the actual death toll may significantly exceed 72, with many more injured. Neither the Egyptian government nor Sudan's de facto authorities had commented as of Sunday. The site is in a border region where Egypt has previously been accused of striking artisanal mining operations: the Sudan Tribune reported claims from June that Egyptian forces had used drones and artillery to target mining sites in the same area, killing several. Egypt's border agency said in June it had seized 86 tonnes of gold ore and launched a sweeping security campaign along the border to combat illegal migration, drug smuggling, and what it describes as illegal mining. Emergency Lawyers stated that the attacks "may constitute war crimes" and represent "a violation of Sudan's sovereign territory."

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of this story — to the extent it receives mainstream framing at all — treats the Sudan-Egypt border strikes as an extension of a regional law enforcement problem. Artisanal or "wildcat" mining, the argument goes, has exploded in Sudan since the outbreak of civil war in April 2023, and the informal gold trade feeds into cross-border smuggling networks that also carry drugs and migrants. Egypt, facing its own economic pressures and a restive southern border, is attempting to protect what it sees as legitimate commercial interests in the gold market and to assert sovereign control over porous terrain. The Sudan Tribune has noted that Egypt's border force seized 86 tonnes of gold ore in June — a figure that suggests the scale of illicit extraction is genuinely substantial. On this reading, the strikes, while lethal and legally contested, are a law enforcement response to a security and economic problem created by Sudan's state collapse.

## A different read

The law enforcement framing has a certain surface plausibility but collapses under scrutiny, and a conservative analysis of sovereignty and international order demands a harder look at what is actually happening along this border.

First, the facts as known. These miners were unarmed civilians engaged in informal economic activity. Artisanal mining — panning or digging with rudimentary tools — is technically illegal in Sudan under rules that have not been enforced since the state effectively ceased to function in most of the country. The men at these sites were not fighters, smugglers, or insurgents. They were people trying to earn a living in a country whose formal economy has been destroyed by two years of civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces. Emergency Lawyers' description of "deliberate targeting of unarmed civilians" is a factual characterization of the strike pattern, not advocacy hyperbole.

Second, the history. The June incidents — if the reports are accurate — established that this kind of strike was not a one-off response to an acute security emergency but a pattern. Egypt does not announce these operations. Neither Sudan's armed forces nor the RSF control the region well enough to credibly deny or authorize them. The gold-rich River Nile state sits in a zone of effective state vacancy — which is precisely what makes it dangerous and precisely what makes accountability so elusive.

The broader lesson here is one that the international community has been slow to absorb: civil wars that produce state collapse do not stay contained. Sudan's internal disintegration has not remained a Sudanese problem. It has generated a refugee crisis, a regional food security emergency, a surge in illicit mining with geopolitical consequences, and now — apparently — an extended pattern of strikes by a neighboring state's forces on civilians inside Sudanese territory. This is what sovereign breakdown looks like from the outside.

Egypt is a significant US security partner. It receives approximately $1.3 billion annually in American military assistance. If Egyptian military assets are conducting strikes that kill dozens of unarmed civilians in a neighboring country, that is a material question for American foreign policy — not a peripheral humanitarian note. The Trump administration's current focus is overwhelmingly on Iran, Russia, and the midterm elections; Sudan barely registers. But the precedent being set here — that a regional power may strike civilian economic activity across a border with impunity, provided the target state is dysfunctional — is a precedent with applications far beyond the Sahara.

A realist conservative would not pretend that great powers have no interests in border stability or illicit resource flows. But realism also requires that you name what is happening accurately. What appears to be happening, on the available evidence, is the targeted killing of civilian miners — a violation of the basic laws of armed conflict regardless of the economic provocation.

## What to watch

Watch whether the UN Security Council or any regional body formally acknowledges these strikes: Sudan is a permanent agenda item but the RSF-SAF conflict crowds out border-incident analysis. Watch Egypt's response if and when it comes — Cairo's silence is itself informative, and any statement it does issue will be carefully examined for whether it acknowledges the strikes or implies they did not occur. Watch whether the provisional death toll rises further, as Sudan Tribune's survivor testimony suggests it may. And watch the gold price: global gold markets track large-scale supply disruption, and a sustained campaign against artisanal mining in gold-rich Sudan border regions could, counterintuitively, appear as upward price pressure while masking the human cost of that supply reduction.
