NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte issued an unusually pointed public intervention on Thursday, telling Britain it must demonstrate ["a credible path"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/17/nato-head-britain-must-show-credible-path-of-annual-defence-budget-increases) of steady annual defence spending increases to reach the alliance's target of 3.5% of GDP by 2035 — equivalent to roughly £30 billion more per year in military spending than the UK currently allocates. The BBC separately reported that [Rutte said the UK needs a "credible path" on defence spending](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm0e35pwje2do), making clear that London could not back-load the increase to arrive just before the 2035 deadline. The intervention came a day after Rutte met Prime Minister Andy Burnham. It follows weeks of public pressure from within NATO on the new UK government — which took office in late July — to clarify its defence commitments at a moment when Poland is publicly warning that [Russia's plans may include strikes against Ukraine's allies](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/17/russian-plans-include-strikes-against-ukraines-allies-poland-says), and Greenpeace is accusing Russia of [sabotaging power supply lines at the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/17/russia-sabotaging-power-supply-lines-at-zaporizhzhia-plant-greenpeace). The strategic environment has not paused to allow Burnham's government to find its footing.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for restraint on defence spending is honest about its priorities rather than dishonest about the threat. It runs roughly as follows: Britain's public services are in genuine distress after years of austerity. The NHS, social care, housing, education — these are the things that make life liveable for ordinary people, and a government that was elected on a mandate of domestic renewal cannot immediately redirect tens of billions of pounds into military hardware. Moreover, NATO's 2% target was always as much political theatre as strategic necessity — the alliance's combined military capacity vastly exceeds Russia's conventional forces, and throwing money at defence procurement is notoriously inefficient. Rutte, the argument goes, represents the institutional defence bureaucracy's perennial appetite for more resources. Burnham was elected to change domestic priorities. He should proceed carefully before yielding to alliance pressure.

## A different read

There is genuine substance to the fiscal pressure argument. The BBC reported that [UK borrowing costs have hit their highest level since 2007](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw804154z90ko), and Burnham's own chancellor is already navigating a difficult pre-Budget environment with unions demanding spending and markets watching the fiscal position nervously. The defence-or-services trade-off is real, not manufactured.

But Rutte's intervention is significant precisely because it is not the usual communiqué language. He said explicitly that Britain cannot wait until just before 2035 to reach 3.5% — that annual, steady increases are required. This is the NATO secretary general telling a major ally, publicly, that its current trajectory is inadequate. That is not a bureaucratic talking point. It is a political warning.

The deeper problem for Burnham is one of strategic timing. He inherited a security environment that did not emerge because he was elected; it has been deteriorating since 2022. The Al Jazeera report that Poland is now [warning Russia may be planning strikes on Ukraine's allies](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/17/russian-plans-include-strikes-against-ukraines-allies-poland-says) — a country that has spent more on defence as a percentage of GDP than almost any NATO member — represents the eastern flank's increasingly urgent assessment of Russian intent. When Warsaw, which has skin in the game, says the threat is existential, it is not making a procurement argument.

The historical resonance here is unavoidable. British governments have been having essentially the same argument — domestic needs vs. alliance commitments — since the 1960s. Harold Wilson's decision to withdraw from "east of Suez" in 1968 was domestically defensible and strategically consequential: it accelerated the vacuum that others, including the US, struggled to fill. The cuts of the early 2010s under the Coalition and then Cameron's Conservatives left the Royal Navy with capability gaps that took a decade to partially address. Every time Britain has prioritised short-term fiscal relief over long-term defence investment, the cost has been paid later, usually by governments of a different party.

Burnham's "Manchesterism" — the devolved, community-oriented, pragmatic social democracy that built his political brand — is a real governing philosophy with genuine achievements in Greater Manchester. But Manchester-style politics does not have an obvious answer to the question of what happens if Russia decides to test Article 5 on Estonia while Britain is arguing about council tax. The BBC noted in the same week that [Russia is sabotaging power lines at Zaporizhzhia](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/17/russia-sabotaging-power-supply-lines-at-zaporizhzhia-plant-greenpeace) — not a diplomatic incident, but an act of potential nuclear brinkmanship. The gap between Burnham's comfortable domestic political vocabulary and the vocabulary required for this moment is real and widening.

The most useful thing Burnham could do is not match Germany's defence spending tomorrow — that is not possible — but to announce a credible, costed, multi-year trajectory that satisfies Rutte's "steady increases" test. That would cost political capital domestically. Not doing it will cost strategic credibility internationally, and strategic credibility is very expensive to rebuild once lost.

## What to watch

- The upcoming UK Budget: whether Healey produces a defence trajectory that Rutte can publicly endorse, or whether the numbers fall short again.
- Whether NATO formally triggers any Article 3 review mechanism regarding British capability — an escalation from public pressure to institutional process.
- Poland's continued public statements on Russian threat assessment: Warsaw's alarm is the most reliable indicator of how seriously the eastern flank reads the current situation.
- The BBC-reported discussions about the [UK joining a global defence bank led by Canada](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6e30e98v7j1o): whether this represents creative capability-building or a way to defer direct spending decisions.
