The European Union's new Entry/Exit System (EES), which requires all non-EU nationals entering the Schengen area to register fingerprints and a facial photograph upon arrival, has tripled border-crossing times at major European airports, according to airport operators. [Rome's Fiumicino airport](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckg5gg6n9x3o), one of Europe's busiest, reports that the time for UK nationals to clear the border has risen from seven minutes to twenty minutes even after significant improvements — including integration with e-gates — and that the €12 million spent on standalone kiosk machines has proven largely impractical for high-volume passenger flows. Border police at Portugal's Faro airport told the BBC the system suffers from bugs, including server crashes that affect all member states simultaneously, caused by interdependencies in the EU's IT infrastructure. Ryanair has told passengers travelling to Europe this summer to expect "extended waits at passport control." Passengers at multiple airports have reported queues of one to two hours; some have missed flights. The EES has been phased in since October and covers the 29 Schengen-area countries. The European Commission says disruption is "limited in most EU airports" and that it will continue supporting member states in implementation.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream defence of the EES is that it is a necessary and proportionate response to security challenges in a Europe that has faced significant migration pressures and terrorism threats. The system builds a record of all short-stay visitors and is designed to detect overstayers, improve border security data, and make the Schengen area more legible to its own authorities. The Brexit context — British travellers now fall into the "third-country national" category and must be processed as such — is an unfortunate consequence of the UK's own democratic choice, not the EU's design failure. The progressive framing adds that border security investment is always politically popular with European publics and that temporary implementation difficulties are the normal cost of deploying complex new technology at continental scale. The European Commission's assurance that disruption is "limited in most airports" is technically defensible; most airports, particularly smaller ones, are processing volumes that the new system can handle.

## A different read

The EES rollout is a case study in exactly the kind of failure that sceptics of centralised technocratic governance have been predicting for years, and the failure mode is instructive precisely because it is not the result of malice or corruption but of the institutional logic that drives large bureaucratic projects.

Consider the architecture: the system was designed centrally by the European Commission, implemented across 29 member states with wildly varying technological capacity, airport infrastructure, and passenger volumes, then deployed with a single technical dependency — EU-level servers — that creates simultaneous failure across all member states when anything goes wrong. [Faro's superintendent of border police described it explicitly](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckg5gg6n9x3o): "sometimes something that happens in Warsaw affects our system here." This is not a bug but a feature of how the system was architected. Centralised IT infrastructure produces centralised failure modes. The choice to depend on EU-level servers rather than federated national systems was a governance choice that optimised for data integration at the cost of resilience — and airports are discovering that cost in real time.

The €12 million spent on self-service kiosks at Fiumicino that are now being bypassed in favour of e-gate integration illustrates the problem of large-scale procurement in bureaucratic contexts. The kiosks were specified years before deployment, procured at scale based on the assumption that standalone machines would be the processing backbone, and are now sitting largely unused because the actual passenger-flow reality defeated the design assumptions. This is not unusual in government IT — the UK's NHS electronic patient records programme, the EU's own EUROSUR border surveillance system, and countless others have followed similar trajectories — but the fact that it is normal does not make it acceptable.

The Commission's response — assurances that disruption is "limited in most airports," insistence that implementation support will continue, rejection of requests to allow proactive suspension before busy periods — follows the classic pattern of bureaucratic defensiveness. An earlier meeting between airports, airlines, and the Commission "did not lead to any change" in the suspension policy, according to the BBC's reporting. This is the managerial preference for preserving the system's integrity over the interests of the actual users: British families queuing for two hours at Fiumicino, parents carrying children who must be processed by a border officer because the e-gates cannot handle under-12s.

The Brexit angle is worth noting, though not in the way the Remain-inclined press usually frames it. The argument is that British travellers are now subject to third-country processing as a consequence of Brexit, and that therefore the queue is Britain's problem. This is technically correct and politically tendentious. The EES applies to all non-Schengen nationals — Americans, Australians, Canadians, Japanese tourists — not only British ones. The system's failures are architectural, not specifically directed at post-Brexit travellers. The EU cannot hide a fundamentally broken system behind the convenient explanation that its most vocal victims chose to leave.

## What to watch

- Whether the European Commission allows member states to suspend EES proactively during peak travel periods — specifically August — or insists on case-by-case exceptions.
- Whether Ryanair and other airlines escalate from passenger advisories to formal complaints to the Commission or to national courts, which would force the Commission to quantify the economic harm.
- Whether the server dependency architecture is revised to federated national systems, which would require a fundamental redesign but would eliminate the simultaneous all-member-state failure mode.
- Whether any member state invokes the "exceptional circumstances" suspension clause unilaterally — Portugal and Sweden are the only two countries using the EU's pre-registration app, suggesting member-state variation in engagement with the system.
