The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has [surpassed 1,300 deaths](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/ebola-deaths-in-drc-surge-past-1300-as-virus-spreading-like-a-wildfire), with health officials describing the virus as "spreading like a wildfire." The scale of the crisis is significant: 1,300 fatalities in a single outbreak is among the largest in recorded Ebola history outside the catastrophic 2014–16 West African epidemic, which killed over 11,000. The outbreak has now reached beyond DRC's borders in one confirmed respect: a health worker who had been working in the DRC is [being monitored for Ebola at a London hospital](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/21/health-worker-being-monitored-for-ebola-in-london-hospital-after-working-in-drc), though UK health authorities have stressed the risk to the general public remains low. In the DRC itself, the combination of active armed conflict, collapsed healthcare infrastructure, and community distrust of official responders has made containment extraordinarily difficult.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and public health establishment's framing is essentially correct as far as it goes: this is a public health emergency requiring urgent international resource mobilisation, better community engagement with affected populations, and a review of how global health bodies — the WHO chief among them — coordinate their response. The argument for increased Western funding is made on both humanitarian and self-interested grounds: infectious disease respects no borders, and early containment in the DRC is exponentially cheaper than attempting suppression after international spread. The Guardian's coverage has drawn attention to [healthcare systems in sub-Saharan Africa](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/25/g-s1-135365/cancer-breast-chemotherapy-nigeria) that are already overwhelmed by other disease burdens. Advocates rightly point out that the DRC's conflict-affected eastern provinces, where the outbreak is concentrated, are precisely the areas least able to mount an effective public health response without external support.

## A different read

All of that is true, and none of it fully explains the disconnect between the scale of the outbreak and its near-absence from Western political discourse. While Europe's wildfires and the Trump-EU trade confrontation dominated headlines this week, 1,300 people dying of a haemorrhagic fever in one of the world's most resource-rich and most mismanaged countries barely registered.

Part of this is distance and familiarity. Ebola has struck the DRC repeatedly — this is at least the fourteenth recorded outbreak in the country — and repeated crises without resolution produce a kind of compassion fatigue in donor countries. The 2018–2020 Kivu outbreak, which ultimately killed 2,299 people and was only the second-largest in history, ran for nearly two years against a backdrop of armed militia interference and community resistance. It ended not through a decisive international intervention but through a combination of vaccination campaigns, sheer endurance, and eventual exhaustion of the transmission chains. That outcome — messy, long, expensive, inconclusive — does not generate the kind of political narrative that sustains Western attention.

But there is a harder question underneath the compassion-fatigue explanation. The DRC is a state that has received [extraordinary levels of international engagement](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/25/ebola-deaths-in-drc-surge-past-1300-as-virus-spreading-like-a-wildfire) — UN peacekeeping missions, bilateral aid programmes, development bank financing — for decades, and the underlying conditions that make Ebola containment so difficult (weak state institutions, persistent armed conflict, infrastructure collapse) have not materially improved. This is not a failure of international generosity; it is a failure of the theory of change that underpins much Western development assistance: that sufficiently resourced external interventions can substitute for functional domestic governance.

The London hospital case is the kind of event that briefly concentrates Western minds. It will probably not lead to sustained spread in the UK — the health worker was monitored promptly, and the UK's contact-tracing and isolation capacity is robust. But it should serve as a reminder that the epidemiological wall between the DRC and Europe is thin when international flights operate and health workers rotate through conflict zones. The lesson of COVID-19 — that early, energetic containment at source is the only cost-effective strategy — appears not to have been durably institutionalised in Western governments' political priorities.

The DRC's tragedy is that it sits at the intersection of every factor that makes disease containment hardest: active conflict, community distrust of the state, an overwhelmed health system, and a political economy that has historically absorbed international aid without building the institutions capable of deploying it effectively. The right-of-centre reading of this crisis is not to argue against emergency humanitarian assistance — that is clearly warranted. It is to be honest that money alone will not solve the underlying governance problem, and that any sustainable improvement requires the DRC to develop domestic state capacity rather than perpetual external dependency. That is an unfashionable thing to say, but it is what the evidence of thirty years suggests.

## What to watch

- Whether the WHO formally declares the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) — that designation triggers accelerated international response protocols and resource mobilisation.
- The London hospital case outcome: if the health worker tests positive, watch whether European governments convene emergency coordination meetings, which would signal a different political prioritisation than we have seen so far.
- Vaccination coverage: the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine has proven effective in previous DRC outbreaks but requires cold-chain infrastructure and community trust — both in short supply in the conflict-affected east.
- Whether the armed groups operating in eastern DRC interfere with health worker access, as occurred during the 2018–2020 outbreak — that variable more than any other determines whether containment is possible.
