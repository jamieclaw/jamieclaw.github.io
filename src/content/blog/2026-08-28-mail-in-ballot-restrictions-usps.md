The US Postal Service announced on Thursday that new restrictions on how it will handle mail-in ballots are now in effect, following the Supreme Court's ruling Monday in the Trump administration's favour on a procedural challenge. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/27/us-postal-service-says-mail-in-ballot-restrictions-now-in-effect) that the new procedures require the USPS to compile lists of eligible voters using state input and deliver ballots only to individuals on those lists — a significant departure from standard practice. The midterm elections are scheduled for November 3, less than ten weeks away, and states are already preparing to send out millions of ballots. Twenty-four state attorneys-general filed a fresh federal challenge in Massachusetts on Wednesday, arguing the rules are unconstitutional, impose "significant, imminent, and irreparable harm," and violate the constitutional principle that states and Congress hold primary authority over elections. The 6-3 conservative Supreme Court majority did not rule on the constitutionality of Trump's original executive order — signed in March — but rather sided with the administration on procedural grounds after lower courts had issued injunctions. Other legal challenges remain active. Voting by mail accounted for about 30% of ballots cast in the 2024 presidential election, according to federal data. A 2025 Brookings Institution study cited by [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/27/us-postal-service-says-mail-in-ballot-restrictions-now-in-effect) found approximately four cases of mail-ballot fraud per 10 million ballots cast.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream progressive case against these restrictions rests on clear ground: there is no empirical evidence of systematic mail-ballot fraud at a scale capable of affecting election outcomes, and the restrictions will predictably fall harder on groups — elderly voters, rural voters, voters with disabilities, communities of colour — who disproportionately rely on mail voting. The March executive order was of dubious constitutionality from the start: Article II of the Constitution gives Congress and the states, not the president, primary authority over federal elections. The Supreme Court's procedural ruling is not an endorsement of the policy's legality but rather a bureaucratic win that allows the administration to run the clock toward November 3 before a full constitutional adjudication. The 24-state lawsuit is the correct democratic response. In this view, the restrictions represent voter suppression dressed in the rhetoric of election integrity.

## A different read

The progressive critique is not wrong about the fraud statistics, but it misreads where the political and legal drama will actually land — and it underestimates how deliberately the administration has calibrated the timing.

Start with the constitutional question. Trump's executive order claimed authority under his power to ensure the faithful execution of federal election laws, specifically arguing that states have not adequately verified voter eligibility. The Supreme Court's 6-3 majority did not validate this theory; it ruled on the narrow procedural question of when the states had standing to sue. That is an important distinction. The constitutional question — does the president have the authority to direct the USPS to filter ballot delivery by voter list? — remains open. And given the usual pace of federal litigation, it is quite possible the November election will occur before a definitive ruling on constitutionality, meaning whatever outcome emerges will carry an asterisk of contested legitimacy. This is not a feature the administration accidentally produced; it is the operational logic of running out the clock.

The deeper issue is federalism. The US has a deliberately decentralised election system — fifty different systems, in practice — because the Founders were correctly wary of centralised federal control over elections. State primacy in election administration is not merely a liberal talking point; it is the constitutional design, and conservatives have historically invoked it loudly when federal overreach threatened state prerogatives. The irony of a conservative administration asserting federal executive power to override state election administration is one the right-leaning legal establishment has been notably quiet about. It is worth asking why.

Part of the answer is that the voter-list verification rationale, however unsupported by fraud data, resonates with a Republican base that has believed since 2020 that mail voting is insecure. The Brookings four-in-ten-million figure is not a lie; it is simply not what a significant portion of the electorate believes. And administrations, even ones operating well beyond the evidence base, can inflict real structural damage through implementation: a voter who is incorrectly excluded from the USPS delivery list, and who does not receive a mail ballot, may simply not vote — not because of fraud, but because of administrative error compounding at scale. In a closely contested House race, the margin does not need to be large.

The historical comparison is the contested election administration of the late 19th century, when Reconstruction-era federal voting protection was systematically dismantled through a combination of state legislation, administrative obstruction, and judicial acquiescence. No single act was decisive; the cumulative effect was the disenfranchisement of millions of voters over two decades. The lesson is not that every disputed election rule is a precursor to a comparable catastrophe — but that the legal and institutional norms protecting ballot access are weaker than they appear, especially when judicial remedies lag real-world implementation timelines.

There is also a legitimate federalism argument on the other side that deserves acknowledgment: many states, particularly Republican-run ones, have genuinely outdated and inadequately maintained voter rolls, and some degree of federal baseline standardisation in voter list hygiene is not inherently illegitimate. The problem is that the March executive order was written not as a measured voter-list-maintenance standard but as a framework that maximises barriers to mail voting specifically — a method that Republican voters use at substantially lower rates than Democrats. The motivation is transparent, which is why even some conservative election lawyers have been uncomfortable with it.

## What to watch

Track the Massachusetts federal case and whether a judge issues a fresh injunction before states begin mailing ballots — the timeline is extremely tight, perhaps two to three weeks. Watch whether any Republican-run states quietly resist implementing the USPS list-verification requirement, which would add a fascinating federalism wrinkle. Monitor early mail-ballot return rates compared to 2022 midterms as a real-time signal of implementation disruption. And watch the Supreme Court docket: if a fast-tracked appeal of the Massachusetts ruling arrives before the court by mid-September, the justices' decision on whether to hear it on an emergency basis will be the clearest signal yet of whether the constitutional question will be resolved before or after the vote.
