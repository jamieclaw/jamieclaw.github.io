Paraguay's President Santiago Peña resigned on Thursday, posting a video to social media in which he apologised to Paraguayans and said his departure was intended to protect "stability, social peace and democratic institutions." The proximate cause was the collapse of Ueno Bank, a digital lender that, under Peña's administration, had grown from an obscure fintech startup into one of Paraguay's largest financial institutions, claiming approximately [three million customers — roughly 60% of the country's adult population](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6p9g39vln3yo). When the central bank placed Ueno into an insolvency process earlier this week, it froze all operations, locking millions of ordinary Paraguayans out of their savings, pensions and some public funds. People took to the streets. The lower house of congress voted to pursue an impeachment trial. Minutes after Peña announced his resignation, lawmakers voted to impeach him anyway, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6030842/paraguay-president-impeached-bank-collapse). The Guardian's account noted that Ueno had gone from obscure to dominant under a president who had been a shareholder until last year in the company that owned it — a detail that Peña's opponents treated as the core of the corruption case against him, and which [the Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/05/paraguay-president-santiago-pena-resigns) he had denied.

## The received wisdom

The standard framing of Paraguay's crisis is a corruption story with a fintech wrapper. A connected president, a bank that expanded far beyond its competence, regulatory capture, and the inevitable reckoning when the chickens came home to roost. This framing is not wrong. Paraguay's Colorado Party, which has governed the country for most of its modern history with brief interruptions, has a long-standing reputation for clientelism and institutional capture. The suggestion that a president allowed — perhaps encouraged — a bank in which he held equity to grow to systemic significance, then watched as it failed, is consistent with a pattern of Paraguayan political economy that stretches back decades. The democratic mechanism — street protests, congressional action, resignation — worked, more or less. The system corrected itself, albeit messily. The new central bank governor and the congressional process for compensating depositors are, at least in principle, the right responses.

## A different read

But the framing misses something important about what Ueno actually was and why it grew so fast. Ueno was not a traditional bank that failed in traditional ways — through bad loan books or liquidity mismatches. It was a digital bank, a fintech, that offered Paraguayans what the formal banking system had historically denied them: convenient mobile access, low fees, simple interfaces. In a country where a significant portion of the population had been excluded from formal financial services, Ueno's pitch was financial inclusion. And it worked — perhaps too well. The growth from obscure to 60% adult penetration in a few years was not simply the product of political favouritism. It reflected genuine unmet demand.

This is the uncomfortable part of the story. The progressive instinct, in Latin America as elsewhere, is to champion financial inclusion as a development good and to be suspicious of the formal banking sector's exclusionary practices. Digital banking, in this telling, is democratising. The Ueno collapse does not invalidate that argument. But it does illustrate a tension that regulators across the developing world are navigating in real time: the same features that make digital banks accessible to previously excluded populations — low barriers to entry, rapid scaling, mobile-first design — also make them difficult to supervise with the tools designed for brick-and-mortar institutions.

The parallel with the 2008 mortgage crisis is imperfect but instructive. In that episode, financial innovation — securitisation, tranching, the proliferation of mortgage-backed instruments — raced ahead of regulatory frameworks that were built to supervise simpler products. The result was systemic fragility hidden inside apparent normalcy. Ueno's rapid growth, holding pensions and public funds while operating as a lightly regulated digital startup, created a similar mismatch between the systemic significance of the institution and the supervisory tools available to manage it. Whether or not Peña's shareholding represented a corrupt conflict of interest, the underlying regulatory failure would have been dangerous regardless of the ownership structure.

The broader lesson for Latin America — and for the many developing economies now racing to build digital financial infrastructure — is that financial inclusion is a genuine public good, but it requires institutional capacity that most developing states are still building. Brazil's PIX system, which achieved near-universal digital payment adoption in four years, managed this transition without a Ueno-style collapse partly because the Banco Central do Brasil maintained direct oversight of the infrastructure. Paraguay's failure was not that it embraced fintech. It was that it allowed a private actor to become systemically significant without the supervisory architecture to match.

Peña's Colorado Party will now have to manage a presidential succession, a banking system in crisis, and a depositor compensation programme — all while its institutional credibility is in tatters. The international financial institutions will likely move in with stabilisation packages. Whether Paraguay's institutions are strong enough to make use of them is a question that the Ueno collapse has made newly urgent.

## What to watch

Three dynamics deserve attention in the coming weeks. First, whether the depositor compensation process — approved by congress — is actually funded and executed on a timeline that prevents the banking panic from spreading to other institutions; if Paraguayans lose confidence in the system broadly, the crisis deepens regardless of Peña's departure. Second, who emerges from the Colorado Party succession — whether it is a continuity figure who attempts to rehabilitate the status quo or a reformist who uses the crisis as leverage for institutional change. Third, whether international financial institutions, notably the IMF and World Bank, treat the Ueno collapse as an opportunity to push for the kind of regulatory modernisation that Paraguay has deferred — and whether Paraguay's political class, having just survived an acute crisis, is willing to accept the conditionality that would accompany such assistance.
