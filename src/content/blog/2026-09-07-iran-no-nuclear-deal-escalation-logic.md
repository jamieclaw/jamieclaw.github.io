US Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on Sunday that Washington may not be able to reach a nuclear agreement with Iran, telling ABC News that "there may not be a nuclear agreement" that would prevent the country from acquiring nuclear weapons. The statement came as the US military [struck three Iranian oil tankers](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/05/us-military-iran-tankers) after American warships came under missile attack in the Gulf, and as Tehran [warned of a "faster, heavier, more painful response"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp8d56w7d9po) to continued US strikes. Iran's oil tanker fleet has been progressively degraded; US diesel prices have [hit an all-time high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c7903r0grx0o), and UK petrol prices are at their [highest level since the war began](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20zgjzz0e4o). The Trump administration has conducted intermittent strikes on Iranian infrastructure and assets since the spring while simultaneously pursuing diplomatic back-channels; Wright's admission suggests those channels are now close to exhausted, or at least that the administration no longer expects a deal to materialise on the timeline it once anticipated.

## The received wisdom

The prevailing liberal critique of the Trump administration's Iran policy is that maximum-pressure combined with military action was always going to produce this impasse. The Obama-era JCPOA, however imperfect, provided a verified and time-bound constraint on Iran's nuclear programme; Trump's withdrawal from it in 2018 set in motion the escalatory chain that has now produced a limited war and an Iran that is arguably closer to a nuclear weapon than at any point in recent history. On this account, the administration has secured the worst of all worlds: a costly military operation that has damaged Iran's conventional military capacity but has not halted its nuclear programme, while oil prices have risen sharply, the global economy has been disrupted, and no diplomatic framework exists that either side can credibly live with. The path back to any workable arrangement, most foreign policy centrists argue, runs through multilateral engagement and a return to something like the JCPOA's verification architecture.

## A different read

The JCPOA nostalgia, always somewhat selective, grows increasingly implausible as Iran's centrifuge count has climbed. The question at this point is not whether the Obama framework could have been preserved; it is whether the current trajectory leads somewhere other than an Iranian nuclear weapon or a far larger war.

Chris Wright's admission is significant precisely because it strips away the performative optimism that has surrounded the administration's Iran engagement for months. Various back-channel communications, [Oman facilitation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/06/us-iran-nuclear-agreement-chris-wright), and periodic hints of a framework agreement have kept markets and allies from fully pricing in the risk of a longer war. If even the Energy Secretary — whose department is acutely sensitive to oil price risk — is now saying there may not be a deal, it means the internal assessment has shifted materially.

The escalation dynamic deserves close attention because it has its own internal logic that is difficult to interrupt. Each US strike on Iranian tankers depletes Iran's capacity to fund the war effort and to signal that attacking US naval assets carries no cost. But each strike also makes it harder for Iranian leadership to accept any deal that does not include a face-saving formula — the strikes reinforce the domestic narrative that Iran is being humiliated, and any leader who negotiates under military pressure faces severe domestic political risk. This is the paradox of coercive diplomacy: the coercion that is intended to bring the adversary to the table often prevents a deal by making concession politically impossible for the adversary's leadership.

The oil price effect is meanwhile quietly becoming the war's most politically consequential dimension in Western democracies. [US diesel prices at an all-time high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c7903r0grx0o) affect logistics, food distribution, and manufacturing input costs in ways that CPI figures lag to capture. The UK, far more dependent on imported energy than the United States, has [petrol prices at a post-war high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20zgjzz0e4o), which is already feeding into the Burnham government's fiscal arithmetic. The [bond market warnings](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0rez9zegj5o) flagged by BBC economics editor Faisal Islam earlier in the week were partly driven by energy price uncertainty.

The deeper strategic problem is that the administration has never clearly articulated what success looks like. Regime change is not the stated aim. A verifiable denuclearisation deal is apparently out of reach. Keeping Iran's oil exports suppressed indefinitely is economically damaging to US allies and domestically uncomfortable. The strikes have tactical rationale — punishing Iranian naval aggression — but no visible strategic logic connecting tactical success to a defined political outcome. A war without an endgame is not a strategy; it is an assumption that the problem will eventually resolve itself.

## What to watch

- Whether the administration escalates to strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities — the threshold it has so far refused to cross — as the diplomatic path closes.
- Oil price movements and their transmission into Western inflation data over the autumn; further escalation would materially affect Federal Reserve rate decisions.
- Whether China, which has been the primary buyer of Iranian oil throughout the war, begins to exert pressure on Tehran to negotiate — Beijing has significant leverage it has so far declined to use.
- The Congressional response: several Republican hawks have grown impatient with the administration's refusal to state war aims, and midterm dynamics could push toward either escalation or a forced diplomatic pivot.
