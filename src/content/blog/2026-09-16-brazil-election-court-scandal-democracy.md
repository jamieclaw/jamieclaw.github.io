A [corruption scandal has exploded inside Brazil's supreme court](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/15/brazil-supreme-court-presidential-election) at the precise moment the country's presidential election campaign is reaching its peak, with polls showing a dead heat between the candidates. The timing is not incidental: the supreme court has been one of the primary institutional actors in the years-long legal battles over Jair Bolsonaro's political future, and its credibility is directly bound up with the question of whether Brazil's democratic rules are applied impartially or as political instruments. [Flávio Bolsonaro is drawing enormous crowds in Minas Gerais](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/14/brazil-bolsonaro-far-right-minas-gerais), Brazil's bellwether state, firing up a base that has consistently argued the judiciary acted as a partisan actor against the Bolsonaro movement. President Lula's government, meanwhile, faces questions about whether the court it cultivated as a bulwark against the right has become a liability.

## The received wisdom

The standard liberal-democratic framing of Brazil's political crisis places the Bolsonaro movement — and its international analogues in Trump's MAGA, Orbán's Fidesz, and European far-right parties — as the primary threat to democratic institutions. On this reading, Brazil's supreme court, whatever its imperfections, has served as an essential firewall against a movement that openly fantasised about military intervention, questioned electoral integrity without evidence, and cultivated an anti-institutional political culture. The justices who investigated Bolsonaro's circle were not acting politically; they were applying the law to powerful people who expected to be above it. Any scandal touching the court must be evaluated against the backdrop of what the court was defending against.

This framing has been dominant in the Western press coverage of Brazil for five years, and it is not without merit. The January 8, 2023 Brasília riots were real. The anti-democratic currents in the Bolsonaro movement were real. Institutional defence of electoral democracy has genuine value.

## A different read

The trouble with using institutional legitimacy as a weapon against political opponents is that institutions are legitimated by their neutrality — and once that neutrality is credibly questioned, the weapon turns against its wielders.

Brazil's supreme court is not a passive constitutional arbiter. Under Chief Justice Alexandre de Moraes, it has assumed extraordinary powers: investigating political actors, issuing content removal orders against social media companies, and playing an active role in the political fortunes of Bolsonaro's movement. These actions were framed as defensive — protecting democracy from a populist threat — and much of the Western media accepted that framing uncritically. But the institutional precedent being set was always more dangerous than the immediate political target: a court that has discovered it can act as a political actor when the cause seems just will be difficult to restrain when the cause is less sympathetic.

The corruption scandal now erupting [on the eve of the election](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/15/brazil-supreme-court-presidential-election) is a direct consequence of this dynamic. When institutions accumulate power beyond their traditional mandates, they also accumulate the corruption and factionalism that power always attracts. This is not a specifically Brazilian problem; it is a structural one. The same pattern played out in Poland under the Law and Justice government, which also justified judicial overreach as defensive necessity against a hostile establishment — and which produced exactly the kind of captured judiciary it claimed to be fighting.

Students of Roman history will recognise the logic: Sulla marched on Rome to "restore the Republic," and the Republic never recovered from his restoration. The lesson is not that Bolsonaro was right about the 2022 election (the evidence overwhelmingly indicates he was not) but that the response to populist pressure on institutions must itself be institutional — transparent, rule-bound, and modest in scope — rather than adaptive and expansive. An activist court that saves democracy from one threat has also demonstrated that courts can be political actors; the next government will draw its own lessons accordingly.

The dead-heat polling in Brazil is a reminder that elections are not primarily moral verdicts; they are mechanisms for managing political conflict without violence. If [Flávio Bolsonaro](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/14/brazil-bolsonaro-far-right-minas-gerais) wins — and the polls suggest he might — the question of how the current court's legacy is handled will be the first test of whether Brazil's democratic institutions can absorb an alternation of power. If the court's recent activism becomes a political liability for Lula's coalition, it will also be a cautionary tale for every liberal democracy that has convinced itself that using powerful institutions against populists is a clean solution to a messy problem. It is not. It is a gamble, and the house does not always win.

## What to watch

- The specific nature of the supreme court scandal: whether it involves direct corruption or procedural overreach will determine its electoral impact differently.
- Minas Gerais polling in the final campaign week — this state has correctly predicted the national winner in every election since 1994.
- Whether the Lula campaign attempts to distance itself from the court's record or doubles down on defending it.
- International observer assessments: the EU and OAS electoral missions will be crucial validators of whatever result emerges, and both sides have already framed their legitimacy narratives.
