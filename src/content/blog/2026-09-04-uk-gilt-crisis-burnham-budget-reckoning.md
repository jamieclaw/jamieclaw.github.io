Andy Burnham has been Prime Minister for barely a fortnight, and the bond market is already issuing its verdict. [UK long-term borrowing costs have risen to their highest level since 1998](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8d39vq779no?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), pushing the yield on 30-year gilts above levels that make Whitehall officials quietly anxious. At his first Prime Minister's Questions this week, Burnham was [pressed repeatedly on the economy](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn9wv7pene2o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as opposition benches accused him of lacking a credible fiscal plan for the October Budget. The BBC's economics editor has described the bond market pressure as a ["wildfire keeping world leaders up at night"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0rez9zegj5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). With [almost half of UK households](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crerw510gdzo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) reporting they do not feel the benefits of economic growth, the political arithmetic is forbidding.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for Burnham's position is coherent, and it deserves to be stated plainly. His predecessor Keir Starmer inherited a structural mess: a decade of Conservative austerity followed by pandemic-era spending, followed by post-Brexit supply shocks and an energy crisis that left Britain with unusually high underlying inflation. The argument that gilt yields are rising because global bond markets are pricing in a stronger-for-longer central bank rate — rather than because investors distrust Labour specifically — has genuine empirical support. The IMF and others have noted that sovereign borrowing costs have risen across G7 economies simultaneously. Burnham's allies also point out that the former Manchester mayor built a reputation precisely for pragmatic delivery — the Metrolink extension, the homelessness programme — rather than ideological signalling. If anyone can triangulate between his party's left wing and a sceptical bond market, the argument goes, it is him.

## A different read

The charitable reading above is true as far as it goes, but it systematically understates what is distinctive about Britain's predicament — and about the political constraints that make it unusually hard to address.

Start with the numbers. [UK long-term borrowing costs at their highest since 1998](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8d39vq779no?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) is not simply a synchronised global story. In 1998, Britain was in the early phase of Gordon Brown's self-imposed fiscal rules — the period of conspicuous prudence that gave New Labour the credibility to spend later. The gilts market then was rewarding the signal that a left-of-centre government understood the cost of losing investor confidence. What investors are looking for now is a comparable signal from Burnham, and so far they are not receiving it.

Burnham's rhetoric at PMQs was notably vague on the spending side. He spoke about [devolution and public control of essential services](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z528rdp6o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — a policy direction that implies substantial ongoing commitments — without specifying what existing programmes would be squeezed. His allies' promise to tackle ["mega-donors" in UK politics](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c7839kyekm0o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) is the kind of populist gesture that plays well in the hall but does nothing for the spreadsheet.

The deeper structural problem is one that no British prime minister of either party has been willing to confront honestly since roughly 2010: the gap between what the electorate expects the state to provide — NHS, social care, pensions, defence — and what tax receipts at current rates can plausibly fund. Each new government inherits this gap and responds by either raising taxes (which reduces growth), borrowing (which raises yields), or promising reform that never materialises. Burnham's ["Manchesterism"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyl3z2xld8o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) is a genuine governing philosophy — decentralised, community-oriented, pragmatic — but it was developed to manage a city-region budget, not to close a £50bn-plus structural hole in the national accounts.

There is a historical parallel here that should give Burnham's team pause. François Mitterrand arrived at the Élysée in 1981 with enormous popular energy and a bold programme of nationalisations and reflation. Within two years the franc had been devalued three times and his government had performed the famous "tournant de la rigueur" — turning sharply towards austerity. The bond market, in other words, tends to win. The question is not whether Burnham will eventually have to disappoint his base on fiscal matters, but whether he chooses to do so on his own terms (planned, credible, clearly communicated) or on the market's terms (chaotic, forced, politically devastating). Gordon Brown's framework in 1997 is the positive British precedent; Liz Truss's forty-five days in office in 2022 is the cautionary one.

Kemi Badenoch, now Tory leader, has already accused Burnham of ["kicking defence spending into the long grass"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crk3xdv0z71o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), which is politically shrewd: in an era of Ukrainian-theatre conflict and expanded NATO commitments, any government seen to be soft on defence will pay a price with the median voter. Burnham's position is that he supports the NATO two-percent commitment in principle but has not yet found the money. That answer will not survive contact with the October Budget.

The [Reform UK spending plan](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/czxz4dl1plwo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) promising £80 billion in cuts is, in the short run, a political gift to Burnham — it allows him to occupy the responsible centre by comparison. But it is a dangerous comfort. Farage's populist pressure from the right will constrain Burnham's room to raise taxes, while his own backbenches will resist any serious spending restraint. That is a pincer that produces inertia — and inertia, in a gilt market already on edge, is its own form of crisis.

## What to watch

- The October Budget announcement: whether Burnham's team can produce credible multi-year spending figures that satisfy the Office for Budget Responsibility without triggering a backbench revolt.
- Thirty-year gilt yields in the week before and after the Budget statement — the market will deliver its verdict faster than any poll.
- The Reform UK conference response: if Farage successfully frames any tax rises as vindication of his warnings, Labour's poll lead could erode sharply.
- Any sign that the Bank of England adjusts its forward guidance in response to fiscal concerns — that would effectively force Burnham's hand before he is ready.
