[The FBI on Monday arrested Wanying Zhang, also known as Heather, on charges of acting as an unregistered agent of the Chinese government](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/05/g-s1-146410/fbi-arrests-a-woman-accused-of-spying-on-taiwan-leaders-family-for-china). According to US prosecutors, Zhang surveilled the family of the Taiwanese president's son and his associates in the United States, collecting information on their movements, relationships, and contacts for transmission to a Chinese state handler. The operation is part of a pattern that American counterintelligence has been tracking for several years: Chinese intelligence services using individuals embedded in Chinese-American communities as surveillance assets against Taiwanese government officials' overseas family members, Democratic Progressive Party diaspora activists, and Falun Gong community leaders. Zhang's arrest is the latest in a series of prosecutions — the FBI has brought charges against Chinese intelligence operatives in at least six states over the past three years — but the surveillance of a sitting foreign head of state's family marks an escalation in the brazenness of the operations that prosecutors are willing to charge.

## The received wisdom

The liberal foreign policy establishment tends to respond to Chinese espionage cases with a mixture of genuine alarm and nervous institutionalism. Yes, China's foreign intelligence operations are aggressive, pervasive, and include activities that would not be tolerated if conducted by any other state. But — the mainstream argument continues — overreacting risks derailing the broader US-China relationship at a moment when cooperation on climate, fentanyl precursors, and financial stability is both necessary and fragile. The Biden-era framework, now partially carried over in Trump's more transactional approach, was essentially: prosecute the individual cases, express concern through diplomatic channels, and avoid the kind of escalatory rhetoric that turns a law enforcement matter into a geopolitical crisis. The arrest of Zhang is accordingly welcomed as appropriate law enforcement while any suggestion that it reveals a systemic problem in US-China relations — one that requires structural rather than case-by-case responses — is treated with considerable caution.

## A different read

The caution is understandable and almost entirely misplaced.

The Wanying Zhang prosecution is not an isolated case. It is the visible tip of an intelligence operation that American security professionals have been describing in increasing detail and with increasing frustration for the better part of a decade. [The FBI and MI5 have both publicly stated — in a joint briefing in 2022, unprecedented in its directness — that Chinese state intelligence represents the largest single counterintelligence threat to the United States and United Kingdom, exceeding Russia in scale if not in the immediacy of its kinetic effects](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cv5yn3nwp5n5o). The operations documented in American federal court filings over the past three years reveal a systematic programme: identifying Chinese nationals in America with community credibility, leveraging family connections in China as coercive pressure, and tasking the resulting network with surveillance, intimidation, and intelligence collection against dissident communities, Taiwanese officials' networks, and Uyghur activists.

The specific targeting of the Taiwanese president's son's family is significant for a reason the news cycle has underplayed. Taiwan's president is democratically elected and represents a government that the United States has committed, under the Taiwan Relations Act, to providing with sufficient defensive capability to resist coercion. Surveilling that president's family members in the United States is not a grey-area intelligence operation; it is a direct assertion by Beijing that the TRA's implied commitments do not constrain Chinese intelligence activities on American soil. The message is: we can reach into your country and collect information about your most sensitive bilateral relationship, and we are willing to do so openly enough that you will eventually find out, because demonstrating capability is part of the deterrence communication.

The comparison with Russia's operations is instructive precisely because of the contrast. Russian intelligence in the West has been aggressive and violent — the Salisbury poisonings, the interference operations, the cyber attacks — and the Western response, though imperfect, has included serious diplomatic, financial, and technical countermeasures. Chinese intelligence operations are more patient, more diffuse, and in aggregate far more damaging to Western institutions, technology bases, and strategic decision-making: [the MI5 warning about Chinese intelligence infiltration of British universities, covered here last week](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cv5yn3nwp5n5o), is part of the same pattern. Yet the response has been systematically muted, because the economic interdependence that makes the threat so hard to counter also makes the confrontation of it politically costly in ways that confronting Russia never was.

The Zhang arrest is a data point in the longer argument about whether America is genuinely prepared to treat China's intelligence activities as the systemic problem they demonstrably are, or whether the case-by-case prosecution model — which absorbs significant FBI and DOJ resources while leaving the underlying programme intact — is actually a way of managing political discomfort rather than addressing a national security problem. The prosecutions are necessary. They are not sufficient.

## What to watch

- **Zhang's trial and what it reveals**: Whether prosecutors introduce evidence of a broader network will determine how much of the operational picture becomes public. Chinese intelligence tradecraft in these cases is designed to limit what can be disclosed in open court.
- **Taiwan's response**: Taipei has been quietly pressing Washington to treat Chinese intelligence targeting of Taiwanese officials and their families as a treaty-level concern under the TRA. Whether this case accelerates that conversation will be visible in the next set of US-Taiwan security consultations.
- **UK parallel action**: Given the MI5 briefings about Chinese intelligence at British universities and political institutions, watch for a parallel UK prosecution or expulsion of diplomatic personnel as a show of solidarity.
- **Midterm politics**: Chinese espionage prosecutions have historically been handled by both parties as bipartisan national security matters. Whether the Trump administration uses this case as a domestic political instrument — or keeps it in the law enforcement lane — will signal something about its China strategy's coherence.
