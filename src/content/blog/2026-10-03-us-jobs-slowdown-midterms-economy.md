The United States economy added only [29,000 jobs in September](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5989140/jobs-labor-wages-federal-reserve), a number so anaemic it barely registers against the roughly 150,000 new workers who enter the labour force each month. The unemployment rate edged up to 4.2 percent. Equally worrying, revisions shaved another 60,000 from the July and August tallies, meaning the summer's apparent resilience was even softer than it looked. The report arrives six weeks before the November midterm elections, at a moment when [Al Jazeera noted](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/2/us-job-growth-slows-as-unemployment-rises-before-midterm-elections) that the administration had been pointing to labour-market stability as proof that its trade and energy policies were working. That claim has now become much harder to sustain.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream economic commentary will reach for the obvious explanations. Tariffs on imported inputs have raised costs for manufacturers; the ongoing diesel-price shock from Trump's export-ban threats has cascaded through logistics and distribution; and the Federal Reserve, navigating an uncomfortable combination of slowing growth and sticky services inflation, has hesitated to cut rates aggressively enough to stimulate hiring. Progressive economists will argue this is precisely what heterodox trade nationalism was always going to produce: a stagflationary squeeze that hurts workers more than the corporate executives it was nominally designed to discipline. There is genuine evidence for this reading. Manufacturing payrolls have been soft all year. Retailers have slowed hiring as consumer confidence wobbles. Even the services sector, which carried the post-pandemic economy, is no longer generating the monthly surpluses that masked deeper structural problems. The labour market was the last strong card in the administration's economic hand, and September may have bent it.

## A different read

There is a temptation, common in political commentary, to treat every weak data point as decisive proof that an entire policy philosophy has failed. That temptation should be resisted — but so should the opposite reflex, which is to dismiss a weak report as a single-month blip and wait for "more data." Both readings are evasions, and the September number deserves a harder look at what it actually reveals about the structural choices the Trump administration has made.

The core problem is not just sluggish hiring. It is the nature of the slowdown. [The BBC's business desk](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm2d6gg0642yo) described the report as a "sharp slowdown," and the details bear that out: gains were concentrated in healthcare and government, the two sectors most insulated from market forces, while private-sector goods-producing industries were essentially flat. This is not a pattern that suggests a temporary weather- or strike-related disruption. It looks more like the leading edge of a demand compression that began when tariffs raised consumer prices and the diesel shock hit distribution networks.

Here is where the right-of-centre case for Trump's economic programme runs into an honest difficulty. The intellectual argument for economic nationalism — the case made by trade hawks from Pat Buchanan to Marco Rubio's reindustrialisation theorists — rests on the premise that short-term pain in consumption would be offset by longer-term gains in manufacturing employment and strategic autonomy. That is a defensible proposition in principle. Alexander Hamilton made a version of it in 1791; Friedrich List elaborated it for nineteenth-century Germany; the post-war developmental states of East Asia tested it with real success. The historical record on industrial policy is not uniformly negative.

But the Hamiltonian bargain has a condition attached: the short-term pain must be borne by those who can absorb it, and the longer-term gains must be credible enough that workers accept the transition. Neither condition currently obtains. Working-class households, who disproportionately depend on consumer goods imports for their standard of living and who cannot hedge against diesel-driven price spikes, are absorbing the costs. Meanwhile, the manufacturing renaissance that was supposed to justify the sacrifice remains embryonic. [NPR's Planet Money team](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5988075/why-is-diesel-more-expensive-than-regular-gas) noted that diesel prices, which carry through into virtually every supply chain in the country, remain at historic highs. That is not a foundation for a hiring boom in goods production.

There is a second, politically sharper problem. The administration's electoral coalition was assembled on the implicit promise that its economic disruptions would deliver material gains to the constituencies — blue-collar workers in manufacturing states, rural communities dependent on agriculture and energy — whose living costs had been rising for a generation before Trump arrived. A 29,000-job September suggests that promise is fraying. The Federal Reserve's hesitancy to cut rates, perfectly rational given its dual mandate, effectively transmits the cost of the administration's fiscal and trade choices to the very mortgage-payers and small-business owners who voted for it. That is not a sustainable political economy.

None of this means the administration's critics are right that free trade and globalisation were costless. They were not, and the rustbelt hollowing-out that preceded Trump's first term was a real policy failure by both parties. But a coherent economic nationalism requires sequencing: industrial capacity has to be built *before* you extract prices from consumers, not *after.* Running the costs before the benefits is a peculiarly self-defeating version of an otherwise serious intellectual tradition.

## What to watch

Watch whether the Federal Reserve's next meeting produces a rate cut and, if so, whether the administration takes credit for it or attacks the Fed for acting too slowly — the political response will signal whether anyone inside the West Wing understands the underlying mechanics. Watch monthly revisions: if August and September are both later marked down, the Q3 labour-market story becomes considerably darker entering Q4. Watch consumer-credit data: if households are borrowing to maintain spending while job growth stalls, the next shock — a large delinquency spike — could arrive quickly. And watch midterm polling in Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Michigan, where the gap between Trump's rhetorical promise to working people and the observable economic reality will be litigated most directly.
