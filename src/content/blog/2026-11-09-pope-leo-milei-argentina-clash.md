Pope Leo XIV arrived in Argentina on Sunday for a three-day visit — the first papal trip to the country since John Paul II in 1987 — walking into what the Guardian described as a [calculated ideological confrontation with President Javier Milei](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/08/popes-visit-to-argentina-sets-up-showdown-with-hyper-capitalist-milei). Leo, who completed the Uruguay leg of his South American tour on Saturday, began his Argentine itinerary at the Cottolengo Don Orione, a Catholic-run home for roughly four hundred residents with disabilities outside Buenos Aires. The facility's coordinator told the Guardian that the government had cut off the supply of medications, special foods, and nappies since the previous November, with the matter now in court. Milei's administration has made sweeping cuts to disability funding as part of a broader austerity programme that the government argues was necessary to eliminate a fiscal deficit that was driving hyperinflation. The thinktank Mate estimates that retirees on the minimum pension — sixty-three percent of the total — have lost roughly thirty percent of their purchasing power since Milei took office. Al Jazeera further reported that [the pope's visit follows his predecessor's historic reconciliation with the region's social movements](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/8/president-milei-welcomes-pope-leo-during-historic-visit-to-argentina) and comes during a papal Latin American tour that includes Peru.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading treats this as a morality play with clear roles: the humble pastor arrives to speak truth to power, and the chainsaw-wielding ideologue squirms. Milei, after all, called Leo's predecessor Francis an "imbecile" and the "representative of the Evil One on Earth" before softening his language when it became politically useful. His government's disability cuts, his refusal to regulate artificial intelligence, his inaugural address brandishing an actual chainsaw as a symbol of public spending reduction — all of this marks him, in progressive eyes, as exactly the kind of leader that Catholic social teaching was built to rebuke. The left-wing reading further holds that the Church, having rehabilitated its relationship with Latin America's poor through Francis and now Leo, has become an unlikely counterweight to the ideological right. Buenos Aires Archbishop Jorge García Cuerva has already said Leo will find "a divided Argentina and a large number of very poor brothers and sisters." The cardboard sign at the weekly retirees' march — "Save us Pope Leo XIV — Milei is killing us" — writes the story for those who wish to see it that way.

## A different read

The more interesting and less comfortable question is whether the Church's critique of Milei is itself as clean as the moral geography implies. Argentina's recurring crises are not primarily the product of spending cuts; they are the product of decades of exactly the kind of expansionary fiscal policy that Catholic social doctrine, at its sentimental worst, tends to applaud. Argentina has defaulted on its sovereign debt nine times. Its inflation rate before Milei's reforms reached triple digits. The pension system that Milei is being accused of gutting was already delivering poverty-level incomes to most beneficiaries — not because of his cuts but because the fund had been progressively raided by previous administrations. When the thinktank Mate reports that retirees have lost thirty percent of their purchasing power under Milei, the unasked question is what they were losing each month under the inflationary policies his reforms were designed to reverse.

This does not make the disability funding cuts morally costless. The Cottolengo home's coordinator described real deprivation — missing medications, court battles, four hundred vulnerable people caught in an administrative impasse that has lasted a year. These are not abstractions. But the Church's social teaching at its best distinguishes between the obligation to the poor and the political mechanism for fulfilling that obligation. Leo XIV himself said in May that AI must be "disarmed"; the Church's preference for human dignity over algorithmic efficiency is coherent and worth defending. That same coherence is not automatically served by endorsing the fiscal arrangements that drove Argentina into its recurring destitution.

Milei's reception of the pope at the airport was deliberately civil. His government's communications team has worked to frame the visit as evidence of normalcy and international legitimacy rather than a rebuke. "There's no way the pope is going to come here to comment on local politics," said Social Policy Minister Sandra Pettovello. That is partly true, and partly a wager: Leo, like Francis, operates through suggestion and itinerary rather than direct political endorsement. His visit to the FAdeA state military aircraft factory in Córdoba, where workers have protested Milei's cuts, is not an accident. Neither is his opening at the disability home. The itinerary is the speech.

What makes this genuinely interesting as a conservative question is the historical tension between subsidiarity and solidarity. Catholic social teaching has always maintained both: the principle that decisions should be made at the lowest competent level, and the obligation of mutual care that transcends market outcomes. Milei is an unusual figure — a self-described anarcho-capitalist who carries a chainsaw but also, by his own account, has embraced religious observance and softened his rhetoric toward Francis. Whether there is a version of his economic project that can be made consistent with the Church's anthropology — a state small enough to be efficient but capable of protecting the genuinely vulnerable — is a real theological and political question, not a settled one. The visit may be less a confrontation than a negotiation about what markets are for, conducted in the language of papal itinerary rather than encyclicals.

## What to watch

Watch the precise language of Leo's homily at the FAdeA factory — whether it criticises austerity generically or references Argentina specifically will determine its political reverberation. Watch Milei's response to any papal statement on disability or labour; he is a politician who fights back, and a public dispute with a pope on his home soil carries asymmetric risk. Watch Argentina's inflation figures over the next quarter; Milei's austerity claims legitimacy only if it produces durable price stability rather than simply transferring suffering downward. And watch the evangelical proportion of the Argentine electorate — already at 17.4 percent against 57.7 percent Catholic — as a variable that reduces the Church's political leverage regardless of what Leo says.
