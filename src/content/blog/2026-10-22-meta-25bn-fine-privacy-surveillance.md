A US federal judge has [ordered Meta to pay $25 billion in fines](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/21/judge-orders-meta-to-pay-25bn-in-fines-for-facebook-privacy-violations) for what the court described as deliberate "lies" to users about how their personal data was collected, shared, and monetised on Facebook. The BBC confirmed the ruling, [reporting the fine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqdr8zxrr3dxo) as the largest privacy penalty ever levied against a technology company. The case centres on Meta's repeated misrepresentations to users — and to regulators — about the scope of data sharing, particularly in the period following the Cambridge Analytica scandal. Meta has indicated it intends to appeal. The ruling lands at a politically volatile moment, with US midterm elections less than two weeks away and bipartisan anxiety about Big Tech's role in public discourse running at historic highs.

## The received wisdom

The progressive-regulatory view will greet this ruling as a long-overdue accountability moment. On this reading, Meta's business model — built on the extraction and monetisation of intimate personal data at industrial scale — has always been ethically suspect. The company repeatedly assured users that their data was safe while engineering systems to maximise its commercial utility. The fine, proponents argue, finally attaches meaningful consequence to that conduct. Stricter European-style privacy regulation is the obvious prescription: comprehensive data protection laws, meaningful consent requirements, genuine opt-out mechanisms, and regulators with the resources and political will to enforce them. If the fine is large enough to alter Meta's profit calculations, it may finally incentivise systemic change rather than surface-level compliance.

This is a serious argument. Corporate behaviour does respond to financial incentives, and the scale of this fine — if it survives appeal — is at least plausible as a deterrent.

## A different read

The problem with the triumphalist reading is that it confuses punishment with reform. Twenty-five billion dollars is, on its face, a stunning number. But consider what it actually represents in Meta's financial universe. The company's annual revenue in recent years has exceeded $130 billion; its net income has run at $40 billion or above. A one-time fine, even a very large one, does not restructure the underlying economic incentives that produced the misconduct. It functions less as a deterrent and more as a licensing fee — the cost of doing business in a surveillance economy that regulators have defined but not dismantled.

This is the central contradiction of the European-American regulatory consensus on Big Tech: it imposes fines that generate headlines while leaving the architecture of data extraction intact. The EU's GDPR, widely celebrated when it passed in 2018 as a landmark framework for user privacy, has produced a cottage industry of compliance lawyers, consent pop-ups, and cookie banners — and has demonstrably not reduced the amount of personal data collected by the major platforms. Academic research consistently finds that consent mechanisms, as implemented, are designed to obtain agreement rather than inform choice. The regulation has been processed, absorbed, and largely domesticated by the very entities it was meant to discipline.

There is also a competitive dimension that the fine-and-move-on approach completely ignores. Meta's most serious challengers — TikTok, various Chinese-linked platforms — operate under fundamentally different regulatory regimes and have no interest in the consent theatre that Western compliance demands. Imposing costs on Meta without addressing the structural distortions of the digital advertising market simply advantages rivals who play by different rules entirely. The right answer is not to exempt Meta from accountability; it is to think more carefully about what accountability actually requires.

The conservative-liberal tradition in technology policy — sceptical of both corporate impunity and bureaucratic overreach — has long argued for a different approach: structural remedies rather than fines. Breaking up the data advantage, mandating interoperability, creating genuine data portability rights that users can actually exercise, and targeting the business model rather than its most egregious manifestations. The problem with Meta is not that it lies about privacy, though it does. The problem is that its entire competitive position rests on a data monopoly that cannot be addressed through penalties payable from petty cash.

The timing also raises questions. A $25 billion fine handed down two weeks before a midterm election, against a company whose content moderation decisions are themselves a flashpoint in partisan culture wars, invites suspicion about whether legal process and political calendar have been allowed to become too closely aligned. The judicial system's credibility depends on its insulation from electoral pressures, and rulings of this magnitude require particularly careful handling of that perception.

## What to watch

- **The appeal**: Meta's legal team will challenge the fine aggressively, and the appellate record on proportionality in privacy cases is genuinely unsettled. A reduction on appeal would substantially blunt the deterrent effect.
- **Congressional response**: both parties are now competing to be seen as toughest on Big Tech, but their proposed remedies differ sharply. Watch whether the ruling accelerates any legislative action in the lame-duck session.
- **European copycat filings**: the judgment will embolden EU data protection authorities considering their own actions. Whether they produce structural remedies or additional fines will tell us whether the regulatory class has learned anything.
- **Meta's behaviour**: does the company accelerate its pivot toward privacy-preserving architectures (end-to-end encryption, on-device AI processing), or treat the fine as a settled liability and continue as before?
