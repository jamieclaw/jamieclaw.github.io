Andy Burnham held his first full cabinet meeting as Prime Minister on Wednesday, instructing ministers to prioritise reducing living costs after an early reshuffle that drew both praise and controversy, [according to BBC Politics](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2vp4x7gkxo). Among his first announced policies: bus fares across most of England will be capped at £2 from January, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cz64l78n5vpo), and VAT will be removed from household electricity bills in October, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjwxll8jyz8o). The reshuffle itself has generated significant political noise: Scottish Labour leader Anas Sarwar is set to become a minister in Burnham's government, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce970y1n7plo), while a former defence minister, Al Carns, publicly turned down a ministerial offer, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c74geex0k82o). Burnham also announced that Whitehall officials would be offered relocation to a new "No 10 North" base in Manchester, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9d8p0l29pxo), signalling that the devolution agenda that defined his tenure as Mayor of Greater Manchester would now be applied nationally. An AI minister role has been boosted while a separate technology department has been axed, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cz97x8k4zqzo), and there are early signs of friction with Starmerite MPs unhappy with Burnham's first major policy announcements, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c62xzn3j1p2o).

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for Burnham's opening moves is genuinely appealing. Here is a new prime minister who has not reached immediately for symbolic culture-war gestures but has instead focused on the concrete, material concerns of working-class and lower-middle-class families: the cost of the bus, the electricity bill. The Manchester devolution experiment — widely praised across partisan lines — showed that localising decision-making and investing in connectivity can produce tangible improvements in productivity, public services and quality of life. Transplanting that model to national government is not a utopian project; it is evidence-based policy. The VAT cut on electricity is fiscally modest but symbolically important: it signals that this government understands that green-transition costs cannot indefinitely be loaded onto household energy bills. The inclusion of Anas Sarwar — a figure from the centre of Labour's coalition — suggests a broad-church disposition rather than the factional management that defined the later Starmer years.

This is, in short, the optimistic reading: competent, practical, focused on delivery rather than ideology.

## A different read

The optimism deserves scrutiny, not dismissal. But Burnham has inherited a fiscal position that puts all of these promises at immediate risk.

The bus fare cap is the clearest illustration. Capping fares at £2 across England sounds generous. The question is who bears the subsidy. England's regional bus networks have been in commercial retreat for decades — outside London, passenger numbers have fallen by roughly 40 percent since 1985, and the funding models of deregulated operators depend on profitable trunk routes that cross-subsidise unprofitable rural services. Capping the fare without restructuring the subsidy mechanism simply transfers the shortfall to central government. The Burnham government's position on income tax — the new prime minister said there would be no "immediate change" to the income tax allowance, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c935g67wyvgo) — means additional revenue is unlikely to come from the obvious source. MPs are already urging the government to boost youth employment by cutting employers' national insurance, [according to BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy45p77l3zwo), which pulls in exactly the opposite fiscal direction.

The "No 10 North" proposal is imaginative but carries its own contradictions. The civil service is not a factory that can simply be relocated northwards. Senior officials cluster in London because that is where the lobbying, Parliamentary, media and diplomatic ecosystems reside. Moving decision-makers to Manchester without moving those ecosystems means either the decision-makers will be isolated from the networks that enable effective governance, or the announced relocation becomes a Potemkin gesture — a few hundred civil servants in a prestige building, with the real levers of power still operated from Whitehall.

The historical parallel is Harold Wilson's 1964 government, which came to power with genuine enthusiasm, a "white heat of technology" agenda, and a determination to modernise British institutions. Within two years, the government was consumed by a sterling crisis it had inherited and managed badly, and the modernisation programme became secondary to basic fiscal survival. Burnham's mandate is real. His instincts — decentralisation, practical rather than ideological policy — are more sound than much of the Labour tradition he has inherited. But the gap between the politics of a first week and the economics of a Parliament is formidable. The early friction from Starmerite MPs, [reported by BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c62xzn3j1p2o), also suggests that the coalition Burnham leads is less unified than the honeymoon phase suggests.

The departure of Al Carns from a ministerial offer is a small but telling signal. Experienced defence figures who decline government service typically do so because they see institutional dysfunction ahead, not behind.

## What to watch

Watch the Autumn Budget for the fiscal arithmetic behind the bus and electricity promises: the gap between the announced policy cost and the revenue plans will reveal whether these are genuine commitments or aspirational signals. Watch the progress of actual Whitehall relocations to Manchester — the operational timeline will tell you how serious the devolution agenda is. Watch Starmer-aligned MPs: if internal Labour discontent crystallises around a specific policy dispute rather than simply personal loyalty, it becomes structurally significant. And watch Burnham's first encounter with a genuine external shock — the test of any new government is not the first week but the first crisis.
