Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced on Sunday that the killers of Police Constable Andrew Harper — Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers, convicted of manslaughter for dragging the officer to his death behind a car in 2019 — would not benefit from the early release scheme the government introduced to ease record prison overcrowding, [the BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crr4wd81pdpo). The Sentencing Act 2026 was designed to release large numbers of inmates earlier than planned, with the first batch due in October; England and Wales's prison system is more than 97% full. Burnham, responding to months of campaigning by Harper's family and police colleagues, said anyone convicted of unlawful killing — including all forms of manslaughter — would be excluded from the scheme, along with those convicted of indecent assault, death by dangerous driving, and historic child sex offences. He estimated the change would mean "hundreds" more people remain in prison, requiring additional capacity to be found. As a partial offset, Burnham announced plans to deport foreign prisoners — numbering around 10,134 as of June, representing 12% of the prison population — and to release inmates serving indefinite Imprisonment for Public Protection (IPP) sentences, which he described as people who had "more than served their time."

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and penological case for the government's original scheme was real and should be acknowledged honestly. England and Wales has one of the highest incarceration rates in Western Europe, and the prison estate is genuinely at breaking point. A system at 97% capacity cannot safely manage its existing population, let alone absorb additional prisoners. Rehabilitation programmes collapse when prisons are overcrowded; violence increases; staff burnout accelerates. The IPP sentence, which Burnham has now committed to abolishing, was itself a scandal: 2,271 people remain on IPP terms as of June, of whom 856 have never been released despite having completed their minimum terms — [per Ministry of Justice data](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crr4wd81pdpo) — because the Parole Board was not satisfied they were safe. That is an indefinite detention without the procedural safeguards of a life sentence, and its persistence is a genuine injustice. The Domestic Abuse Commissioner was right to note her "deep concern" that many domestic abusers remain eligible for early release despite Sunday's additions to the exemption list.

## A different read

That said, Burnham's announcement illustrates a peculiarly British form of penal incoherence: the tendency to treat prison capacity as a given rather than a policy choice, and to manage the resulting crisis through a series of politically reactive exemptions that satisfy the public's moral intuitions without addressing any of the underlying structural problems.

The Harper case became a political flashpoint precisely because the instincts of the public and the logic of the system were in visible conflict. The killers were convicted of manslaughter, not murder — a distinction with genuine legal meaning, reflecting the jury's assessment of intent — but most people regard dragging a police officer to death behind a car as conduct that warrants more than half of a thirteen-year sentence. When the government initially warned it would not be possible to exclude "one or two offenders" from the scheme, it was stating a logical truth about the consequences of principled rule-making. Burnham then did what politicians under public pressure usually do: he changed the rule. The result is not a principled exception but a bespoke carve-out for a politically visible case, followed by the broader exemption of all manslaughter convictions — a significant category change that will have cascading effects on the overall scheme.

The foreign prisoner deportation plan — described as covering "hundreds" rather than the 10,134 total — illustrates the same pattern. There is a strong case on rule-of-law grounds for deporting foreign national prisoners after sentence, and successive governments have struggled to do it at scale because of practical barriers: lack of returns agreements with key countries, legal challenges under human rights law, and the operational complexity of coordinating removals. The Burnham government has not announced any new returns agreements; the numbers being discussed suggest a marginal contribution to the capacity problem, not a solution.

The parallel with the 2019 Conservative government's string of sentencing reforms — expansions followed by caps followed by further expansions — is uncomfortable for a Labour prime minister. Britain has been cycling through the same prison capacity ratchet for thirty years. Governments announce tough sentencing to signal seriousness on crime, then release prisoners early to manage the resulting overcrowding, then face public outcry over particular cases, then announce new exemptions. The Conservative shadow justice secretary's call for Burnham to bring forward "emergency primary legislation" rather than making changes by ministerial announcement reflects a legitimate concern about parliamentary scrutiny — and recalls the similar use of secondary legislation by Conservative predecessors to avoid difficult Commons votes on justice.

The IPP abolition — burying a genuine injustice within an announcement designed to project toughness — is the one component of Sunday's package with clear moral legitimacy. The IPP regime was introduced by the Blair government in 2005, abolished by the coalition in 2012, and has spent fourteen years slowly releasing the courts' worst cases while holding hundreds of people beyond their tariff with no clear path to release. Burnham is right to end it. The cross-party oversight group he proposed for managing the remaining 856 high-risk IPP prisoners is a sensible safeguard. It should have happened years ago.

## What to watch

- The October 1 start date for the early release scheme: whether the logistics of separating those newly excluded from those due for release can actually be managed in time.
- The Justice Secretary's parliamentary statement on Tuesday, which will give more precise figures for how many people the exemptions affect and how much additional capacity is actually available.
- Legal challenges to the IPP release programme from victims' groups or the Parole Board's own risk assessments — the oversight group's credibility will depend on whether its decisions are genuinely cross-party or merely political cover.
- Prison population figures over winter 2026-27: if the capacity problem worsens despite the scheme, the government will face pressure to revisit the scheme's scope again — potentially in an even more politically charged environment heading toward a general election.
