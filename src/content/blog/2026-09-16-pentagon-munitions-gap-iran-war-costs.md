The United States Pentagon's inspector general has [confirmed that the war against Iran has produced significant munitions shortfalls](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9gk58xgng0vo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), finding a bottleneck in resupply chains despite President Trump's public claims that US military supplies are "virtually inexhaustible." The report lands in the same week that [two sailors went missing after a tanker was attacked in the Strait of Hormuz](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3n07ydqdzlvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), with Iran claiming the vessel caught fire after striking mines while the US says it was hit by a missile followed by a drone strike. The inspector general finding is significant precisely because it breaks with the administration's official posture: it is an internal government audit, not an opposition talking point or a media inference. Separately, [Republican congressman Thomas Massie has introduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/g-s1-143407/massie-impeach-hegseth-iran) over the conduct of the Iran war, marking a rare but telling break within GOP ranks.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream case for the Iran intervention rests on two pillars. First, Iran was too close to a nuclear weapon to tolerate — the window for military action was closing, and a nuclear Iran posed an existential risk to the regional order and to Israel. Second, American airpower is uniquely capable of degrading Iranian military infrastructure in ways that no other country could replicate, making US involvement not just justified but indispensable. This framing has dominated in Washington's foreign policy establishment from the Atlantic Council to the pages of *Foreign Affairs*. Hawks would add that a show of weakness in the Middle East would only embolden China's posture toward Taiwan.

There is substance here. Iran's nuclear programme genuinely threatened regional stability. The Abraham Accords architecture — painstakingly assembled across two administrations — depended on a credible deterrent against Iranian adventurism. Nobody serious argues that a nuclear-armed Iran would be preferable to a non-nuclear one.

## A different read

But the inspector general report exposes something that the interventionist framework systematically ignores: wars have resource costs that do not pause to wait for the next crisis.

The United States has now confirmed, through its own internal auditing machinery, that a shooting war in the Persian Gulf has depleted munitions stocks at a rate that outpaces replenishment capacity. This is not a theoretical concern. [The Hormuz tanker attack](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3n07ydqdzlvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — with two sailors still missing — is a reminder that the conflict has not ended; it has entered a phase of attritional pressure, which is exactly the kind of warfare that punishes power-projection militaries most severely. The US defence industrial base was already under strain from weapons transfers to Ukraine. The Iran campaign has added a second simultaneous draw on the same limited production lines.

Students of American strategic history will recall that the post-Cold War "peace dividend" consensus — which prevailed from 1991 through the early 2000s — gutted the procurement pipelines that sustained large-scale conventional warfare. The industrial logic of just-in-time supply chains that transformed commercial manufacturing cannot be applied to munitions without catastrophic vulnerability in prolonged conflict. Senator McCain and a handful of defence hawks warned of exactly this problem through the Obama and Trump-I years; they were largely ignored. We are now living in the world their warnings described.

The Massie impeachment move is easy to dismiss as libertarian grandstanding, and in procedural terms it will go nowhere. But it voices something that a significant portion of the American right genuinely believes: that the Trump administration's Iran operation was launched without adequate strategic planning for what comes after the initial airstrikes, and that the men and women of the US armed forces are now absorbing the consequences of that improvisation. [Montana farmers are already reeling from the trade-war retaliatory tariffs](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5964930/canada-tariffs-hurting-montana-farmers) that Trump's broader foreign policy confrontations have triggered. The Iran war is not an isolated enterprise; it is one node in an interconnected set of commitments whose total cost has never been honestly presented to the American public.

The fiscal dimension is pointed. [US borrowing costs have hit their highest level since 2007](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw804154z90ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), with the 10-year Treasury yield at historic highs. Running simultaneous trade wars, military operations, domestic spending programmes, and an increasingly exposed supply chain for munitions, all against a backdrop of rising debt costs, is not a recipe for sustained strategic power. It is a recipe for the kind of imperial overstretch that Paul Kennedy anatomised in *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers* — a book written in 1987 that reads more presciently with each passing year.

The right lesson is not isolationism. The lesson is that hard power requires industrial and fiscal foundations, and that governments which allow those foundations to erode while making open-ended military commitments are not acting strategically; they are improvising. An honest accounting of the Iran war's costs — in munitions, in treasure, in diplomatic capital — is the beginning of an honest debate about what America can and cannot sustain.

## What to watch

- The inspector general's full report: the public summary suggests there are findings more damaging than what has been released, and congressional pressure for the full text will mount.
- The Hormuz situation: any further tanker attacks or Iranian escalatory moves will test whether the US has the munitions to sustain a higher operational tempo.
- The Massie impeachment articles in the House: the vote count on any procedural motion will be a real-time indicator of how deep GOP discontent with Hegseth runs.
- US defence industrial base investment announcements: watch for emergency procurement authorisations that would signal the administration privately acknowledges the shortfall it publicly denies.
