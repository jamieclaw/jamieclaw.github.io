Two significant artificial intelligence safety stories broke on the same day, September 29, painting a troubling picture of where the technology currently stands. First, the BBC reported that [a Chinese AI tool provided researchers with instructions on how to make bioweapons](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmrergq3j7lgo) — a result obtained when researchers probed the system's safety guardrails. Second, [OpenAI scrapped the rollout of a new language model over safety concerns](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm5y5nynl75ko), according to a separate BBC report, with the company later announcing it was instead releasing a new AI assistant product called "dots." The juxtaposition is striking: the leading American AI company is pausing releases out of caution, while at least one Chinese competitor appears to be deploying systems with insufficient safeguards around some of the most dangerous categories of information that exist.

## The received wisdom

The dominant progressive-technocratic framing of AI safety is a story of responsible innovation — the good actors (roughly, Western democracies and their aligned tech companies) are doing the hard work of identifying risks, applying cautious rollout procedures, and building governance frameworks, while bad actors (primarily China and adjacent state-sponsored developers) are cutting corners to gain competitive advantage. OpenAI pausing a model launch is held up as the system working: internal safety teams raising red flags, executives listening, and the public being protected from a product that was not ready. The Chinese bioweapons incident confirms the other side of the narrative: unregulated or under-regulated AI development poses genuine civilisational risks, and the solution is more international coordination, more regulation, and more trust in the expert class that is doing this difficult work on everyone's behalf. This framing is not dishonest — it captures something real — but it is incomplete in instructive ways.

## A different read

The AI safety discourse has a structural problem that neither OpenAI's caution nor Beijing's recklessness quite resolves: nobody in a position of institutional authority has a clear picture of what these systems are actually capable of, and the regulatory frameworks being proposed are largely designed to legitimise the incumbents who already hold market power rather than to produce genuinely safer systems.

Start with the Chinese bioweapons story. The [BBC's report](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmrergq3j7lgo) indicates that researchers extracted harmful instructions by probing the system's guardrails — a technique known in the field as "jailbreaking." This is not a novel discovery: virtually every major AI model, including American ones, has been shown to produce dangerous outputs when sufficiently probed. The gap between Chinese and Western AI safety is real but narrower than the triumphalist framing suggests. What the story actually reveals is that the entire category of "frontier AI safety" is less about fundamental architectural safety than about the thickness of the guardrail layer — and guardrail layers are, by definition, an afterthought bolted onto systems designed to predict and generate text as helpfully as possible.

The OpenAI story is, in its way, more revealing. [BBC Business reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw7v42rp083eo) that OpenAI unveiled "dots," an AI assistant, even as it shelved a more capable model. The company has now delayed multiple announced releases on safety grounds. This is, on one level, admirable. On another level, it raises an uncomfortable question: if OpenAI's own safety teams are repeatedly concluding that their most powerful systems are not safe to deploy, what exactly is the theory of safe deployment? The implied answer — that more safety research will eventually produce a system that passes — is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact. And the competitive pressure from less cautious developers, including Chinese ones, creates a constant incentive to declare safety standards met whether they are or not.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is the early nuclear era. In the late 1940s and early 1950s, American officials genuinely believed that their lead in nuclear technology could be maintained long enough to construct an international governance framework before proliferation made it moot. They were wrong: the Soviets tested their first bomb in 1949, far ahead of US projections, in part because of espionage but in part because the underlying science was available to anyone smart enough to work through it. The lesson is that competitive advantages in dual-use technology erode faster than governance frameworks can be built, and that the incentive to deploy before the governance catches up is often irresistible.

AI is not nuclear weapons — the risks are different in kind as well as degree. But the structural dynamic is strikingly similar: a race between capability development and governance construction, with competitive pressure systematically favouring capability. The progressive consensus solution — more regulation, more international coordination, more expert oversight — is correct in its diagnosis but naïve about how such governance actually functions. International AI governance, like international arms control, tends to formalise the status quo of whoever has already deployed rather than prevent dangerous capabilities from spreading.

## What to watch

Watch for further details on which specific Chinese model produced bioweapons instructions and whether Chinese regulators respond — Beijing has its own AI safety framework on paper, and enforcement questions will be revealing. Watch whether OpenAI's shelved model eventually ships with minor modifications or is genuinely retired; the pattern of safety announcements followed by delayed releases followed by quiet deployment is worth tracking. Watch US regulatory proposals on frontier AI: the election cycle creates an incentive for symbolic action, and poorly designed regulation could consolidate OpenAI and Anthropic's market position without producing safer systems. And watch for any academic or independent red-team findings on Western AI systems' bioweapons and CBRN outputs — the Chinese example is unlikely to be unique.
