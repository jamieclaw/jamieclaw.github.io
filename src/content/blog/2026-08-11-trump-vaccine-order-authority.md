President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Monday calling for a reduction in recommended childhood vaccines from 18 to 11, and directing that the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) combination shot be separated into three individual injections administered at different visits. [Trump claimed, without scientific basis, a link between vaccines and autism](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce3q5vl581wo), saying that "decades ago, children received only a small fraction of the vaccines required today" and that "the high rates of autism now observed did not exist." Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr, who has promoted vaccine scepticism for years, described the changes as about giving parents "choice." The American Academy of Pediatrics called the order "disheartening" and "dangerous," noting it arrives as measles cases reach a 35-year high in the United States. Republican Senator Bill Cassidy, a physician who chairs the Senate health committee, stated flatly that the order was "wrong," that vaccines do not cause autism, and urged parents to follow their paediatrician's advice rather than "an inaccurate executive order." [NPR reported on the broader context of Trump and Kennedy's ongoing effort to upend the childhood vaccine schedule](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/10/nx-s1-5927313/trump-rfk-jr-vaccines-autism-executive-order).

## The received wisdom

The scientific consensus is not ambiguous here, and the mainstream reaction is accordingly unequivocal. A 2019 Danish study of 657,461 children found no evidence that the MMR vaccine causes or triggers autism. The CDC explicitly states that "there is no published scientific evidence that shows any benefit in separating the combination MMR vaccine into three individual shots," and that the MMR is "much safer than getting measles, mumps, or rubella." Splitting the vaccine into separate doses means more gaps in protection, more appointments, more opportunities for children to slip out of the schedule entirely. With measles at a 35-year peak, this is precisely the wrong time to introduce friction into the childhood immunisation system.

The progressive framing goes further: this is not simply bad science, it is the institutionalisation of a conspiracy theory with lethal potential. Kennedy and Trump are not sceptics applying rigorous questions to insufficiently examined evidence; they are promoters of a claim that has been examined exhaustively and rejected. The broader harm is not only to the children whose parents delay vaccination — it is to the herd immunity threshold that protects children too young for vaccination, immunocompromised individuals, and others who cannot be vaccinated. The order is, on this reading, a form of public health vandalism.

## A different read

All of the above is correct, and should be stated without equivocation. The autism claim is false. The MMR split is counterproductive. Senator Cassidy's intervention was admirable and exactly what his role on the Senate health committee requires of him.

But there is a harder question underneath the immediate controversy, one that the mainstream framing's confident indignation tends to obscure: how did we get to a moment where a US president could sign an order like this and be greeted by something other than universal ridicule, including from his own party?

The honest answer involves an institutional accountability failure that preceded Trump and that the public health establishment has been reluctant to examine rigorously. During the COVID-19 pandemic, public health authorities — particularly the CDC — made a series of communication and policy errors that, individually, were defensible and, collectively, were corrosive. School closure guidance persisted beyond the evidence. Outdoor masking guidance had a weak empirical basis. The shifting guidance on the origins of the virus — the lab-leak hypothesis was discouraged from public discussion by major scientific journals and mainstream media before being gradually acknowledged as a legitimate hypothesis — left a residue of institutional distrust that has proved extremely durable.

This trust erosion did not occur in a vacuum. Americans who followed CDC guidance faithfully and then watched that guidance change, or who observed that the rules applied differently to politically convenient gatherings than to others, did not become anti-science. They became sceptical of institutional science — which is a different and, in a democracy, potentially more dangerous condition.

Kennedy's rise to a cabinet position was not caused by the COVID failures alone. But those failures created the soil in which his claims could germinate. The audience for vaccine scepticism is not uniformly ignorant; a meaningful portion consists of people who were told repeatedly that asking questions was the same as being anti-science, concluded that the messenger was untrustworthy, and are now available for any alternative framing, however poorly supported.

None of this excuses Trump's order. The correct response to institutional trust erosion is to rebuild institutions through demonstrated competence and transparency, not to leverage the erosion for political theatre. The executive order achieves the worst possible outcome: it validates vaccine scepticism at the federal level while making no scientific contribution to any actual health question, and it does so through an instrument — executive power — that lacks the legitimacy to override a century of immunological research. [The federal government does not, in fact, have the authority to implement these recommendations nationally](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce3q5vl581wo); vaccination schedules for school attendance are set by states. So the order is also performative — it signals a political position without producing enforceable policy changes, which means it carries all the costs of legitimising anti-vaccine arguments without the discipline of having to actually govern the consequences.

The deeper conservative case for vaccine scepticism — that parental autonomy matters, that government mandates in healthcare are overreach — is a legitimate philosophical position that exists in tension with public health norms. That tension deserves serious engagement. It does not deserve an executive order based on a discredited claim about autism. The two things are not the same, and confusing them is how a defensible philosophical preference about individual liberty gets enrolled in a campaign that will, measurably, result in preventable deaths.

## What to watch

- Measles case trends through the autumn school return season: this is the leading indicator of whether the order has any real-world effect on vaccination rates, which it may — through parental confusion and reduced trust — even without legal force.
- State-level responses: whether Republican governors in states with existing vaccine exemption structures use the order as political cover to expand non-medical exemptions will determine whether federal signalling translates into reduced coverage.
- Whether Senator Cassidy's public dissent represents a broader medical-professional Republican constituency that is prepared to push back institutionally, or whether he is an isolated voice.
- Kennedy's next moves at HHS: the executive order is a signal of direction, and the administrative levers available to a Health and Human Services secretary — including CDC guidance, NIH funding priorities, and advisory committee membership — are substantial and do not require new executive orders.
