South Korea announced it will [not send military forces to support the United States' ongoing war on Iran](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/18/south-korea-will-not-send-military-to-support-us-war-on-iran-defying-trump-pressure), directly defying pressure from the Trump administration to contribute to coalition operations in the Persian Gulf. Seoul cited constitutional constraints on overseas military deployments — South Korean law requires National Assembly approval for foreign combat operations — but the political signal was clear: the Yoon successor government in Seoul, already navigating enormous pressure from a domestic opposition that controls the legislature, was not willing to risk the National Assembly rejection that any military commitment to an unpopular Middle Eastern conflict would almost certainly produce. The announcement lands at a delicate moment in US-Korea relations, with negotiations over burden-sharing on the Korean peninsula — the cost of stationing 28,500 American troops in South Korea — already fraught.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and liberal internationalist reading of Seoul's refusal is approving: South Korea is exercising appropriate democratic sovereignty, declining to participate in a war that the UN has flagged as raising serious legal questions, and signalling to Washington that alliance obligations do not extend to open-ended support for American military adventurism. The left-wing corollary is that Trump's pressure tactics — essentially telling allies to contribute troops or face consequences in bilateral negotiations — represent the weaponisation of America's security umbrella, turning a mutual defence arrangement into a transactional protection racket. On this view, Seoul is right to resist, and other US allies in the Asia-Pacific should take note. Japan, Australia, and the Philippines, all of whom have also quietly declined to contribute to Gulf operations, are making the same calculation: that being seen as co-belligerents in the Iran war creates domestic political problems and regional diplomatic risks that no degree of American gratitude could offset.

This critique of Trump's alliance management has real force. But the implied conclusion — that Seoul's refusal is cost-free, or even admirable — understates the structural consequences.

## A different read

The US-Korea alliance is among the most concrete and operationally significant defence relationships in the world. It exists because South Korea faces an existential military threat from North Korea — a nuclear-armed, heavily militarised state that has demonstrated both the intention and the capability to attack the South. The 28,500 American troops on the Korean peninsula are not a symbolic gesture; they are a deterrence guarantee that has kept the peninsula at uneasy peace since 1953. Seoul's ability to decline American requests elsewhere rests, implicitly, on this guarantee. The question worth asking is whether that guarantee is as robust as Seoul's confidence in it assumes.

Trump's transactionalism is well documented. He has [repeatedly expressed scepticism about the value of stationing troops in South Korea](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c63d7lexyym1o), framing the alliance as a subsidy to a wealthy country that should be paying its own defence bill. His first-term negotiations on the Special Measures Agreement — the cost-sharing deal — were famously brutal, with American negotiators demanding fivefold increases in South Korean contributions. The current burden-sharing negotiations are happening against the same background. When Seoul publicly refuses a direct American request for military cooperation, it does not improve its negotiating position in those talks; it weakens it, by demonstrating that Washington cannot assume automatic allied support.

There is also a broader strategic coherence problem. The United States' stated purpose in the Middle East — restraining Iranian power, protecting Gulf energy flows, maintaining freedom of navigation — intersects directly with South Korea's economic interests. South Korea is a major importer of Gulf oil; [disruption to Gulf shipping has economic consequences for Seoul](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq0m3gmv8n7ko) that Seoul's government tends to elide in its public messaging. A South Korea that benefits from a stable Gulf order but declines to contribute to its maintenance is free-riding in a manner that is difficult to justify on any consistent principle — constitutional constraints notwithstanding.

None of this means Seoul should have sent troops. The constitutional argument is real, and a democratic government that cannot get parliamentary approval for military action has good reason not to seek it. But there is a difference between declining due to genuine domestic legal constraints and framing that declination as a principled stand against American overreach — which is what the South Korean opposition, and much of the international commentary, has been encouraged to do. The former is understandable realpolitik; the latter creates a false template for alliance defection that will be cited by every US ally the next time Washington asks for something difficult.

The right lesson from Seoul's refusal is not that allies should blindly follow American military commitments, but that the Trump administration's management of its alliance network is producing a structural credibility problem: allies are rationally concluding that the costs of visible non-compliance are lower than Trump's rhetoric suggests, while the guarantees of visible compliance are less reliable than the institutional assurances imply. That is a genuinely dangerous equilibrium, not a healthy assertion of sovereign independence.

## What to watch

**Burden-sharing negotiations outcome.** Whether the US extracts punitive terms from South Korea in the next cost-sharing agreement will indicate how seriously Washington is willing to follow through on implied threats.

**North Korea's reading.** Pyongyang watches American alliance reliability very closely. Any perception that the US-Korea alliance is fraying benefits Kim Jong-un's deterrence posture and emboldens North Korean nuclear and missile programmes.

**Japan's posture.** Tokyo is watching Seoul's defiance closely. If it is unpunished, Japan may feel more latitude to resist American pressure on its own military deployments. If it produces visible consequences, the lesson runs the other way.

**Congressional reaction.** Whether Republican hawks push back on Trump's alliance management, or whether the transactional approach becomes entrenched party doctrine, matters for the long-term durability of the Indo-Pacific security architecture.
