After years of dominating Serbian politics through a combination of media control, economic patronage, and nationalist appeal, Aleksandar Vučić has lost the presidency to a coalition led by the student protest movement that has roiled the country for the better part of two years. [BBC News reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw5yn3xlprpmo) scenes of celebration in Belgrade, with protesters who had occupied public squares and blocked roads finally seeing their persistence rewarded at the ballot box. [Al Jazeera's analysis](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/26/what-vucics-defeat-means-for-serbias-foreign-policy) notes that the result carries significant implications for Serbia's foreign policy, which under Vučić had maintained an uncomfortable balancing act between the EU accession process and close ties with Russia and China. The [Guardian's coverage](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/26/serbia-student-list-preparing-for-talks-on-forming-government-despite-no-clear-leader) reports that the Student List movement is already preparing for government formation talks, though it lacks a clear single leader — a fact that could prove either a democratic virtue or a governing liability.

## The received wisdom

International liberal commentary has greeted Vučić's defeat with something close to jubilation. The narrative is of a brave civic movement — young, idealistic, photographically compelling — overcoming an entrenched autocrat who had slowly strangled press freedom, captured the judiciary, and managed elections through a combination of administrative advantage and media monopoly. Within this frame, the result is presented as evidence that populist authoritarianism is fragile, that civil society can organise even under pressure, and that the European democratic model retains its pull even in countries that have drifted toward a more managed form of politics. The EU institutions have broadly welcomed the outcome, and there is a real sense in Brussels that Serbian accession talks could be reinvigorated.

## A different read

The jubilation is understandable. Vučić did represent something genuinely corrosive to democratic norms, and his removal through electoral means rather than street revolution is exactly the kind of orderly democratic transition that those who care about institutions should want to see. So far, so good.

But the harder question is what comes next — and history counsels caution. Eastern Europe's post-1989 experience is littered with democratic breakthroughs that proved shallower than they appeared in the moment of triumph. Poland's Solidarity movement produced a democratic revolution in 1989, yet the same society that carried Walesa to power eventually produced the Law and Justice party's systematic judicial capture a generation later. Ukraine's Orange Revolution in 2004 produced not stable liberal democracy but a decade of oligarchic dysfunction before Maidan. The student movements that drove these transitions were genuine expressions of democratic aspiration; what they lacked was the institutional infrastructure — independent courts, professional civil service, a pluralist media ecosystem — to sustain the values they articulated.

Serbia presents particular complications. Vučić spent years hollowing out precisely those institutions. [The Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/26/serbia-student-list-preparing-for-talks-on-forming-government-despite-no-clear-leader) that the victorious Student List has no clear leader, which raises a genuine question: governing a country of seven million people, managing a complex relationship with the EU while Russia presses from one side and China invests from another, requires more than civic energy. It requires people who understand how public administration actually works — and the Vučić system deliberately degraded that knowledge by filling positions with loyalists rather than technocrats.

There is also the foreign policy question. [Al Jazeera notes](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/26/what-vucics-defeat-means-for-serbias-foreign-policy) that Vučić's "all four pillars" foreign policy — maintaining ties with the EU, US, Russia, and China simultaneously — was incoherent in principle but arguably functional in practice, giving Serbia economic options that more ideologically committed governments lacked. A new government that moves decisively toward Euro-Atlantic integration will face Russian pressure on energy, Chinese pressure on infrastructure debt, and potentially renewed instability in Kosovo, where Vučić's transactional approach had at least kept a lid on the worst violence.

None of this should dampen the real democratic achievement. But those who genuinely want Serbia to succeed should be preparing to help it build the unglamorous institutional scaffolding that makes democratic government actually work — not simply applauding the election result and moving on.

## What to watch

- **Coalition negotiations**: whether the Student List movement can construct a governing coalition with experienced parties, or attempts to govern with its moral authority as a substitute for institutional knowledge.
- **Judicial reform**: Vučić's captured courts remain in place. Whether the new government has a coherent strategy for restoring judicial independence — without simply replacing one form of capture with another — will be decisive.
- **Kosovo**: any change in Serbian government creates uncertainty about the fragile modus vivendi in the north of Kosovo, and the new leadership's approach to Pristina will be closely watched.
- **Russia's response**: Moscow will not welcome the result. Watch for energy pricing adjustments, disinformation campaigns, and pressure through Serbian Orthodox Church networks.
