Chinese authorities have [launched an investigation into Chen Jining](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/25/china-investigates-politburo-chen-jining-xi-jinping-purge), a Politburo member widely regarded as a rising star in the party hierarchy, in what observers are describing as Xi Jinping's latest purge of potential rivals or dissenters within the senior leadership. Chen, who served as party secretary of Shanghai and had been considered a possible candidate for even higher office, is the latest in a series of senior officials — including former defence ministers and technology regulators — to fall in a disciplinary process that the party frames as anti-corruption enforcement. Separately, a [leading British lawyer](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/25/china-misusing-interpol-target-dissidents-uk-lawyer) has accused Beijing of systematically misusing Interpol red notices to pursue dissidents abroad, an allegation that adds an international dimension to the picture of a party increasingly willing to use its institutional reach to settle scores beyond its own borders.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western liberal critique of Xi's anti-corruption campaign is that it functions primarily as a political purge rather than a genuine effort at institutional reform. This reading has much evidence in its favour. The targets of investigation have frequently been figures with ties to competing factions — former supporters of the Shanghai clique, allies of the Jiang Zemin era, and technocrats who might have provided the basis for a more collective form of governance — rather than the systematically corrupt officials embedded throughout the party's lower levels. The campaign has not resulted in structural reforms to the incentive systems that produce corruption in the first instance: the opacity of local government finances, the lack of independent audit, the absence of a free press to perform the accountability function that formal institutions cannot. Critics from [Minxin Pei at Claremont McKenna](https://www.amazon.com/Chinas-Crony-Capitalism-Dynamics-Corrupt/dp/0674737598) to Dali Yang at the University of Chicago have argued that personalised anti-corruption drives without institutional change are inherently self-limiting — they discipline rivals but do not change the underlying system.

## A different read

What is worth examining more carefully is not whether Xi's consolidation of power is good for China — it almost certainly is not, by any liberal-democratic standard — but what it reveals about the structural logic of Leninist party-states and what that portends for how China will behave externally.

The most important thing Chen Jining's investigation tells us is that Xi has not finished consolidating. The 20th Party Congress in 2022 appeared to complete his dominance, with allies placed in every major position and the two-term convention on presidential tenure formally abolished. Yet the purges continue. This is not surprising: in systems where power is personal rather than institutional, the consolidation is never complete, because rivals and their networks can reconstitute around any surviving figure of standing. The Soviet Politburo went through the same cycle, as did the CCP itself in the Mao era. The logic of the one-man state demands perpetual vigilance, which translates into perpetual purges, which in turn demands ever-tighter control of information — including, apparently, the use of international law enforcement infrastructure to pursue critics abroad.

The Interpol misuse allegation is significant precisely because it illustrates the extraterritorial character of Xi's governance model. A state confident in its own institutional legitimacy does not need to chase critics through Interpol notices; that behaviour suggests a regime that understands its own legitimacy as fragile and treats external criticism as an existential threat rather than a manageable annoyance. This has direct implications for Western liberal democracies, which have been slow to build the institutional defences — in academia, in finance, in the legal system — against the kind of transnational authoritarian influence the Interpol allegation represents. The United Kingdom, in particular, has been repeatedly identified by parliamentary intelligence committees as a preferred venue for illicit Chinese state financial and legal operations.

There is a foreign-policy corollary that tends to get lost in the domestic governance analysis. As [Hal Brands and Michael Beckley](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/china/2021-06-22/danger-zone) have argued, a regime at peak power consolidation and facing growing domestic legitimacy questions is not necessarily a more cautious or status-quo actor externally. The historical pattern — from Wilhelmine Germany to the Soviet Union under Brezhnev — is that internal consolidation sometimes precedes external adventurism as a displacement activity and a source of nationalist legitimation. Xi's Taiwan posture, his assertiveness in the South China Sea, and his refusal to constrain Russia in Ukraine should be read in this domestic-political context, not purely as strategic calculation.

The question for Western policymakers is whether this pattern of perpetual purge represents a stable equilibrium or whether it accumulates the institutional fragility — the destruction of competent second-tier officials, the chilling effect on honest advice, the atrophying of genuine expertise in favour of loyalty — that historically precedes systemic failure. The answer matters enormously for how the West should calibrate its China policy: a genuinely weakening party-state requires different responses than a consolidating one.

## What to watch

Watch whether Chen Jining's investigation results in a formal expulsion from the party and prosecution — the full "double designation" (shuang gui) process — or whether a quieter demotion signals that his removal was more about neutralising a rival than a full disciplinary example. Watch Beijing's response to the Interpol allegations: a denial is routine; an actual reform of its red-notice practice would be historically unprecedented and would signal that external reputational pressure has some purchase. Watch the Shanghai political network that Chen represented — whether its surviving members are progressively neutralised will indicate whether this is a targeted removal or the opening of a wider sweep. And watch Xi's public appearances and policy statements in December: the rhythm of internal consolidation often precedes major external policy moves.
