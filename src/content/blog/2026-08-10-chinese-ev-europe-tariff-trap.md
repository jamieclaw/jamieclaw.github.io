Chinese electric vehicle brands captured [14.2% of western European battery electric vehicle sales](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/09/chinese-electric-car-sales-surge-to-a-record-high-in-europe) — one in every seven BEVs sold — in the first five months of 2026, according to Schmidt Automotive Research, up nearly five percentage points from the same period in 2025. Brands including BYD, Chery, SAIC, and Xpeng recorded 171,800 sales across the 18 largest Western European markets. The United Kingdom accounts for a quarter of those sales, having declined to follow the EU's supplementary tariffs — which reach as high as 35.3% for some Chinese manufacturers on top of the standard 10% duty. Italy, meanwhile, experienced an anomalous spike driven by Leapmotor's T03 model exploiting an Italian government purchase subsidy, at points reducing the car's effective price to as low as €5,000. Chinese brands have simultaneously launched more than 120 different models in Europe — compared with roughly 100 from European manufacturers. Tesla, by contrast, saw a 60% year-on-year rebound in European sales after its steep 2025 decline.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and industry consensus reads this as a call for stronger protective measures. European carmakers are under "intense pressure" as tighter emissions rules force them to accelerate their own EV transitions while Chinese brands, backed by state subsidies, undercut them on price. The EU's existing tariffs have clearly been insufficient — market share has risen *despite* them. The response should be higher tariffs, expanded to cover plug-in hybrids (PHEVs) as well as pure battery vehicles, closing what researcher Matthias Schmidt calls the current "gap in the door." Volkswagen CEO Oliver Blume has already called for PHEV tariffs, noting European PHEV models are uncompetitive against Chinese equivalents. The framing is industrial strategy: Europe must protect its manufacturing base and workers while building competitive domestic capacity.

The case is not unreasonable. State-subsidised dumping is a real phenomenon, and European automakers employing millions of workers deserve a level playing field.

## A different read

But the protection-first response carries serious unexamined costs — and the dynamics revealed in this data are more complicated than the headline suggests.

The first complication is that the tariff policy is demonstrably porous by design. Schmidt's observation that Chinese manufacturers are already pivoting to PHEVs — which *are not* currently covered by EU supplementary tariffs — because of their calculation that "they will prioritise PHEVs over the next 12 months given hybrids are omitted from extra tariffs placed on BEVs only" suggests that the EU's regulatory boundary is a tactical one, not a strategic one. Chinese manufacturers are not being stopped; they are being rerouted into the tariff gap. The response — extend tariffs to PHEVs, which the EU is reportedly considering — is permanently one step behind.

The second complication is distributional. European consumers are buying Chinese EVs in record numbers because they are cheaper and, apparently, good enough. The UK's position — no supplementary tariffs — reflects a real calculation: lower-income consumers benefit from affordable EVs, and forcing them to pay more to protect French and German factory jobs is a regressive choice. Protectionism always transfers costs from protected workers to consumers, and in an EV transition where the goal is mass-market adoption, those consumer price increases directly slow decarbonisation. This is not an argument against any industrial policy — it is an argument that the trade-offs should be made explicitly and honestly.

The third complication is geopolitical and harder still. The EU's dependence on Chinese battery supply chains — the same issue that has prompted the US to impose [a 15% tariff on polysilicon](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cdrvn686dljo), a key chip and solar material where China holds near-monopoly production — cannot be addressed by auto tariffs alone. Chinese EV penetration in Europe is a symptom of a broader strategic condition: China's industrial policy, operating over thirty years with state direction and patient capital, has achieved dominance in the supply chains of the energy transition. Tariffs delay the moment of reckoning; they do not resolve it.

The structural parallel is the European solar panel industry. In 2012, the EU imposed anti-dumping tariffs on Chinese solar panels. Within five years, the European solar panel manufacturing industry had essentially ceased to exist anyway — not because the tariffs failed, but because China's production cost advantage was so large, and their technology quality so competitive, that European manufacturers could not close the gap in the window the tariffs bought. The question is whether European auto manufacturers, with their engineering base and brand heritage, can avoid the same fate. The honest answer is: possibly, but not by tariffs alone.

What is actually needed is the thing European industrial policy has historically been reluctant to do: direct, at-scale public investment in competitive capacity — battery gigafactories, rare-earth processing, R&D at the frontier — combined with a serious conversation about which segments of the auto industry Europe realistically wants to compete in globally versus which it is willing to concede. That requires hard choices that tariffs defer rather than resolve.

## What to watch

- Whether the EU moves to extend supplementary tariffs to PHEVs, and on what timeline — this is the immediate battleground
- BYD's progress on its EU production facility in Hungary, which would allow it to sell locally-made cars that avoid import tariffs entirely
- The UK's review of its tariff position: a quarter of Chinese European EV sales flowing through the UK suggests mounting political pressure from domestic carmakers
- Chinese countermeasures: Beijing has already signalled willingness to respond to auto tariffs with restrictions on European goods, adding another dimension to any escalation
