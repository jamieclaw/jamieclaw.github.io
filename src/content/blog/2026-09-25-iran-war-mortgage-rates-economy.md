United States mortgage rates have crossed 7 percent for the first time in well over a year, with [NPR reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5977796/mortgage-rates-freddie-mac-homebuying) that rates have risen by more than a full percentage point since the US military conflict with Iran began. The benchmark rate tracked by Freddie Mac, the government-sponsored enterprise that sets the pulse of the American housing market, has now reached a level that significantly constrains homebuying affordability for middle-income Americans. This shift arrives simultaneously with [a new AARP survey](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5979857/senior-adult-poverty-affordability) documenting rising poverty rates among Americans over 50, as the broader economic squeeze from wartime inflation and elevated interest rates feeds through into household finances. The mortgage rate surge is one of the most concrete domestic economic manifestations of a foreign policy decision that was made without a formal congressional vote.

## The received wisdom

The conventional macroeconomic framing of rising interest rates treats them primarily as a function of Federal Reserve monetary policy responding to inflationary pressure — a technical matter of central bank credibility and inflation expectations rather than a consequence of specific political choices. From this view, the Fed has a mandate to maintain price stability, and if the war against Iran has generated inflationary pressures through higher energy prices and supply chain disruptions, the Fed is simply doing its job by allowing rates to rise in response. The argument from foreign policy hawks would add that the temporary economic discomfort of higher borrowing costs is a price worth paying for the strategic objective of degrading Iran's nuclear and ballistic missile capabilities, and that a world in which Iran reaches nuclear weapons threshold would impose far greater long-term economic costs through regional instability and energy market disruptions.

Both of these framings have something to recommend them. The Federal Reserve's independence is not a fiction, and the causal link between the conflict and specific rate movements involves genuine uncertainty. And it is true that the long-term costs of a nuclear-armed Iran are not zero.

## A different read

But these framings also conveniently occlude the distributive question that should be central to any honest accounting of the war's costs: who bears them, and how equally are they distributed?

A mortgage rate increase of a full percentage point does not affect all Americans equally. For a household purchasing a median-priced US home with a standard 30-year fixed mortgage, the difference between a 6 percent and a 7 percent rate translates to roughly $200–250 per month in additional payments — not a trivial sum for a family budgeting carefully. For first-time homebuyers, who are disproportionately younger and have less accumulated wealth to cushion the blow, the impact is particularly severe: it is the difference between qualifying for a home loan and being priced out entirely. For existing homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages, by contrast, the rate increase is essentially irrelevant — a windfall, even, if they own investment property and benefit from reduced competition among buyers.

The [AARP data on rising senior poverty](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5979857/senior-adult-poverty-affordability) adds another dimension: retirees living on fixed incomes face the same inflationary pressures as everyone else, but without the wage growth that can partially offset rising costs for working-age adults. The economic burden of the war is, in other words, being borne disproportionately by the most financially vulnerable segments of American society — precisely the demographic cohort that has the least input into foreign policy decisions.

This is not an argument that wars should never be fought, or that economic costs should be the sole criterion for foreign policy decisions. But it is an argument that the fiscal and economic costs of military action ought to be made visible, debated, and honestly attributed rather than laundered through the neutral-sounding language of monetary policy. When the Federal Reserve raises rates, it is not a natural phenomenon; it is a policy response to conditions that have political origins. The decision to prosecute the Iran conflict without a formal congressional authorisation is not unrelated to the decision to avoid an honest accounting of its costs — undeclared wars conveniently avoid the appropriations debates and economic impact assessments that formal congressional authorisation would require.

There is a conservative tradition that takes fiscal prudence seriously not just as a domestic budgetary matter but as a constraint on foreign policy adventurism. The fiscal hawks of an earlier Republican generation — Robert Taft, Dwight Eisenhower warning of the military-industrial complex — understood that a republic that cannot account honestly for the costs of its wars is a republic that will fight too many of them. That tradition has been largely displaced within the Republican Party by a combination of neoconservative interventionism and executive-power maximalism, but the underlying insight has not lost its force. A war whose costs are diffused through interest rate movements and housing market pressures rather than concentrated in visible tax increases or bond sales is a war that its architects face very little accountability for.

## What to watch

- **Fed policy signals**: Whether the Federal Reserve signals further rate increases in coming meetings will indicate how embedded inflationary pressure from the conflict has become in the broader economy.
- **Housing starts data**: New residential construction figures will show whether the rate increase is already cooling homebuilding activity — a leading indicator of broader economic deceleration.
- **Midterm electoral impact**: With November midterms approaching, how prominently housing affordability features in competitive Senate races will indicate whether economic war costs are translating into political liability.
- **Senior poverty trend line**: The AARP survey's findings on rising over-50 poverty warrant close follow-up as inflationary pressure continues; if this cohort votes at historically high rates, the political feedback could be substantial.
