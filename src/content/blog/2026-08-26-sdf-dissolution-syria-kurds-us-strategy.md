Mazloum Abdi, commander of the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF), [announced Tuesday evening](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/25/kurdish-led-forces-in-syria-dissolve-after-merging-with-the-army) in a televised speech at the presidential palace in Damascus that the SDF had completed its dissolution as an independent military force and merged into the Syrian national army. "We announce today the end of the mission of the Syrian Democratic Forces and declare, with full responsibility, their dissolution as an independent military force," Abdi said, in remarks broadcast on state media. The SDF once governed a de facto autonomous zone across northeastern Syria — territory seized from the Islamic State during the US-backed military campaign that cost the Kurdish forces roughly eleven thousand fighters over several years. The dissolution completes a process that began earlier this year when the new Damascus government, led by interim President Ahmed al-Sharaa, launched an offensive that stripped the SDF of most of its northeastern territory. In exchange for the merger, al-Sharaa reportedly recognised the right to Kurdish-language education in Syrian schools. US Special Envoy Tom Barrack [praised Trump's "far-sighted leadership"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/25/kurdish-led-forces-in-syria-dissolve-after-merging-with-the-army) for opening the path to the SDF's integration into Syrian state institutions.

## The received wisdom

The dominant progressive reading of this development is cautiously positive, framed through the lens of Syrian national reconciliation. The argument goes: the SDF's autonomous northeast was always an unstable arrangement — a de facto statelet dependent on US military presence that had no long-term constitutional legitimacy and that neighbouring Turkey treated as an existential threat. Better, on this reading, to have a unified Syrian army under a government that the US has now moved to legitimate — Washington removed Syria from its terrorism sponsors list on Monday, the day before the SDF announcement — than a permanent partition that would have invited Turkish military intervention or renewed civil war. The language that al-Sharaa has used about "a new Syria for all its people — the Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen and Assyrians" is the language of inclusive state-building. If the Kurdish language in education is genuinely implemented, that represents a concrete cultural guarantee that no previous Syrian government had offered.

## A different read

The problem with the reconciliation narrative is the gap between the ceremony in Damascus and the balance of coercive power that preceded it. The SDF did not merge into the Syrian army after a negotiated political settlement; it merged after the government launched a military offensive this January that stripped the Kurds of most of their territory. That is capitulation dressed in the language of partnership. Abdi spoke of "turning an important page in Syria's history" — but pages turned under military duress tend to be written in ink that favours the victor.

The deeper issue concerns American strategic credibility. The SDF was the [primary US partner in the fight against ISIS](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/25/kurdish-led-forces-in-syria-dissolve-after-merging-with-the-army), bearing the bulk of the ground casualties in a campaign that the Obama and first-term Trump administrations treated as a core national security objective. The United States provided the SDF with weapons, training, air support and — crucially — the implicit promise that their political interests would be protected in any post-war settlement. What they received instead was progressive abandonment: Trump's first-term withdrawal announcement in 2019, the post-Assad pivot to al-Sharaa's government, and now the quiet endorsement of a merger on the Damascus government's terms. US Envoy Barrack's statement congratulating Trump's "far-sighted leadership" for this outcome is striking precisely because it treats the dissolution of America's most effective Arab military partner as a diplomatic success.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is the fate of South Vietnamese forces after the 1973 Paris Agreement. The US obtained a ceasefire agreement that it portrayed as "peace with honour"; its Vietnamese partners obtained two years before collapse. The comparison is imperfect — the SDF is not being annihilated, and Syria is not Vietnam — but the structural dynamic is similar: a smaller partner accepts paper guarantees because the alternative is to fight alone, and the great power accepts those paper guarantees because it allows an exit from an open-ended commitment. Whether the guarantee of Kurdish-language education proves durable is a question al-Sharaa's government will answer over years, not in a speech at the presidential palace.

There is also a geopolitical dividend for Turkey, which has spent years pressuring the US to abandon the Kurds. Ankara's insistence that the SDF was inseparable from the PKK — the Kurdish independence movement that Turkey designates as a terrorist organisation — was always a distortion, but a politically potent one. The merger gives Turkey exactly what it wanted without Turkey having to make any concession in return. That is not a good precedent for the reliability of US security assurances elsewhere in the region.

## What to watch

- **Kurdish-language education implementation**: Whether al-Sharaa's government actually introduces Kurdish instruction in schools within the current year, as promised, will be the clearest near-term test of whether the merger guarantees are real.
- **Former SDF commanders in government**: Abdi and senior Kurdish political figure Ilham Ahmed were reportedly promised "substantive roles" in the Syrian government — their actual appointments and portfolio scope will signal whether integration is genuine or ceremonial.
- **Remaining ISIS detention capacity**: The SDF maintained dozens of detention facilities holding ISIS fighters and their families. Who controls those facilities now, and under what legal framework, is a serious counter-terrorism question.
- **Turkish military posture**: Ankara's response to the merger will determine whether Turkey pulls back its forces from northern Syria or uses the new arrangement as cover to pursue remaining PKK-linked targets.
