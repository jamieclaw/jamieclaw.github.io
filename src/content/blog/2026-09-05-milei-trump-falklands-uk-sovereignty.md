Argentine President Javier Milei declared on 4 September 2026 that the "winds of change" now favour Argentina's claim to the Falkland Islands, [citing suggestions from Donald Trump that the US might reconsider its traditional support for British sovereignty](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/04/president-javier-milei-winds-of-change-support-argentina-claim-to-falkland-islands). Milei also threatened faster sanctions against British oil companies operating in Falklands waters. The UK government responded the same day: Ed Miliband stated Britain's position remained [unwavering](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/sep/04/uk-ministers-hit-back-argentinian-falklands-claim), and Downing Street echoed that UK support for the islands was not open for negotiation. Trump, meanwhile, has [suggested he might support Milei's drive](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/04/trump-argentina-falklands-uk) to reclaim sovereignty, framing it partly as favour to his Argentinian political acolyte and partly as a vehicle for "retribution" against a UK he perceives as insufficiently aligned. The episode has the surreal quality of a historical grievance being reopened by a real-estate developer who has reportedly never fully understood why Britain fought a war over what he once described as "rocks in the South Atlantic."

## The received wisdom

The immediate British political response — firm, bipartisan, unremarkable — reflects the comfortable assumption that the Falklands question was settled by the 1982 war and subsequent democratic choices by the islanders themselves. The progressive internationalist framing takes a somewhat different line: that Milei is a useful canary for the broader pathology of right-wing populism, that Trump is being characteristically provocative, and that neither should be taken entirely literally. The BBC's coverage is appropriately sober. The Guardian frames the episode principally as a story about Trump's "retribution" impulses and his willingness to weaponise foreign policy for domestic and personal purposes, which is not wrong.

## A different read

The deeper problem is not Milei's rhetoric, which has always been flamboyant, nor even Trump's trolling of the UK government, which has become a predictable feature of American foreign policy under his second term. The deeper problem is what it reveals about the structural condition of British foreign policy after the Iran war and after the slow erosion of the special relationship that has characterised the past year.

Britain is simultaneously waging an energy war on the home front — [UK petrol prices are at their highest level since the Iran conflict began](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20zgjzz0e4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — managing a new PM's first weeks in office, dealing with a bond market that is pricing UK sovereign risk at levels not seen since 1998, and now discovering that its most important bilateral relationship provides no reliable deterrence against a third-party territorial challenge. This is the Suez analogy that British commentators reach for but rarely fully articulate. In 1956, Britain discovered that American support for its global interests was conditional, transactional, and capable of being withdrawn at the most embarrassing moment. The lesson drawn — that Britain's security depended on maintaining Washington's goodwill at nearly any cost — produced a foreign policy of permanent deference that has, for seventy years, largely held.

What Trump's second term has demonstrated is that this deference no longer purchases the security guarantees it once did. The US president is now sufficiently post-ideological, sufficiently unconstrained by the Atlanticist establishment that staffed previous Republican administrations, and sufficiently motivated by personal relationships (Milei being a close ideological ally) that the basic Falklands commitment — which every US administration since 1982 has maintained in some form — is now genuinely uncertain. The [BBC's reporting on Argentine oil sanctions threats](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyk18g1l8ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) notes that Milei has leveraged this uncertainty to threaten faster action against British energy interests in the South Atlantic.

For Britain, the uncomfortable conclusion is that the Falklands' security ultimately rests not on American backing but on British capability. The 1982 war was won with a task force that no longer exists in anything like its 1982 form. The Royal Navy's surface fleet is smaller than at any point since the Napoleonic era. The garrison on the islands and the air defence capability provided by the Typhoon detachment are substantial, but the logic of deterrence requires that any potential aggressor believe the cost of attack exceeds the benefit. If Washington is now genuinely ambiguous, and if Buenos Aires reads that ambiguity as an operational green light, the deterrence calculation has shifted.

The right's instinct — national sovereignty, strong defence, reliable alliances — is sound. The failure has been in delivering the defence expenditure and industrial base that makes national sovereignty more than a slogan. Britain cannot defend the Falklands on sentiment alone.

## What to watch

- Whether the Trump administration makes any formal statement clarifying (or not clarifying) US policy on Falklands sovereignty — silence is itself a signal.
- Argentine parliamentary and military movements: Milei's rhetoric needs domestic political support and constitutional authorisation for any escalatory action. Watch Buenos Aires.
- UK defence budget signals in the Burnham government's first Budget — if the Royal Navy's surface fleet and the South Atlantic garrison are not explicitly protected, the deterrence gap will widen.
- The response of other US allies, particularly Australia and Canada, who are watching whether the Washington compact on territorial integrity for democratic allies is still operative.
