The International Atomic Energy Agency's Board of Governors voted this week to refer Iran to the United Nations Security Council for nuclear non-compliance, with the resolution initiated by the United States, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom. The BBC [reported Iran's response](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckgwj4kl5lno) as a condemnation of the move, with Tehran blaming US and Israeli strikes on its nuclear facilities for disrupting IAEA inspections and thus making compliance monitoring impossible. Al Jazeera [described the referral](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/9/iaea-board-resolves-to-report-iran-to-security-council-over-nuclear-program) as a "symbolic escalation" initiated by the four Western powers. The IAEA referral mechanism has been used before — most notably in 2006, before the framework that eventually produced the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action — and carries a mixed record of producing meaningful restraint.

## The received wisdom

The case for the IAEA referral is rooted in institutional integrity. The Non-Proliferation Treaty system exists precisely for moments like this: when a state that is a signatory to the NPT is found to be failing its obligations, there is a graduated escalation ladder that eventually reaches the Security Council. Using that ladder is not warmongering — it is the multilateral rules-based order functioning as designed. If the West abandons the referral mechanism because it fears Iranian anger or Russian and Chinese vetoes, it signals that the NPT has no enforcement spine. The referral puts Iran formally on notice, creates a legal record, and gives the international community a documented basis for further action. For defenders of the liberal international order, this is exactly what "going through proper channels" looks like.

This is a genuine and defensible position. The referral mechanism exists for a reason, and failing to use it would itself be a signal — of impotence rather than restraint.

## A different read

The honest question is: what happens next? Because the Security Council is where multilateral processes go to be vetoed.

Russia and China, both permanent members with veto power, have no strategic interest in sanctioning Iran more severely than they already have. Russia's relationship with Iran has deepened considerably since the Ukraine war began; China continues to buy Iranian oil at discount prices that are, in effect, subsidised by the same Western sanctions regime that is supposed to be squeezing Tehran. A Security Council referral that produces a resolution Russia and China vote down, or that results in a toothless statement, does not strengthen the NPT — it demonstrates its weakness. The 2006 referral eventually produced three rounds of Security Council sanctions, but only because the political constellation of that moment — before the full deterioration of US-Russia and US-China relations — was more permissive. That constellation no longer exists.

There is also a deeper structural problem that Tehran's defenders have, not entirely in bad faith, identified: the IAEA's ability to conduct inspections has been compromised by military strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities. This is not a defence of Iranian opacity, which predates the current war by decades. But it does create a genuine epistemological problem for the referral process. If inspectors cannot access the sites, the "non-compliance" finding rests partly on inference and intelligence rather than on direct verification — which gives Iran rhetorical cover and gives Russia and China procedural ammunition to challenge the referral's evidentiary basis.

The conservative foreign policy tradition — or at least its realist strand — has always been somewhat sceptical of multilateral institutions as enforcement mechanisms when the underlying great-power consensus has broken down. Henry Kissinger's famous remark that the United Nations is useful as a venue for diplomacy but not as a substitute for it captures the relevant scepticism. The IAEA referral is not useless: it matters for the historical record, for the willingness of other states to maintain IAEA obligations, and for domestic political audiences in the US and Europe who need to see institutions being used. But the gap between what the referral symbolises and what it can actually accomplish — in a world where the two states most capable of enforcing additional pressure are instead economically deepening their ties with Tehran — is wide.

North Korea is the cautionary tale here. It was referred to the Security Council, faced sanctions, developed nuclear weapons anyway, and is now [operating a two-storey uranium enrichment facility](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy0zqk2w92lo) according to the IAEA — a fact that the same institution reported with what the BBC described as "serious concern" this week. The concern is warranted. The question is whether "serious concern" combined with Security Council referrals constitutes a policy.

What the West actually possesses, beyond the institutional machinery, is the oil embargo — and the evidence of this week's $100 barrel suggests that it is the tanker strikes, not the Security Council resolutions, that have the most immediate effect on Iranian behaviour and Iranian revenues. Whether that effect is sustainable, strategic, and produces the desired outcome is a separate and harder question.

## What to watch

- **Russian and Chinese response at the Security Council**: Whether they veto outright, abstain, or negotiate a softened resolution will reveal the current state of great-power alignment on the Iran file.
- **IAEA access to Iranian sites**: Any resumption or further restriction of inspector access will determine the evidentiary basis for future proceedings.
- **Iranian uranium enrichment pace**: IAEA monitoring data, to whatever extent it remains available, on enrichment levels near the weapons-grade threshold — this is the actual proliferation clock.
- **Parallel with North Korea precedent**: Watch whether Iran draws publicly on the North Korean model as a template for surviving multilateral pressure while maintaining a latent nuclear capability.
