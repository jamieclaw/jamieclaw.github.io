The United States Department of Justice [charged Russian agents Wednesday](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143590/us-justice-department-russian-operatives-attacks) with plotting attacks both inside the United States and against Ukrainian allies abroad, with the indictment describing the defendants as belonging to "one arm of the Russian Federation's apparatus used to carry out external attacks worldwide." The BBC simultaneously reported that the [US has formally accused Russia of attempting to kill Ukrainian allies overseas](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjp3077led9qo), a charge that, if fully substantiated, represents a significant escalation in Russian intelligence operations targeting the West. The indictment and the diplomatic accusation arrive as [Congress passes a Russia sanctions bill](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/nx-s1-5971289/russia-sanctions-house-vote-lindsey-graham) — named for the late Senator Lindsey Graham — that would give the President authority to impose tariffs on major importers of Russian oil and gas, including China and India. The convergence of these three developments marks a significant intensification of the formal US-Russia confrontation beyond the battlefield in Ukraine.

## The received wisdom

The progressive-realist critique of aggressive US posturing toward Russia runs roughly as follows: by expanding NATO, flooding Ukraine with weapons, and now imposing extraterritorial sanctions on countries that trade with Russia, the United States is pushing a nuclear-armed power into a corner in ways that increase rather than reduce the risk of catastrophic escalation. On this view, the indictment of Russian agents — while legally proper — is more useful as a domestic political signal than as a substantive deterrent; Moscow is not going to be deterred by American court filings, and the exercise risks being mistaken for weakness dressed up as rule-of-law activism. The sanctions bill, targeting Chinese and Indian imports of Russian energy, is read on this view as a form of secondary sanctions overreach that will damage US relationships with two critical Indo-Pacific partners without meaningfully constraining Russian behaviour.

There is a legitimate point buried in this critique. The history of American indictments of foreign state actors — from North Korean hackers to Iranian IRGC members — demonstrates that criminal charges rarely produce the defendants in an American courtroom or modify adversary behaviour directly. The symbolic dimension of indictments can give the appearance of action while substituting for the harder work of building actual deterrent consequences.

## A different read

But the "indictments are just symbolism" critique is incomplete in a way that matters. The DOJ's charges, according to [NPR's reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/g-s1-143590/us-justice-department-russian-operatives-attacks), describe a network that is "one arm of the Russian Federation's apparatus used to carry out external attacks worldwide" — language that attributes the conspiracy directly to the state, not to rogue actors or plausibly deniable freelancers. This attribution matters for the legal and political record. It forecloses the diplomatic option of treating Russian assassination and sabotage campaigns as aberrations that a good-faith diplomatic engagement could address; it formally classifies them as state policy. That reclassification has downstream consequences: it affects how allies respond, how financial institutions calibrate risk, and how future administrations justify whatever measures they take.

The [British accusation that Russia has been targeting Ukrainian allies overseas](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjp3077led9qo) connects to a pattern that has been visible but under-discussed in Western policy circles. The attempted assassination of Sergei Skripal in Salisbury in 2018, the poisoning of Alexei Navalny in 2020, the [suspected sabotage of Netherlands rail infrastructure](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8ly49w9g1edo) reported earlier this week — these are not isolated incidents. They are components of a doctrine that Western intelligence services have long described as "hybrid warfare": the use of coercive tools that sit below the threshold of conventional military attack and therefore below the threshold of NATO Article 5 response. The strategy is rational from a Russian perspective precisely because it imposes costs on adversaries while avoiding the symmetrical response that an open military attack would trigger.

The historical reference point is the Soviet "active measures" campaign of the Cold War, which combined disinformation, political subversion, and selective violence to weaken Western cohesion without inviting direct military confrontation. The current Russian campaign is operating in a similar framework but with updated tools — cyber attacks, hired contractors, cryptocurrency-funded networks — and in a more permissive environment created by the degradation of Western counterintelligence capacity after 2001, when resources rotated toward counterterrorism.

The Lindsey Graham sanctions bill is a different kind of instrument. By [giving the President authority to impose tariffs on countries that import Russian oil and gas](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/nx-s1-5971289/russia-sanctions-house-vote-lindsey-graham) — specifically naming China and India — Congress is attempting to weaponise secondary sanctions in a way that could genuinely constrain Russian export revenues. The critique that this damages relationships with Beijing and Delhi is real, but it should be weighed against the alternative: a Russian oil export market that continues to fund the war machine, insulated from Western financial pressure by the willingness of large non-Western economies to absorb sanctioned Russian supply at a discount. There is no costless option here. The question is not whether to impose costs but on whom.

## What to watch

Watch whether any of the indicted Russian operatives are in jurisdictions with extradition treaties — the legal record is more consequential when there is at least a theoretical path to enforcement. Watch how China and India respond to the passage of the sanctions bill — their diplomatic reactions will reveal how seriously they take the tariff threat. Watch the [Zelenskyy-Trump UN meeting](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/16/efforts-underway-for-zelenskyy-trump-meeting-at-un-next-week) for signals about whether the US is coordinating its Russia pressure campaign across diplomatic and legal channels simultaneously. And watch whether the Netherlands rail sabotage investigation produces a formal attribution — a European ally formally accusing Russia of infrastructure sabotage would be a significant escalatory data point.
