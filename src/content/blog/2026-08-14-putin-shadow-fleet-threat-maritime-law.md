The MT Smyrtos, a tanker carrying Russian oil in violation of Western sanctions, was boarded by Royal Marine Commandos in the English Channel in June and has since been anchored off Weymouth. This week its detention triggered an unusually direct threat from Vladimir Putin. Speaking while overseeing Russian Pacific Fleet drills, Putin [warned Western countries](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn0npn249qvo) that if plans to seize more shadow-fleet vessels were enacted, Russia would "respond in kind — and not necessarily in the waters where raids on our ships and vessels are planned, but wherever we deem it necessary and appropriate, anywhere." Viktor Liina, commander of Russia's Pacific Fleet, added that Russia had "the capacity to inspect and detain vessels of unfriendly states." The UK government defended the seizure as fully lawful; the EU's foreign affairs spokesperson said European nations would not be deterred. The Smyrtos's captain has been charged with breaching sanctions.

## The received wisdom

The dominant framing is reassuring: Putin's bluster is mostly noise. The West is operating well within international maritime law — indeed, the UK MoD explicitly notes that boarding vessels suspected of sanctions violations is permissible under the same legal frameworks Russia claims to respect. Russia's shadow fleet, [more than 700 vessels by MoD estimates, responsible for carrying 75% of Moscow's sanctioned oil](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn0npn249qvo), is the actual lawbreaker here. More than 630 shadow-fleet vessels have been sanctioned by the EU as of mid-August, and since the start of 2026 nine have been seized across European waters. The West has economic leverage, legal cover, and alliance coherence on its side. Putin's threats are the predictable tantrum of a state caught cheating; the right response is to calmly continue enforcement.

There is substance to this reading. The legal architecture of sanctions enforcement does give Western navies considerable latitude when a vessel is suspected of carrying sanctioned cargo, particularly when flying a flag of convenience and operating in international or territorial waters with consent. The Smyrtos's captain has been charged under UK domestic law, not some invented jurisdiction.

## A different read

And yet the confident dismissal of Putin's threat warrants more careful consideration than it is currently receiving.

The shadow fleet is not merely a sanctions-evasion mechanism. It is, increasingly, a strategic asset that Russia has spent four years building with patient, sophisticated effort. The fleet uses obscure ownership structures across multiple jurisdictions — often Cyprus, UAE, or Pacific island flag registries — to make attribution and liability murky. The vessels are mostly old, cheaply insured, and crewed by nationals from states that have not joined Western sanctions. Russia has invested considerable diplomatic energy in maintaining relationships with those states precisely because it anticipated the pressure it is now under.

The pivot that matters is Putin's Pacific framing. He issued this threat not from Moscow but while inspecting naval drills in the Pacific — simultaneously visiting the disputed Kuril Islands, a pointed reminder to Japan that Russia is not only a European problem. The subtext is deliberate: Russia is positioning itself as a revisionist power with global maritime reach, not merely a land army bogged down in Ukraine. Whether that reach is real is a separate question; the signalling is unmistakable.

The historical precedent worth reaching for is not a comfortable one. In the 1980s, the tanker wars in the Persian Gulf produced a dynamic in which great-power competition steadily eroded the informal rules that had kept commercial shipping relatively safe. The 1987 re-flagging of Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag drew the US Navy into active convoy escort operations and eventually produced the USS Vincennes incident. Tit-for-tat maritime confrontations have a way of escalating past the point that the original logic seemed to justify.

None of this means the West should back down from enforcing sanctions — the financial pressure on the Kremlin is one of the few levers available, and allowing Russia to circumvent it freely would be both a strategic and a moral failure. But "we have the legal authority therefore we should proceed without limit" is not a strategy. The West needs a clearer answer to several questions it has not yet publicly addressed: What happens if Russia actually detains a Western commercial vessel in, say, the Red Sea or off the coast of a non-aligned state? What is the escalation ladder, and at what point does it intersect with the already complex Iran-war naval picture? And who is coordinating the legal, diplomatic, and military components of shadow-fleet enforcement across the fifteen-plus Western states now participating in it?

The EU has sanctioned 630 vessels; nine have been seized this year. That pace will accelerate pressure on Russian oil revenues — which is precisely why Putin is reacting. The question is whether Western capitals have thought through what happens at the end of that escalation path as clearly as they have thought through the legal basis at the beginning of it.

## What to watch

Watch whether Russia attempts even a symbolic detention of a Western-linked commercial vessel — a small one in an amenable jurisdiction would be a proportionate probe of Western resolve. Watch the Italian and French navies, which have been [the most active boarders](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn0npn249qvo) in Mediterranean waters, for any change of tempo after Putin's threat. Watch the price of Urals crude: sustained enforcement pressure should show up in a widening discount to Brent as buyers price in higher transport costs and legal risk. And watch the G7 finance ministers, who have periodically discussed tightening the $60-per-barrel oil price cap — closing that gap would do more economic damage to the Kremlin than any single tanker seizure.
