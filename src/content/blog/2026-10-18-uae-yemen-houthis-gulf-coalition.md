The United Arab Emirates has reached an agreement with Saudi Arabia to re-enter the Yemen conflict as Houthi forces continue to make territorial gains in the north of the country. [Egypt's President el-Sisi simultaneously pledged to defend Saudi Arabia from Houthi attacks](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/10/17/10-17-egypt-saudi-houths-clip), and a Saudi-led coalition reported intercepting multiple drone strikes. The developments come as Iranian television aired footage purportedly showing a tanker explosion near the Strait of Hormuz, underscoring the widening geographical scope of the conflict. The UAE had largely withdrawn from active combat operations in Yemen in 2019, pivoting to a proxy-heavy approach focused on southern separatists and port control, but the Houthi advance appears to have convinced Abu Dhabi that disengagement is no longer sustainable. Regional analysts noted that Egypt's public commitment represents a significant escalation in the coalition's political weight.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian case against deepening Gulf military involvement in Yemen is substantial and should be heard. The war since 2015 has produced one of the world's worst humanitarian catastrophes: [a conflict that has killed hundreds of thousands of people](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/17/saudi-led-coalition-intercepts-drones-as-egypt-weighs-sending-help), displaced millions, and left large parts of the country dependent on international aid. Critics on both left and right have argued that Saudi Arabia's air campaign in particular — with its high civilian toll and targeting of infrastructure — made the Houthis stronger, not weaker, by providing a narrative of foreign aggression that sustained their recruitment and popular legitimacy. The Obama administration's limited support for the coalition drew condemnation from human rights organisations; the Biden pause on offensive weapons sales was a recognition that the policy was not working. On this reading, the UAE's return and Egypt's pledge are simply more combustible material thrown onto a fire that should be being extinguished, not stoked.

## A different read

This framing, however sympathetic, misreads the structural logic of what has changed since 2019 and particularly since the Red Sea attacks of 2023 and 2024. The Houthis are no longer simply a Yemeni insurgency fighting to control territory in a failed state. They have demonstrated the capacity to strike Riyadh's international airport repeatedly, to disrupt global shipping lanes in ways that raised insurance premiums for containers from Asia to Europe, and to function as a genuine strategic asset for Iran's broader regional project. [The Saudi coalition's interception of drone strikes](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/10/17/iranian-tv-airs-video-said-to-show-tanker-explosion-in-hormuz) is a defensive necessity, not an aggressive choice.

The UAE's original 2019 withdrawal was strategic prudence under different circumstances. Abu Dhabi had achieved its core objectives — securing the port of Aden, establishing a foothold for its preferred Yemeni factions in the south, and limiting its own casualty exposure — and calculated that further engagement produced diminishing returns. That calculus has now changed. Houthi territorial expansion in the north of Yemen gives Iran's proxies greater strategic depth and removes the internal constraints that geography previously imposed on their offensive reach. An expanded Houthi-controlled territory is not merely a humanitarian problem; it is a forward base for ballistic missile and drone launches against Saudi population centres and Gulf shipping.

Egypt's commitment deserves particular attention. Cairo under el-Sisi has been, in strategic terms, primarily focused on domestic consolidation, the Libyan frontier, and the Ethiopian dam dispute. For Egypt to publicly pledge its forces in defence of Saudi Arabia against Houthi attacks is a significant political signal — it suggests that Riyadh has been making the case in Arab capitals that the current trajectory is existential, not merely inconvenient. Egypt also has unique leverage: its population and military size give any coalition it joins a different character, and its historical involvement in Yemen during the 1960s under Nasser — a costly and ultimately failed intervention — means Cairo's generals do not enter this lightly.

The Iran dimension cannot be overlooked. Tehran has been the strategic architect of the Houthi capability build-up: providing drone technology, missile components, intelligence support, and political direction. The question the Gulf coalition is now implicitly answering is whether Iran can continue to wage a low-cost, high-impact proxy war that keeps its own fingerprints legally ambiguous while its proxies extract enormous costs from a rich neighbour. The answer that Riyadh and Abu Dhabi appear to have reached is: not indefinitely. There comes a point at which strategic deterrence requires demonstrating that the cost of proxy aggression will rise faster than Iran's willingness to absorb it.

That is the right general principle, even if its application carries genuine risks. Military re-escalation in Yemen risks accelerating the humanitarian disaster already in progress. It risks a cycle of escalation that draws in Iranian forces more directly. And it risks further entanglement in a conflict whose political resolution — some form of power-sharing arrangement that gives the Houthis a role in a post-war Yemen — cannot ultimately be achieved through military means alone. None of these objections are wrong. But the alternative — accepting a Houthi-dominated Yemen as the permanent strategic landscape — has costs that the Gulf states, and by extension the global shipping economy, cannot absorb indefinitely.

## What to watch

Watch whether Iran responds to the re-entered coalition by escalating Houthi operations or by signalling through diplomatic back-channels a willingness to negotiate limits. Watch the volume and targeting of Houthi drone operations against [Saudi and Emirati territory](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/17/uae-yemen-conflict-agreement-saudi-arabia-houthi-gains) in the next two to four weeks — the pattern will reveal whether Tehran views the coalition's re-engagement as a serious deterrent or a manageable escalation. Watch for any US response: the Trump administration's approach to the Houthis has been inconsistently hawkish, and whether Washington formally backs the coalition's renewed campaign will determine its international legitimacy. And watch the UN Security Council — China and Russia's veto positions will shape whether any meaningful international humanitarian mechanism can be established alongside the military campaign.
