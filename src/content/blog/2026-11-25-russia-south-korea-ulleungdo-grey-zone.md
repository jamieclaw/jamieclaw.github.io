On Tuesday morning, South Korea's interior ministry issued an emergency shelter-in-place alert to the approximately 8,700 residents of Ulleungdo, a volcanic island in the Sea of Japan roughly 120 kilometres off the Korean peninsula's east coast. The alert was triggered after [several Russian military aircraft entered South Korea's air defence identification zone](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/south-korea-tells-island-residents-to-take-shelter-after-russian-military-aircraft-approach) and approached Ulleungdo to the point where a violation of its sovereign airspace was anticipated. The joint chiefs of staff said South Korean air force jets were scrambled and that the Russian aircraft departed before actually entering the island's airspace. The alert, issued at 7.52am, was lifted three minutes later at 7.55am. Television broadcasts were interrupted; sirens sounded on the island; at least one elderly resident telephoned neighbours asking whether war had started. South Korea announced it would summon the Russian defence attaché to lodge a formal protest. A second provincial message, sent in error, described the aircraft as "presumed to be North Korean" — the province had mistakenly used wording from a routine drill template, changed only the time. It was the fourth known entry of Russian military aircraft into the zone this year and the second in a week.

## The received wisdom

Western analysts and Seoul's foreign policy establishment will read this incident as a continuation of Russia's deliberate pressure campaign against Indo-Pacific democracies allied with the United States — a signal of displeasure at South Korea's weapons transfers to Ukraine, delivered through a form of provocation carefully calibrated to fall below the threshold of a casus belli. South Korea began providing artillery shells to Ukraine in 2025, reversing its earlier policy of not supplying lethal aid to either side of an active conflict; Moscow reacted with threats, and military incursions into the air defence identification zone have become a recurring instrument of pressure. In this framing, the incident is a reminder that Russia's war has long outgrown Ukraine's borders, and that democracies in every region should expect continued testing of their resolve.

## A different read

The standard framing is correct as far as it goes. But it understates an important structural feature of these incidents: the air defence identification zone is not sovereign airspace. Russia formally disputes the zone's legal basis and has never recognised Seoul's authority to administer it. An air defence identification zone is a unilaterally declared buffer that extends a country's radar horizon and early-warning capability; entering it without prior notification violates customary practice but does not, in itself, violate international law in the way that entering sovereign airspace would.

This ambiguity is not incidental — it is the whole point. Grey-zone operations are designed to impose costs and signal resolve while remaining below the threshold that obligates a formal response. Russia [used similar tactics](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/24/south-korea-tells-island-residents-to-take-shelter-after-russian-military-aircraft-approach) throughout the Ukraine conflict's earlier phases — energy cut-offs, cyberattacks, disinformation campaigns — to pressure neighbouring states without crossing lines that would trigger Article 5 of the NATO charter or equivalent alliance obligations. The South Korea incidents follow the same logic: they are expensive for Seoul politically and psychologically without creating the clear legal trigger that would justify a formal military response.

According to data submitted to the South Korean parliament, approximately 60 Russian entries without prior notification were recorded in the five years to 2024, compared with roughly 430 by Chinese aircraft over the same period. The Chinese figure is worth pausing on: China is conducting a significantly more intensive version of the same grey-zone air campaign, and it has attracted considerably less alarm in Western commentary. The inconsistency reflects a broader tendency to frame Russia as the acute threat and China as a long-term strategic concern, when the patterns of behaviour are in many respects parallel and the Chinese campaign operates at greater scale. Seoul's defence ministry has summoned the Russian attaché, as it has before: in March 2025, after eight Russian entries in ten days. The strategic effect of these summons is limited.

For conservative commentators who are sceptical of internationalist institutions and prefer realist frameworks, this incident offers an uncomfortable lesson. Alliance structures like the US-South Korea mutual defence treaty, and the extended deterrence that underpins them, are tested not primarily by outright military attack but by exactly this kind of grinding, legally ambiguous provocation. Responding to grey-zone operations requires sustained political will, credible military posture, and alliance coordination over years and decades — not dramatic single responses. It is precisely the kind of patient, institutionalised strategic behaviour that populist sceptics of alliance commitments find difficult to sustain. The shelter alert on Ulleungdo lasted three minutes. The strategic challenge it represents will not resolve in anything like that timeframe.

## What to watch

Watch the frequency of Russian incursions in the coming weeks: an acceleration would signal increased pressure tied to South Korea's Ukraine weapons policy, while a pause might indicate the incident was more tactical than strategic. Watch whether Japan, which faces analogous grey-zone pressure from both Russia and China in its own identification zones, coordinates a joint diplomatic response with Seoul. And watch the South Korean domestic debate: a government that is simultaneously navigating pressure from Washington on trade and military burden-sharing, managing a volatile North Korean neighbour, and now confronting renewed Russian provocations is operating under unusual strategic stress.
