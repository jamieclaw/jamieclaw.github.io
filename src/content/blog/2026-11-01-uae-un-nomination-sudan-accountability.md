The [Sudanese diaspora and international human rights organisations have formally denounced the UAE's nomination to lead a major United Nations body](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/30/sudanese-diaspora-human-rights-groups-denounce-uae-nominee-un-top-job), citing Abu Dhabi's documented material support for the Rapid Support Forces — the paramilitary group accused of mass atrocities in Darfur and the siege of El Fasher. The accusation is not merely civil society rhetoric: [the United Kingdom has already imposed sanctions on Sudanese military leaders implicated in El Fasher's campaign of rape and ethnic violence](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/26/uk-sanctions-sudanese-military-leaders-implicated-in-el-fasher-campaign-of), suggesting Western governments have been willing to acknowledge the scale of the atrocities. The UAE, however, has denied supporting the RSF and retains substantial diplomatic backing from Gulf partners, Asian economies dependent on its financial hub status, and Western governments that simultaneously condemn the Sudan war and depend on Emirati cooperation in counterterrorism, Red Sea shipping security, and the Abraham Accords architecture.

## The received wisdom

The liberal internationalist critique here is, at its core, correct: there is something genuinely corrosive about permitting a state accused of fuelling one of the world's worst active humanitarian catastrophes to occupy a position of institutional authority in the multilateral system. The UN's legitimacy depends, at least partly, on the perception that its constituent processes are not simply a projection of great-power interest dressed in humanitarian language. If the UAE nomination succeeds despite documented concerns, it reinforces the cynical reading that international institutions are instrumentalised by wealthy states and that human rights accountability is applied selectively — to adversaries rather than partners. Human rights organisations and diaspora groups are performing exactly the function that civil society is meant to perform in a functioning multilateral order, and their objections deserve to be heard rather than managed.

## A different read

The progressive critique is valid as far as it goes, but it tells a comfortable story that obscures the structural reality of why the UAE nomination is viable and what it would actually take to make it unviable.

The United Arab Emirates is not an international pariah. It is the financial capital of the wider Middle East and South Asia, a logistics hub through which a significant fraction of world trade transits, a partner in the Abraham Accords that represented the most consequential shift in Arab-Israeli relations in a generation, a host state for American military infrastructure, and a country that has, in various contexts, been a useful interlocutor in precisely the regional stabilisation efforts that the same progressive commentators endorse. The Western states that are most vocal about Sudan accountability are also the states most dependent on Emirati cooperation across a dozen other priority files. The British government sanctioned Sudanese military leaders while simultaneously maintaining its defence and intelligence relationship with Abu Dhabi. This is not hypocrisy in the simple sense; it is the reality of statecraft in a multipolar world where moral clarity and strategic necessity rarely align.

The historical precedent that complicates the civil society case is the long record of UN body composition that has, repeatedly, included states with worse human rights records than the UAE — states that used their UN positions not to improve their conduct but simply to obstruct accountability mechanisms for others. The Human Rights Council's membership history is not a story of progressive improvement; it is a story of the most interested parties ensuring their own insulation from scrutiny. If the standard for UN institutional leadership required clean human rights hands, the membership list would be very short.

None of this is an argument that the UAE nomination is desirable. It is an argument that the accountability gap in multilateral institutions is structural, not episodic — and that denouncing individual nominations without addressing the power dynamics that produce them is a form of moral positioning that feels satisfying without changing anything. The Sudanese civilians dying in Darfur are not served by press statements from human rights NGOs. They are served by the combination of sustained diplomatic pressure, credible economic costs imposed on states supporting the RSF, and the kind of regional political settlement that requires — unfortunately — engaging the very Gulf states whose conduct is being criticised.

## What to watch

The vote timeline for the UAE nomination will reveal how much diplomatic capital Abu Dhabi is spending and how many states are willing to absorb the reputational cost of public support. Watch whether any permanent Security Council member breaks with its usual diplomatic discretion to explicitly oppose the nomination — that would signal a genuine political decision to prioritise Sudan accountability over bilateral relationship maintenance. The more telling indicator may be in Khartoum itself: whether the RSF's military position on the El Fasher front changes in the weeks ahead will tell us more about Emirati strategic calculations than any UN vote. Finally, track the Sudanese civilian casualty trajectory: with famine conditions spreading and cholera re-emerging in displacement camps, the human cost is compounding at a rate that will eventually become impossible for Western governments to absorb without a more visible political response. The question is whether the UN nomination controversy can sustain enough diplomatic attention to force a reckoning before that threshold is reached.
