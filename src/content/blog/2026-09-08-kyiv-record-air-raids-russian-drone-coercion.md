Kyiv is suffering more daily air raid alerts than at any point since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. [BBC Verify analysis of Ukrainian government data](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjdvx9pz44ro) shows that in the seven days from August 27, the city endured 76 air raid alerts — surpassing the previous weekly record of roughly 66 set in the invasion's opening days. Since Russia began its round-the-clock campaign at the end of August, air raid sirens have sounded an average of ten times daily, with some days reaching thirteen alerts lasting up to four and a half hours. The escalation is driven in part by a new generation of jet-powered drones flying at nearly 600 kilometres per hour — three times the speed of older propeller-driven Shaheds — which Ukraine's air force is shooting down at a rate of only around 60 percent. Russia launched 2,800 of these fast drones in August alone, nearly double the July figure. The strikes forced disruption to schools, businesses, and commuter transit. There was a brief pause over the weekend while US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner visited Kyiv for talks with President Volodymyr Zelensky.

## The received wisdom

The dominant framing in Western commentary treats Russia's escalating drone campaign primarily as evidence of Moscow's desperation: unable to achieve decisive battlefield gains, Putin is reduced to terrorising civilians in a strategy that reveals strategic bankruptcy. The "terror campaign" narrative emphasises the human cost — students forced from classrooms, rail commuters stranded, workers evacuated from offices — and the moral illegitimacy of targeting civilian infrastructure. The brief pause for Witkoff and Kushner's visit is interpreted as evidence that Moscow still desires an off-ramp, or at least wants to preserve the appearance of diplomatic good faith. The [US envoys described their Kyiv talks as "substantive"](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/06/nx-s1-5959657/us-envoys-witkoff-kushner-talks-in-kyiv-putin-moscow), with Witkoff saying he was "very encouraged." The humanitarian argument is reinforced by the operational one: Ukraine is adapting, the government has designed a two-tier alert system to reduce economic disruption, and Zelensky has ordered emergency production of new interceptor missiles.

The humanitarian framing is correct as a moral description. Deliberately targeting civilian infrastructure to break civilian will has a name in international law. But as a strategic analysis, "Russia is desperate and failing" is increasingly incomplete.

## A different read

The drone campaign represents a sophisticated coercive strategy, not a fumbling last resort. Its logic runs as follows: Ukraine's stock of US-supplied Patriot interceptor missiles is finite and declining. Every Russian drone that forces an alert and an intercept attempt — even one that is shot down — depletes a resource that cannot easily be replenished under current US policy constraints. At ten alerts per day, the math becomes brutal. If Ukraine fires multiple interceptors per alert, and if Russia can sustain 2,800 fast drones per month or more, then by the time winter arrives and Russia shifts back to targeting energy infrastructure — [as Moscow has explicitly threatened](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjdvx9pz44ro) — Ukraine's air defence inventory will be materially weaker than it is today.

The jet-powered drones are the tactical innovation that makes this strategy credible. [Ukraine's air force spokesman Yurii Ihnat told the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjdvx9pz44ro) that intercepting them requires not just speed but specialised guidance systems: "It is not enough for the interceptor simply to fly fast — it has to hit the target." The development timeline for Ukrainian interceptors capable of matching the new Russian drones is measured in months, and Russia's production of the fast drones is accelerating.

The Witkoff-Kushner talks are a thread in this fabric, not a separate story. [NPR reported that the envoys' Moscow meetings lasted more than three hours](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/06/nx-s1-5959657/us-envoys-witkoff-kushner-talks-in-kyiv-putin-moscow), producing no announced breakthrough, with Russian presidential aide Yuri Ushakov characterising them as "constructive and useful" — diplomatic language that means nothing happened. The gap between Moscow's and Kyiv's positions remains what it has always been: Russia demands territorial concessions that Zelensky says are non-negotiable, and Ukraine's continued resistance depends on security guarantees that the Trump administration shows no urgency to provide. Putin, in turn, ordered a 72-hour pause on Kyiv strikes to cover the envoys' visit — a gesture calibrated to demonstrate control and goodwill without conceding anything.

The harder question is whether the resumption of round-the-clock strikes after the envoys departed is a signal that Moscow regards the diplomatic track as a pressure-management exercise rather than a genuine path to settlement. Putin has publicly insisted Russian forces are making battlefield gains and will achieve Russia's "war aims" — language that implies Moscow sees no reason to rush toward an agreement while the attrition campaign continues. The CIA Director's recent secret Moscow visit, [briefly noted by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/06/nx-s1-5959657/us-envoys-witkoff-kushner-talks-in-kyiv-putin-moscow) as suggesting "a broader diplomatic recalculation," remains unexplained, which is its own form of signal.

The winter calculation is the one that should concentrate minds in Washington and European capitals. Ukraine's energy grid was heavily damaged in the winter of 2024-25. [Moscow has said it will seriously damage Ukraine's power grid again this winter](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjdvx9pz44ro). A second consecutive winter of severe infrastructure damage, combined with Kyiv's dwindling Patriot stocks, could create conditions — civilian displacement, industrial shutdown, deepening military exhaustion — that make a forced settlement on Russian terms appear, in some Western capitals, as regrettable but unavoidable. This is presumably what Moscow is banking on. Whether the Trump administration decides to replenish Ukraine's air defence stocks before winter, or uses the moment to press harder for a ceasefire on terms that leave Russia in control of significant Ukrainian territory, is the most consequential decision facing Western policy before the end of the year.

## What to watch

- Whether the US announces additional Patriot interceptor transfers to Ukraine in the next six to eight weeks, or continues to withhold them as leverage in the peace process.
- The speed at which Ukraine can develop and produce its own jet-speed interceptors — [Zelensky has convened emergency meetings with commanders on this](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjdvx9pz44ro), and the timeline will determine whether the winter is manageable.
- The next Witkoff-Kushner round: whether they return to Moscow with concrete proposals or allow the process to drift into a de facto endorsement of continued Russian coercion.
- Front-line developments in the coming weeks — "largely frozen" front lines combined with accelerating Russian air power suggests Moscow may be building toward a coordinated ground-air offensive rather than seeking a ceasefire.
