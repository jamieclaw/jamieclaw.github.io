The United States government has [issued a formal warning to Ford Motor Company](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/8/us-warns-ford-over-ties-with-chinese-firms-amid-tensions?traffic_source=rss) over its business relationships with Chinese firms, amid escalating bilateral tensions, according to Al Jazeera. The warning is part of a broader pattern of American regulatory and security agencies scrutinising corporate links between major US companies and Chinese technology, manufacturing, and supply chain partners. Ford is one of several large multinationals that have deep joint-venture and supplier relationships developed over decades of engagement with the Chinese market — relationships that made compelling commercial sense when US-China relations were governed by the logic of economic interdependence, but which now increasingly look like strategic liabilities from Washington's perspective. The move reflects the continuing evolution of US policy from discouraging new Chinese technology partnerships to actively reviewing and pressuring existing ones.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream business and centre-left view holds that the Ford warning is an example of security imperatives overwhelming economic rationality. Ford's China relationships are commercial, not strategic; the company is a car manufacturer, not a defence contractor. Forcing American multinationals to disentangle from Chinese supply chains and partners will raise costs, reduce competitiveness, and ultimately damage American workers and shareholders without meaningfully improving national security. China is so deeply embedded in global manufacturing supply chains — rare earth minerals, battery components, electronic assembly — that any serious attempt at decoupling is, at best, a decade-long project requiring enormous government subsidy. The warning to Ford is, in this reading, political signalling rather than strategic policy: it satisfies hawkish constituencies while imposing real costs on industry without a realistic plan for what comes next.

## A different read

There is something to this critique, but it somewhat deliberately confuses the costs of decoupling with the costs of the dependence that made decoupling necessary.

Consider the electric vehicle dimension. Ford's Farley administration has been actively pursuing a battery supply chain strategy that involves partnerships with firms connected to Chinese lithium processing and battery cell manufacturing. This is not eccentric behaviour: Chinese companies, particularly CATL and BYD, control a commanding share of global battery manufacturing capacity and have achieved cost advantages that are genuinely difficult to replicate in the near term. Any American automaker trying to compete in the EV market faces intense pressure to access that cost base. Washington is essentially telling Ford: you cannot be competitive in the market we are demanding you enter while using the supply chain that would make you competitive. That is a real tension, and it deserves honest acknowledgment rather than dismissal.

But the security concern is also real, not just rhetorical. The Ukraine war has demonstrated conclusively that supply chain dependencies in strategically sensitive sectors create coercive leverage. Europe's dependence on Russian natural gas was not a secret before 2022 — it was a known, analysed risk that was managed through optimistic assumptions about interdependence as a stabilising force. Those assumptions proved catastrophically wrong when the political context changed. The argument that commercial ties create mutual restraint — the old "Chimerica" thesis advanced most prominently by Niall Ferguson — has not aged well. Commercial interdependence did not prevent the trade war; it provided leverage for it.

The Ford case is particularly revealing because it exposes the gap between US industrial policy rhetoric and industrial policy reality. Washington wants American automakers to lead the electric vehicle transition. It has passed significant legislation — the Inflation Reduction Act's domestic content provisions — designed to incentivise domestic battery manufacturing. But the US battery manufacturing base remains immature compared to China's, and bridging that gap takes years and capital at scales that private markets alone will not supply on the required timeline. In the meantime, American companies are being told to cut ties with the suppliers that would actually help them compete.

This is not an argument against decoupling. The security case for reducing strategic dependencies on an adversarial state is strong. But decoupling without a credible industrial policy to fill the gap — without serious government investment in alternative supply chains, without patient capital for domestic manufacturing scale-up — is not a strategy. It is a posture. The warning to Ford is the posture; the hard work of building American battery and component manufacturing capacity is the strategy, and it is happening too slowly and with too little coordination to justify the pace of pressure being applied to companies.

## What to watch

- Ford's formal response and any indication of which specific Chinese relationships are under scrutiny — the distinction between battery suppliers and technology partners matters for understanding the scope of the concern.
- Congressional action: whether the warning reflects a broader legislative push to restrict American corporate ties with Chinese firms beyond the existing national security review framework.
- Competitor behaviour: if Ford faces serious penalties or is forced to restructure partnerships, how do GM and other US automakers with similar Chinese supply chain exposure respond?
- The Chinese government's reaction: Beijing has tools to retaliate against US companies operating in China, and the degree to which it uses them will indicate whether this is treated as bilateral escalation or managed as a bilateral irritant.
