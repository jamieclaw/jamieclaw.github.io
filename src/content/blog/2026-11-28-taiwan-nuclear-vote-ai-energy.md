Taiwan is preparing to [vote on whether to revive its nuclear power programme](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/27/will-taiwan-vote-to-revive-nuclear-power-and-why-does-that-matter-for-ai), a referendum triggered in part by the staggering energy demands of AI data centres that have put new strain on an electricity grid already under pressure from the island's semiconductor manufacturing base. The question — whether to extend the life of existing plants and potentially build new capacity — arrives at a moment when [China's strategic posture looms large over Taiwan's domestic politics](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/27/threat-from-china-looms-large-over-taiwans-local-elections), adding a national security dimension to what in other democracies would be primarily an environmental and economic debate. Energy independence, in Taiwan's context, is not an abstract virtue; it is a component of the same survivability calculus that shapes defence spending and civil preparedness.

## The received wisdom

The case against nuclear revival is familiar and has genuine force. The 2011 Fukushima disaster — involving an older reactor design in a seismically active country, Japan — shaped the political consensus in Taiwan as it did in Germany and elsewhere. Taiwan is itself seismically vulnerable; the memory of the 921 earthquake in 1999 is not distant. The waste disposal problem remains unsolved globally, not just in Taiwan. And the costs of new nuclear construction, as the experience of Hinkley Point C in Britain and the Vogtle plant in Georgia has demonstrated, have been catastrophically miscalculated in every recent Western project. The industry's promises of on-time and on-budget delivery have been broken consistently enough to justify structural scepticism.

The progressive alternative — accelerated renewable deployment, demand-side management, regional grid interconnection — has a logic to it, and Taiwan has made genuine progress on offshore wind. The argument is that the nuclear path is a path of dependence on complex, expensive, centrally managed infrastructure, while distributed renewables represent a more resilient model that also happens to be getting cheaper faster than any technology in history.

## A different read

The case against nuclear revival is real, but it has been systematically distorted by ideology in ways that the AI energy reckoning is now making very difficult to sustain. Begin with the basic arithmetic. Taiwan's grid currently relies heavily on liquefied natural gas, the import of which depends on sea lanes that China could interdict. The island's geography — relatively compact, densely populated, with limited land for utility-scale solar and constrained by weather patterns that make wind availability variable — makes it one of the harder cases for a pure renewable transition. These are not theoretical obstacles; they are the reason Taiwan's power system engineers have been warning about reliability margins for years.

The AI dimension is not incidental. [Asia made a very large bet on Nvidia chips](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqevr4lnd1nxo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) and AI infrastructure generally, and the energy intensity of that investment is now arriving on the grid in concentrated form. A large language model data centre draws power continuously, at densities that dwarf conventional industrial loads. Taiwan's semiconductor industry already makes the island's per-capita electricity consumption one of the highest in Asia; AI adds another layer. The idea that this demand can be met through offshore wind alone, within the timeframe that grid planners actually work with, is not a serious engineering proposition.

The Germany comparison is instructive and sobering. Germany shut its last nuclear plants in 2023, completing a phase-out that began after Fukushima. The result was a temporary increase in coal burning, sustained dependence on Russian gas up until the moment that dependence became untenable in 2022, and electricity prices that are now among the highest in the developed world. The German Energiewende was not a failure of renewable technology; wind and solar have delivered broadly as promised. It was a failure of planning, of storage, of grid management — and of the political unwillingness to acknowledge that the transition required either a bridge fuel or a continued nuclear contribution. The German Greens, who drove the phase-out, now govern a country that is building new gas infrastructure to compensate for the gap. The ideology won the political argument and lost the energy system.

Taiwan's situation is more acute because the stakes are not just economic but strategic. [China's threat looms over every domestic Taiwanese political calculation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/27/threat-from-china-looms-large-over-taiwans-local-elections), and an island with fragile energy infrastructure is a less credible deterrent than one with redundant, diversified supply. Nuclear's particular advantage in this context — high energy density, domestic fuel stockpiling possible, not dependent on maritime supply chains — is not primarily an environmental argument but a security one. If Taiwan's grid goes down in the first hours of a blockade scenario, the factories stop, the data centres go dark, and the semiconductor fabs — which cannot safely interrupt their processes — take months to restart. That is not a conversation that fits neatly into the standard green-versus-nuclear framing.

The nuclear sceptics are right that waste disposal is unresolved and that new construction costs have been catastrophic in some jurisdictions. But extending the life of existing, amortised plants — which is what much of the debate is actually about — is a very different proposition from commissioning new builds. The operating cost of an existing nuclear plant, once the capital is sunk, is competitive with almost everything else on the grid. The argument for life extension is not that nuclear is perfect but that shutting down functioning, low-carbon, strategically valuable generation capacity on ideological grounds is a form of self-harm that Taiwan's particular circumstances make unusually costly.

## What to watch

Watch the referendum result and, specifically, the demographic breakdown: younger Taiwanese voters have been more sceptical of nuclear than their parents, partly because they were formed by the post-Fukushima consensus, but that consensus has been shifting in countries — Japan, South Korea, France — that have revisited their phase-out decisions in light of energy security pressures. Watch Taiwan's grid operator's reliability margin reports over the next twelve months, which will be a more honest indicator of the energy system's actual condition than the political rhetoric. Watch whether major AI companies with Taiwanese data-centre investments — Apple, Google, Microsoft all have significant supply chain stakes — make any public statements about grid reliability. And watch Japan, whose own nuclear restart programme is the most significant precedent: if Tokyo's experience of reactivating plants without the anticipated safety catastrophe continues to accumulate, the regional consensus may shift faster than the domestic political cycle.
