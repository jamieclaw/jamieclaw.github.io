Canada's federal government on Sunday activated emergency assistance for British Columbia after 102 wildfires broke out across the province — nearly half classified as "out of control." The entire community of Summerland, population approximately 12,000 and located about 30 miles south-west of Kelowna, was evacuated overnight Saturday; a further 8,000 people around Peachland were ordered out; around 10,000 others were told to prepare for immediate evacuation. The Bald Range wildfire, first detected late Friday, grew within hours to over 40 square miles — [nearly twice the size of Manhattan](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/09/canada-british-columbia-government-wildfires) — racing nearly nine miles toward Okanagan Lake. BC Premier David Eby declared a state of emergency, describing the blaze as "like a bomb going off." The Royal Canadian Mounted Police confirmed a possible fatality near Summerland. In total, approximately 22,000 people have been forced to flee their homes since the crisis began.

## The received wisdom

The dominant framing — articulated clearly by academics like Mike Flannigan, professor of wildland fire at Thompson Rivers University — is that human-caused climate change is the root cause, full stop. Higher temperatures, prolonged drought, and drier vegetation create conditions for "explosive" fires that defeat traditional firefighting practice. Flannigan's point that fires previously 20 kilometres from a community warranted no immediate concern, while today a fire 60 kilometres away may reach a town within hours, captures a genuine and alarming shift in fire behaviour. Southern BC and the Pacific Northwest have had virtually no rain for six weeks, leaving forests "bone dry." Flannigan warns that Canada, like California, may be entering an era of year-round "fire years" rather than seasonal fire periods. This reading calls for immediate climate action — emissions reductions — as the ultimate solution, with expanded firefighting capacity and community evacuation planning as interim measures.

That framing is not wrong. The climatic trend is real. The question is what it leaves out.

## A different read

The climate explanation, taken alone, is incomplete — and its incompleteness matters enormously for policy. The uncomfortable fact is that many Western jurisdictions have spent decades under-investing in the active forest management that demonstrably reduces fire severity. The US Forest Service acknowledged in its own reports that fire suppression as the primary strategy, combined with restrictions on controlled burns and commercial logging, produced catastrophic fuel accumulation across vast stretches of Western forest. The same dynamic applies in British Columbia.

Pre-European settlement, Indigenous burning practices — the deliberate, low-intensity use of fire to manage forest fuel loads and promote grassland habitat — kept much of this landscape fire-resilient. Colonial-era governments suppressed these practices, removed Indigenous communities from their traditional territories, and replaced skilled landscape management with a fire-suppression doctrine inherited from 19th-century American forestry theory. The result, compounding for a century, is forests with dramatically elevated fuel loads. A dry summer is the trigger; the accumulated fuel is the powder. Remove the fuel, and the same weather produces a manageable fire rather than a catastrophe.

This is not a theoretical observation. California has, under enormous pressure, begun expanding prescribed burning programmes and has negotiated with tribal nations to restore cultural burning practices. The results are preliminary but measurable: areas with recent controlled burns show dramatically lower fire intensity than adjacent unmanaged forest, under identical weather conditions. British Columbia has its own controlled-burn programme, but its scale has been chronically underfunded relative to need.

The 102 simultaneous fires across BC also point to a structural resource problem that is not primarily climatic. [BC Wildfire Service](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/09/canada-british-columbia-government-wildfires) had ten standard helicopters and one night-vision helicopter involved in the response Saturday night — manifestly insufficient for a province of 944,735 square kilometres with nearly 50 active "out of control" fires. The night-vision limitation Flannigan describes — traditional firefighting assumes a nocturnal reprieve that no longer materialises — points to a failure to adapt procurement and training to known changing conditions. The province has known for years, from its own fire behaviour research, that fire seasons were intensifying. The procurement response has not matched the risk curve.

Canada's federal system compounds the problem. Emergency Management Minister Eleanor Olszewski's Sunday announcement that she "approved a request for federal assistance" is the standard script — but by Sunday the emergency was already in its third day. The lead time on federal activation reflects a constitutional division of responsibilities between provinces and Ottawa that was designed for a different era of disaster scale. A crisis that doubles in size overnight requires pre-authorised federal assets on standby, not a request-and-response process calibrated for slower emergencies.

None of this diminishes the obligation to address the climatic drivers of increasingly extreme fire weather. But "climate change did this" is a political statement that can justify inaction on the things we *can* control: fuel management, procurement, institutional response speed. Blaming the atmosphere for what is partly a governance failure is, in its own way, a form of buck-passing.

## What to watch

- The RCMP investigation into the possible fatality near Summerland — the human toll will shape the political reckoning that follows
- Whether BC Premier Eby's emergency declaration triggers a post-crisis review of the province's prescribed burning programme and helicopter fleet procurement
- The federal-provincial conversation about pre-positioned assets and quicker activation thresholds for future emergencies
- The fire season extension into autumn: Flannigan's warning about conditions in Washington and Oregon suggests this is a regional crisis, not a localised one
