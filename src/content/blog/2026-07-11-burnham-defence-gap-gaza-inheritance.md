Andy Burnham's path to Downing Street became all but certain on Friday as 322 Labour MPs backed him in the party's first nomination tally, leaving him without a credible opponent for the leadership he is set to assume from Sir Keir Starmer, [according to BBC Politics](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c77y0n7k801o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Yet even as the parliamentary party consolidated behind him, two significant headwinds emerged. First, detailed analysis published by the BBC revealed that Starmer's defence spending commitments leave the next prime minister with a structural funding gap estimated at between £4.7 billion and £5 billion—[the BBC described it explicitly as a "black hole"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9q250511neo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in the defence budget that Burnham will have to address immediately on entering office. Second, Burnham moved to distance himself from Labour's early handling of the Gaza conflict, apologising publicly for the party's initial response to the war and acknowledging, in [The Guardian's account](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/jul/09/andy-burnham-labour-didnt-get-it-right-stance-gaza-war), that Labour "didn't get it right." The Gaza apology and the defence arithmetic together define the central tension of the incoming premiership before it has formally begun.

## The received wisdom

Burnham's supporters will frame both developments as evidence of political maturity. The Gaza apology, they will argue, is honest and overdue—Labour's hesitancy in 2023 to call for a ceasefire damaged the party with Muslim voters and with its own membership, and acknowledging error is the first step to rebuilding trust. As for the defence gap: this is Starmer's inheritance to Burnham, not Burnham's creation, and a reforming prime minister committed to fiscal discipline and "Manchesterism"—the devolved, investment-led growth model Burnham has championed—should be capable of finding efficiencies and reprioritisations that close the gap without gutting frontline capability.

The broader progressive narrative will also note that Burnham arrives with genuine working-class credibility and a track record of delivery as Greater Manchester mayor—affordable transport, rough sleeping reduction, an integrated health and social care programme. He is not a metropolitan technocrat; he is, the argument goes, precisely the kind of leader Labour needs to reconnect with the constituencies it has lost. His ascension without a contest reflects not weakness but consensus.

## A different read

That is the favourable reading. The more sceptical one begins with the defence black hole.

NATO has spent the past three years establishing a floor for member spending at two percent of GDP, a threshold that the UK has only recently and narrowly met, and which the alliance's eastern members regard as inadequate given Russia's demonstrated willingness to wage protracted conventional war in Europe. [BBC analysis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c14ymy41v5go?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) documented precisely how Starmer's plan left a gap between announced commitments and funded commitments—between the political promise and the Treasury sign-off required to deliver it. Burnham, who built his reputation on domestic investment and public services, will arrive at Number Ten carrying a defence IOU of nearly five billion pounds. That is not a rounding error; it is roughly the annual budget of the UK Border Force and the National Probation Service combined.

The fiscal context makes this harder, not easier. UK public finances remain stretched after years of elevated borrowing. The Bank of England's chief economist has [signalled this week, per BBC Business](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c932ed8nv75o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), that interest rates may need to rise again given inflationary pressures and sluggish growth. A new prime minister walking into a rate rise environment, with gilts already jittery, is not well-positioned to announce a significant increase in defence borrowing without provoking a market reaction. The options are unpleasant: cut other public spending, raise taxes, stretch the timeline for meeting NATO commitments, or find accounting creativity of the kind that always returns to haunt chancellors.

On Gaza, Burnham's apology is politically shrewd but analytically revealing. [The BBC reported that](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg5yrkeed1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) he and others had actually called for a ceasefire by late October 2023, putting him at odds with Starmer at the time. The apology therefore serves a double function: it distances him from Starmer's record while positioning him as more responsive to the party's activist base and Muslim voters. That is a legitimate political manoeuvre. The question is whether it signals a substantive foreign policy shift—a less US-aligned, more UN-process-oriented approach to the Middle East—or whether it is primarily a domestic political gesture.

For a prime minister about to inherit the defence gap and the UK-US relationship, the distinction matters. Britain's ability to close the £4.7 billion shortfall is partly a function of how the United States perceives UK reliability as a partner. A PM who enters office with a Gaza apology that plays badly in Washington risks finding the transatlantic credit that backstops UK strategic credibility a little thinner than he would like.

The deeper question is whether Burnham's stated domestic priority—Manchesterism, growth through devolution and public investment in the regions—is compatible with the hard demands of a security environment that Starmer, to his credit, took seriously. Burnham has given [little specific detail on defence policy, as BBC analysis noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clywzj2vk3zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). He will need to develop it, fast.

## What to watch

The key signals in the first weeks of a Burnham premiership: whether his Chancellor publishes a credible plan to close the defence shortfall and on what timeline; how he handles his first meeting with Trump, given the Gaza apology's potential reception in Washington; whether devolution commitments survive first contact with Treasury orthodoxy; and whether the Clacton by-election on 13 August, [now confirmed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c15y85gkkx5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), provides an early verdict on whether Burnham's Labour can hold off Reform UK's challenge in the seats it must win to govern with a working majority at the next election.
