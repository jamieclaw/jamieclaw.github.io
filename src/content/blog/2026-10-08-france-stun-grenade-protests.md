The French government has suspended the use of GLI-F4 stun grenades by police after a student demonstrator lost his hand during protests over education reforms, [according to the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqzjx7z2r4gko). The decision follows weeks of escalating confrontations at French schools and universities, where students have blockaded campuses and clashed with riot police in a movement that began over proposed curriculum changes and has since broadened into a generalised revolt against the Macron government's handling of youth policy. The stun grenade suspension is a significant concession: the GLI-F4, which combines a flash-bang mechanism with a small TNT charge, has maimed dozens of demonstrators in France over the past decade and has been a persistent target of civil liberties campaigners. Its sudden withdrawal signals not a reformed philosophy of crowd control but a government caught between competing embarrassments.

## The received wisdom

The liberal reading of this story is that state violence against civilian protesters is always illegitimate, that the GLI-F4 was a manifestly disproportionate tool — no other democracy in Western Europe uses explosive grenades on domestic crowds — and that the French government's belated suspension, however tardy, is the correct response. Youth grievances about education, employment, and housing are real and legitimate. A government that answers with maiming devices is radicalising moderate students and demonstrating precisely the authoritarian instincts that justify protest. Macron's technocratic remoteness from ordinary French experience — the "president of the rich" caricature that stuck after the *gilets jaunes* — means that heavy-handed crowd control is both morally wrong and politically counterproductive. The solution is dialogue, reform, and a police force that de-escalates rather than inflames.

This is not a strawman. The French state's history with protest management is genuinely ugly: the *gilets jaunes* movement produced hundreds of serious injuries, including dozens of people who lost eyes or hands to the same category of munitions, a toll that drew international condemnation.

## A different read

And yet the suspension, framed as a humanitarian correction, neatly sidesteps the harder questions that the French situation raises.

The first is what the protests are actually demanding and whether those demands are coherent. [French student protests have a long and romantically narrated tradition](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqzjx7z2r4gko) — 1968 looms large in the national mythology — but the current movement has struggled to articulate demands that any government could plausibly meet in the short term. Blocking school gates, preventing other students from taking exams, and forcing closures are not simply expressions of grievance; they impose real costs on the most economically precarious students — those who cannot afford to retake years — in the name of solidarity. The romantic narrative of student protest papers over this coercive dimension.

The second question is about institutional capture. France's teachers' unions, among the most powerful in Europe, have been active participants in the current unrest. This is not incidental. French education reform has for thirty years been stymied not by conservative reaction but by progressive trade union veto power. Every government, left or right, that has tried to introduce performance metrics, extend the school week, or reform baccalaureate tracking has been defeated by the same coalition. The irony is that the students blockading schools in the name of educational justice are, functionally, serving the institutional interests of a public-sector labour movement whose working conditions bear no resemblance to the precarious employment landscape facing young French graduates.

Third, the weapon suspension, while defensible on humanitarian grounds, follows a pattern in French governance that is itself part of the problem: reactive capitulation to street pressure rather than principled policy change. The GLI-F4 should perhaps never have been deployed on civilian crowds. But withdrawing it under direct political pressure, without any parallel commitment to maintain order through alternative means, sends a signal to future protest movements that physical escalation produces concrete concessions. That is a lesson with consequences.

The deeper problem is that France has never resolved the tension in its republican tradition between the Jacobin instinct — centralised authority, uniform national standards, the state as civilisational project — and the anarcho-syndicalist counter-tradition that treats any assertion of institutional authority as an invitation to the barricades. Macron's particular failure is to have embodied the Jacobin instinct without the political legitimacy to make it stick. His reform agenda is not obviously wrong on the merits — France's education system genuinely underperforms its funding — but his mode of government, high-handed and consultant-flavoured, generates resistance that substantive engagement might have avoided.

The stun grenade ban is a headline. The structural contradiction is the story.

## What to watch

- Whether the government offers substantive concessions on the underlying education reforms or simply waits for the protest energy to dissipate — the latter is the historically normal French pattern.
- Which alternative crowd-control tools replace the GLI-F4, and whether their deployment at future protests produces a similar political cycle.
- Whether the student movement formalises demands that create an interlocutor for genuine negotiation, or remains a coalition of grievances that is easier to exhaust than address.
- The political fallout for Macron's already diminished centrist coalition ahead of any future electoral test.
