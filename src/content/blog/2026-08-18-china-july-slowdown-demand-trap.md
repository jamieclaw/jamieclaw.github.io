China's National Bureau of Statistics released July economic data on Monday that fell short of analyst forecasts across the board. [Factory output grew 4.5 percent year-on-year](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/17/china-economy-slowdown-signs-extending), down from 5.3 percent in June and missing a Reuters poll estimate of 4.8 percent. Retail sales grew just 0.6 percent, a sharp deceleration from June's 1 percent rise and well below the 1.5 percent forecast. The figures follow China's [annualised growth rate of 4.3 percent](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/aug/17/china-economy-slowdown-signs-extending) for the second quarter of 2026 — one of the weakest quarterly readings since the country began publishing official GDP data in the early 1990s. China's Premier Li Qiang, speaking to the State Council on Monday, acknowledged that "the problem of insufficient domestic demand remains prominent" and called for efforts to "actively stabilise external demand." The NBS attributed some of the July weakness to extreme weather, including typhoons and high temperatures. Analysts at Capital Economics noted a modest positive in continued AI capital expenditure boosting manufacturing, but the overall assessment is that the slowdown is extending beyond the sharp second-quarter fall. [Separate reporting from the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c78gy6ep3n5o) notes that dozens of countries have been helping China dodge US tariffs through transshipment — a strategy now under formal American scrutiny.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream economic analysis of China's current difficulties tends toward one of two framings, both of which contain partial truth. The first is the cyclical story: China is suffering from a temporary confluence of weather disruptions, property sector headwinds still working through the system, and the dampening effect of US tariffs on export volumes. On this view, the policy levers are clear — fiscal loosening, consumption subsidies, infrastructure investment, and patience. The second is the more structural secular-stagnation story: China is experiencing the natural consequences of an economy that over-invested in fixed assets for two decades, created a middle class that saves too much and spends too little, and now faces demographic headwinds as a shrinking working-age population strains both the pension system and long-run productivity growth. Both framings share a common prescription — some combination of domestic demand stimulus and structural reform — and both tend toward a broadly optimistic conclusion: that China's technocrats, who have navigated previous slowdowns, will eventually find the right policy mix. Most Western analysts remain confident that China will muddle through.

## A different read

The confidence in Chinese technocratic management deserves scrutiny, because the current slowdown has a feature that previous ones did not: it is happening simultaneously with a geopolitical realignment that is permanently shrinking the external demand that historically acted as China's safety valve when domestic demand faltered. For most of the past thirty years, when Chinese consumers did not spend enough to sustain growth, Chinese factories could export their way through the shortfall. The US market absorbed hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods annually; the EU added more. Trump's tariff regime — now formally buttressed by a [new US report identifying dozens of countries that helped China dodge tariffs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c78gy6ep3n5o) through transshipment — is not a temporary negotiating posture. It reflects a structural bipartisan consensus in Washington, shared by both parties, that the previous trade relationship produced unacceptable dependencies. European industrial policy, particularly in automotive sectors, is moving in the same direction. That safety valve is closing.

The consequence is that China faces an internal demand problem at precisely the moment when the external demand backstop is unavailable. Li Qiang's call to "stabilise external demand" through "mutually beneficial international economic cooperation" is, in this context, a polite way of saying China needs to find new markets — which is why the BRI push into Africa, Latin America, and Central Asia continues despite its mixed returns. But those markets are smaller, less wealthy, and in many cases already carrying unsustainable Chinese debt loads. The arithmetic does not obviously work.

There is also a deeper political economy constraint on domestic stimulus. The standard prescription — boost consumer spending through direct transfers, expand social safety nets to reduce precautionary savings, allow the property sector to clear at market prices — runs directly against the interests of the constituencies that sustain Communist Party control. Direct transfers to consumers bypass the state-owned enterprise network and the local government finance vehicles through which the Party allocates patronage. Clearing property at market prices would wipe out the wealth of hundreds of millions of middle-class households whose life savings are in apartments. Expanding social safety nets requires fiscal space that local governments — many technically insolvent after the property crash — do not have. This is not primarily a technocratic puzzle; it is a political economy trap in which the solutions to the economic problem are blocked by the political structure that the current leadership depends upon.

The AI capital expenditure bright spot that Capital Economics identified deserves a note. China's manufacturing build-out for AI hardware — servers, memory chips, networking infrastructure — is real and significant, and it is partly insulated from the US tariff regime because China has been rapidly building domestic semiconductor capability since the first rounds of export controls. But AI capex is not a consumption story; it does not directly translate into rising household incomes or retail spending. It boosts factory output numbers while doing little for the demand-side weakness that is the actual pathology. The history of export-led industrial investment strategies — Japan in the 1980s, South Korea in the 1990s, China itself in the 2000s — suggests that investment-driven growth eventually hits a wall when domestic demand and external markets cannot absorb the output. China hit the Japanese wall in the late 2010s with steel and solar; it may be approaching an AI hardware version of the same dynamic by the end of this decade.

## What to watch

- Beijing's fiscal response: any announcement of direct household transfer payments or major consumption subsidy programmes would represent a genuine policy shift, not just more infrastructure spending.
- The transshipment enforcement story: if the US successfully pressures the dozen-plus countries identified in its report, Chinese export routes become meaningfully more expensive and the trade drag compounds.
- Property sector clearance rates: if transaction volumes in tier-one cities begin recovering, it signals that the wealth-destruction overhang is partially resolving; if they continue flat, the savings glut problem persists.
- South-east Asian manufacturing capacity: Vietnam, Mexico, and India are the primary beneficiaries of supply chain diversification away from China — their export numbers in Q3 will tell part of the China tariff-evasion story through the back door.
