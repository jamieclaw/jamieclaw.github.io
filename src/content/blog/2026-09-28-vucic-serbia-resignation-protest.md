Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić announced his resignation on Sunday, ending a decade-long grip on the country's politics that had been tested to breaking point by months of sustained street protests. [Demonstrations erupted](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/27/serbian-president-aleksandar-vucic-resigns-amid-prolonged-protests) after allegations of systemic corruption, cronyism, and a heavy-handed security apparatus became impossible for the public to ignore. Despite announcing his departure, Vučić indicated he intends to run in early elections — meaning the political contest is far from over. The BBC [described his administration](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckpq0g7z5pjjo) as having been "dogged by prolonged protests over allegations of corruption," a diplomatic understatement of what amounts to one of the more consequential popular uprisings in the post-Yugoslav space in years.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream telling of this moment is a straightforward democratic triumph. Citizens took to the streets, sustained the pressure, and forced a strongman from power. Western capitals and European Union officials will likely hail it as proof that civil society can prevail even in semi-authoritarian contexts, and that the arc of democratic backsliding in southeast Europe is beginning to reverse. Commentators will point to Hungary's Orbán, Slovakia's Fico, and now the fall of Vučić as a signal that the region's illiberal wave may be cresting. The European integration narrative will be invoked: Serbia cannot realistically join the EU with this kind of governance record, and perhaps the prospect of membership has quietly incentivised reformers. The student-led protest movement will be celebrated as inspirational — young people organising without a single party capturing the energy, a model, it will be said, for democracies elsewhere. This framing is not dishonest. It just may be incomplete.

## A different read

It is worth recalling that Vučić's previous transformation — from Milošević-era nationalist information minister to seemingly pro-Western presidential moderniser — was celebrated in exactly the same terms. The man is a political survivor of extraordinary resourcefulness. His announcement that he intends to contest the very elections his resignation triggers ought to temper every headline calling this the end of his era. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/27/serbian-president-aleksandar-vucic-resigns-amid-prolonged-protests) that "despite student-led protests and political unrest challenging his rule, Vucic intends to run in the upcoming elections" — a detail buried beneath the triumphant framing of a strongman falling.

The historical record in the Balkans is sobering. In Romania, years of anti-corruption street protests eventually forced governmental changes, but the same political networks reconstituted themselves under new branding. In Macedonia, the so-called Colorful Revolution of 2016 produced a government change but left much of the underlying state capture intact. The pattern across southeastern Europe has been less "strongman falls, democracy blooms" and more "strongman adapts, state apparatus endures." The opposition in Serbia, moreover, is not a unified civic movement waiting to govern. It is a fragmented coalition in which some components are themselves nationalist and anti-Brussels; the prospect of a genuinely liberal successor government is real but not guaranteed.

There is also a structural question about what drove the protests. [The BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckpq0g7z5pjjo) that demonstrations followed allegations of corruption specifically tied to infrastructure projects — the collapse of a canopy at Novi Sad railway station, which killed fifteen people and which opposition figures attributed to politically-connected contractors cutting corners. That kind of trigger — not abstract democratic principle but visceral, lethal state failure — is powerful in the short term. But it is also fragile. When the immediate shock fades, political grievances can be channelled toward demagogues of a different variety. Serbia has a deep pool of competing nationalist grievances — over Kosovo above all — that can be mobilised to crowd out liberal reform energy very quickly.

The European Union's leverage is real but also limited. Brussels has been dangling accession prospects over Serbia for the better part of two decades. Vučić played that leverage expertly, making just enough gestures toward European norms to keep the process alive while maintaining an independent relationship with Moscow and Beijing. Whoever replaces him will face the same structural incentive: exploit the EU membership process for economic benefits and international legitimacy without ever fully committing to the rule-of-law reforms it requires. The EU has never shown the strategic coherence or political will to actually close that door.

The more interesting question — and the one worth watching — is whether the protest movement can institutionalise itself. Civic energy that does not produce durable political organisations tends to dissipate. The Maidan uprising in Ukraine eventually produced a genuine (if imperfect) democratic realignment because it forged political actors who persisted. Serbia's student movement has been impressively disciplined and leaderless by design. That discipline is admirable, but leaderlessness, past a certain moment, becomes a liability. Someone will eventually organise this energy. The question is who, and toward what.

## What to watch

Watch whether Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party fractures or holds together under early-election pressure — if the party fractures, genuinely new political forces may emerge, but if it coheres, Vučić returns in a different formal role. Watch the EU's response: does Brussels attach meaningful conditionality to the accession process or issue warm congratulations without leverage? Watch Serbia's relationship with Moscow, which has supported Vučić and has every reason to back a destabilising successor if a genuinely pro-Western government looks likely. And watch whether the corruption prosecutions that presumably motivated the protests actually proceed — without them, resignation is political theatre.
