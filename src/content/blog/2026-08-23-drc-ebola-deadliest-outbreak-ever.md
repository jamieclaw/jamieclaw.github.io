The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo is now [the deadliest in the country's history, with at least 2,325 dead and 4,945 confirmed cases](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/17/ebola-outbreak-drc-democratic-republic-of-congo-deadliest-in-history), surpassing the 2018-20 outbreak. The DRC's public health institute recorded 101 new cases in a single 24-hour period at the time of last reporting, suggesting the outbreak has not yet peaked. Al Jazeera has [reported separately on the deteriorating security situation in eastern DRC](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/22/armed-men-kidnap-dozens-in-attacks-on-nigerian-villages), where militia activity continues to complicate humanitarian access. The outbreak's scale — and its crossing of a historical threshold — has received remarkably little international attention relative to other crises on the front pages this week. That disparity in attention is itself worth examining.

## The received wisdom

The standard global health community reading of recurring DRC Ebola outbreaks is structural: the country's health system has been chronically under-resourced, conflict in eastern provinces repeatedly disrupts vaccination campaigns and contact tracing, and years of international underinvestment in health infrastructure mean that outbreaks that could be contained in a functional-state environment spin into epidemics. The WHO has repeatedly flagged the correlation between insecurity and outbreak persistence; the 2018-20 outbreak was notable for occurring in an active conflict zone where health workers were attacked. The prescription is more funding, more security, and better international coordination.

This reading is accurate as far as it goes. The DRC has roughly one physician per 10,000 people in many provinces. Laboratory confirmation infrastructure is sparse. Cold chain logistics for vaccines fail in areas without reliable electricity. These are not mysteries; they are documented and persistent gaps.

## A different read

But the standard reading consistently stops short of asking why those gaps remain after decades of international engagement — and what that failure reveals about the model of international health assistance.

The DRC has received billions of dollars in international health aid since the 1990s. The country is home to some of the densest concentrations of international NGOs, UN agencies, and global health implementing partners on the continent. And yet each Ebola outbreak finds the same structural vulnerabilities: inadequate community trust, broken cold chains, insufficient contact tracers, and health workers who are underpaid, undertrained, or simply absent. The question is not whether more resources are needed but whether the current delivery model is capable of building durable state capacity, or whether it has instead created a parallel aid economy that substitutes for, rather than strengthening, indigenous health institutions.

This is not a novel critique. The Lancet and other leading medical journals have [published extensive literature on the "NGO-ization" of public health in fragile states](https://www.thelancet.com) — the pattern in which international actors deliver services directly, local governments are bypassed or treated as obstacles, and no indigenous capacity-building occurs because it would complicate the operating model of organisations whose existence depends on continued need. It is a pattern that serves the incentives of the international system while producing reliably inadequate outcomes for the populations concerned.

There is also a harder political point. The DRC's eastern provinces, where most Ebola outbreaks originate, are subject to overlapping armed group control, weak central government authority, and resource extraction conflicts that have been ongoing since the late 1990s. The mineral wealth of the Kivu provinces — coltan, cassiterite, gold — funds the armed groups that make health interventions impossible. That mineral wealth flows primarily to international technology supply chains. The smartphones and electric vehicles that Western consumers use contain materials whose extraction is financed partly by violence that makes places like this one impossible to vaccinate. The international community's concern about DRC Ebola, such as it is, does not typically extend to examining the supply chains that fund the insecurity underlying it.

The crossing of the historical death toll threshold this week should be a moment for something more than a press release from the WHO and a diplomatic note from donor governments. The 2014-16 West Africa Ebola outbreak — which killed over 11,000 people and briefly threatened international spread — generated an emergency global response that produced, among other things, the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations and the accelerated development of an Ebola vaccine. That vaccine exists; it is being deployed in DRC. And the outbreak is still the largest ever in the country's history. The lesson should be obvious.

## What to watch

- Whether the WHO Director-General declares a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, which would trigger a higher level of international coordination and resource mobilisation.
- Security conditions in eastern DRC: any deterioration in the Kivu provinces will directly affect the health response by cutting off vaccination teams.
- Whether the outbreak spreads across the border into Uganda, Rwanda, or Burundi, all of which have land crossings with affected zones.
- International donor responses: the amount pledged and the speed of disbursement will reveal whether this outbreak is treated as an emergency or as another manageable African crisis.
