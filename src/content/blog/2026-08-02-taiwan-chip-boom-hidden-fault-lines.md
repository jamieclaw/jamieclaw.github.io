Taiwan's economy grew [12.92 percent in the second quarter of 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/8/1/growing-like-gangbusters-can-taiwan-maintain-its-economic-momentum), according to government data released Friday — continuing a trajectory that saw 13.69 percent growth in Q1 and 8.63 percent for the full year of 2025. The engine is artificial intelligence: Taiwan's semiconductor exports, dominated by TSMC, have surged as US technology firms race to secure advanced chips. Earlier this year Taiwan's stock exchange became the fifth-largest in the world by market capitalisation, overtaking the UK, Canada, and India. The United States imported $201 billion worth of goods from Taiwan last year, nearly double the 2024 figure of $116 billion. In May, Taiwan eclipsed China to become the third-largest source of US imports after Mexico and Canada. A Trump administration trade deal signed this year commits Taiwan to invest $500 billion in the United States; in return, Taiwanese firms can import 2.5 times their US factory capacity without steep tariffs, and Taiwan agreed to reduce tariffs on 99 percent of US exports.

## The received wisdom

The dominant narrative in the Western financial press is celebratory, and not without reason. Taiwan's growth is described as a "tiger economy" revival, a vindication of the island's decades-long investment in human capital and technology specialisation. The semiconductor sector is treated as a strategic trump card — a source of both economic and geopolitical leverage that gives Taiwan a measure of deterrence against Beijing that no amount of military spending alone could purchase. The AI boom is real, globally transformative, and Taiwan sits at its supply-chain chokepoint. Experts like Dexter Tiff Roberts of the Atlantic Council have described the trajectory as "long term," pointing to the fact that Taiwan produces roughly 90 percent of the advanced chips that power leading AI models. The trade deal with the Trump administration is presented as smart diplomacy — binding Washington's economic interests to Taiwan's security by making US technology firms structurally dependent on Taiwanese supply chains.

## A different read

The celebratory narrative is accurate as far as it goes, but it has the quality of all boom-time analysis: it extrapolates trend lines and minimises the mean-reversion risks. There are at least three structural vulnerabilities that the current growth rate is papering over.

The first is concentration. TSMC alone accounts for roughly 40 percent of Taiwan's stock market and [four percent of its GDP growth](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/8/1/growing-like-gangbusters-can-taiwan-maintain-its-economic-momentum). That is not a diversified economy; it is a monoculture with an exceptionally valuable crop. The parallel in economic history is the Dutch disease — or more precisely the reverse Dutch disease. The Netherlands in the 1960s saw its manufacturing sector hollowed out by North Sea gas revenues; Taiwan's risk is that its traditional export sectors — plastics, textiles, precision machinery — are already underperforming as capital and talent concentrate in semiconductors. A K-shaped economy is developing, where the top of the labour market captures almost all of the AI windfall while the broader workforce stagnates.

The second vulnerability is demographic. Taiwan has a rapidly ageing population, with roughly a fifth of its residents over 65. The chip industry employs at most 350,000 people in an island of 23 million. The AI boom is enriching a relatively narrow cohort; a "wealth effect" from rising stock portfolios helps the wider population only at the margins. Academic adviser Reza Hasmath at the University of Alberta has warned explicitly that this is "not sustainable," and that if the AI boom reverses, Taiwan faces "a recipe for electoral change." There is an analogy here to the oil states of the Gulf, which have discovered that spectacular aggregate growth can coexist with stagnant median wages and political discontent if the distribution is too uneven.

The third vulnerability is the US relationship itself. The trade deal that Washington struck with Taipei looks mutually beneficial on paper, but it contains a structural tension. Taiwan's trade surplus with the United States is approaching $200 billion and growing. Trump has repeatedly demonstrated that he views bilateral trade deficits as political liabilities, regardless of their economic logic. Hasmath warns explicitly that Trump "will soon look to renegotiate his country's deals with Taipei." Roberts at the Atlantic Council notes, more diplomatically, that Trump is "mercurial" and that this creates "uncertainty." What neither quite states is that the deal Trump signed with Taiwan is more fragile than a treaty — it is an executive agreement with no Senate ratification, and it can be walked back as easily as it was made.

And then there is China. [Beijing has been intensifying its maritime patrols around Taiwan](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/29/china-puts-squeeze-on-taiwan-with-new-maritime-patrols) and has formally characterised the Taiwan-US semiconductor relationship as an attempt to "drain Taiwan's economic interests" and "hollow out" its industry. A Chinese government spokesman has said Taiwan's growing proximity to the US tech sector is a vulnerability, not a strength. That framing is self-serving, but it contains a real strategic logic: if Taiwan's economy is as dependent on US market access as the numbers suggest, then any deterioration in US-Taiwan relations — whether driven by Trump's tariff instincts, a domestic political shock, or a fresh crisis in the Strait — would expose the entire growth story as contingent rather than structural.

The responsible conservative take is not to dismiss the boom but to ask what a Taiwan that is genuinely resilient would look like: more diversified, more domestically demand-driven, less dependent on the goodwill of a single, mercurial great power. That is a harder political sell than celebrating 13 percent growth — which is partly why it is not being made.

## What to watch

Watch the evolution of Taiwan's trade surplus with the US: if it continues to widen toward $200 billion, expect Trump to raise it publicly, probably after a domestic political reverse. Watch also whether TSMC's planned US manufacturing investments — the Arizona fabs — begin to produce competitive advanced chips at scale; that would reduce Taiwan's leverage over Washington even as it reduces the island's military-industrial concentration risk. The China Maritime Patrol Authority's recent intensification of patrols in the Taiwan Strait is a separate thread worth tracking: economic interdependence has historically not deterred military coercion, and the boom is not a substitute for a credible deterrence posture.
