Five European Union member states — Germany, Austria, Greece, Denmark, and the Netherlands — are [inching toward a deal on migrant "return hubs"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/4/five-eu-nations-inch-towards-deal-on-migrant-return-hubs-by-2027?traffic_source=rss) to be established outside EU territory by 2027, according to Al Jazeera's reporting. The hubs would hold rejected asylum seekers prior to deportation, addressing one of the most persistent failures of the current system: the gap between a rejected claim and an actual departure. The five nations have not yet publicly identified where the hubs would be located — a detail that, given the political sensitivities involved, is itself revealing. The initiative follows years of pressure from centre-right and right-populist parties across the continent, and arrives as migration continues to rank among the top concerns of European voters in nearly every polling methodology.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and progressive framing of return hubs focuses on the risks of off-shoring detention: that facilities outside EU jurisdiction will be beyond effective legal scrutiny, that they create conditions for rights violations that the EU itself could not legally countenance within its borders, and that "return" arrangements with third countries routinely collapse in practice when those countries refuse to accept deportees or face their own internal political crises. There is extensive evidence supporting each of these concerns. The UK's Rwanda scheme — before its legal and operational demise — demonstrated precisely how difficult it is to construct a deportation framework that survives contact with both domestic courts and the realities of bilateral diplomacy. Italy's Albania deal, a closer analogue, has faced persistent legal challenges.

The broader progressive argument is that return hubs are a political gesture: they allow governments to appear tough on migration while doing almost nothing to address root causes in countries of origin. On that last point, the critics have a reasonable case.

## A different read

The critics' reasonable case does not, however, amount to a workable alternative. The EU's asylum system as currently constituted has failed on every metric that a morally serious person should care about. It has failed the migrants who cross the Mediterranean in dangerous vessels, many of whom are not fleeing persecution as defined by the 1951 Refugee Convention but are seeking economic improvement — entirely understandable, but legally and politically distinct from asylum. It has failed the frontline states — Greece, Italy, Spain — that bear the administrative and political costs of arrivals disproportionate to their processing capacity. And it has failed the European publics who watch the gap between legal obligation and operational reality and draw the rational conclusion that the system's administrators are not serious people.

The 1951 Convention was designed for the Cold War context of individual political dissidents fleeing defined state persecution, not for mass population movements driven by a combination of climate disruption, failed states, economic stagnation, and active conflict zones across a wide arc from West Africa to Central Asia. The gap between the legal framework and the political reality is not a failure of will; it is a failure of institutional design that no amount of progressive moral rhetoric will close.

The case for return hubs is not that they are ideal. It is that they are part of a credible enforcement architecture — and credible enforcement is, paradoxically, the only humane policy for managing large-scale migration. When people know that an unsuccessful asylum claim will result in prompt return, the incentive to make dangerous crossings diminishes. When they know that the system is essentially unenforced, the incentive structure runs in the opposite direction, generating the demand for smuggling networks and the crossings that kill thousands each year. Denmark's tough-but-consistent approach to asylum since the mid-2010s — deeply controversial among European progressives — has produced measurably lower irregular arrivals than comparably prosperous countries with more permissive systems.

The five-nation initiative is also notable for what it reveals about the politics: Germany under whatever coalition emerges next, the Netherlands after Wilders' fragile majority, Denmark's Social Democrats governing to the right of their natural instincts, Austria's centre-right and Greece's Kyriakos Mitsotakis — these are not countries led by cartoonish xenophobes. They are governments responding to democratic pressure from electorates that have spent a decade watching migration policy made by people who will bear none of its consequences. The failure to specify where the hubs will be located is the tell: the conceptual agreement is easier than the implementation, and the implementation will be fought over fiercely.

## What to watch

- The location negotiations — which third countries the EU approaches, and whether those countries accept (North Africa remains the likely zone, but Morocco, Tunisia, and Egypt all have leverage to extract concessions).
- Legal challenges: the EU Court of Justice and European Court of Human Rights will both face applications the moment any hub is operationalised; the UK's Rwanda experience should be read as a preview.
- Whether the UK seeks any coordination with the EU framework — Anglo-European cooperation on migration enforcement would be politically significant for both the Burnham government and the five-nation group.
- Italian and Spanish reactions — both countries are frontline states not included in the initial five, and their exclusion could become a source of intra-EU tension.
