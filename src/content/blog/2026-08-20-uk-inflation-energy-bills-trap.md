[A sharp jump in energy bills](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cdrv7jr8n74o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) has driven UK inflation to its highest level in four months, complicating the Bank of England's rate path and raising fresh questions about the pace and management of the UK's energy transition. The BBC's business coverage noted that [inflation is heating up](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj63jr5r09zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), though analysts caution against reading another full-blown crisis into the data — the structural story, they say, is one of persistent above-target inflation rather than a spiral. Meanwhile, [global borrowing costs have hit fresh highs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c1k1x1zdywdo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) driven by oil prices, AI infrastructure investment, and inflation concerns, suggesting that the UK's domestic inflation challenge is occurring against an unfavourable external backdrop. The numbers arrive as the Burnham government faces political pressure on cost-of-living, with domestic energy affordability emerging as a central electoral vulnerability.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream energy-and-inflation commentary will frame this story primarily as a failure of market regulation and the legacy of privatisation. The argument goes: Britain deregulated its energy markets, became dependent on volatile wholesale gas prices, and then compounded the problem by building insufficient renewable capacity to buffer households against price spikes. The solution, in this framing, is accelerated renewable deployment, better-designed price caps, and windfall taxes on energy company profits — essentially, more intervention and faster green transition. The human cost of high energy bills falls disproportionately on lower-income households who spend a larger share of their income on energy, making this also a distributional justice issue. Government action, from this perspective, is both economically and morally required.

On the monetary policy side, the received wisdom holds that the Bank of England should be cautious about raising rates too aggressively in response to energy-driven inflation, since this is essentially a supply shock — raising rates cannot produce more gas — and aggressive tightening risks tipping an already fragile economy into recession.

These are coherent arguments. The distributional concern about energy poverty is real and morally serious.

## A different read

The trouble with the "faster green transition" response to energy price spikes is that it gets the causation partially backwards. The UK's energy vulnerability is not primarily a failure to deploy enough renewables — it is the product of a set of policy decisions, made over two decades and across governments of both parties, that accelerated the closure of North Sea gas production, opposed onshore wind and shale extraction on planning and political grounds, relied heavily on interconnectors to European gas markets, and underinvested in domestic storage capacity. The result is an economy with high renewable ambition but limited energy security — a combination that produces exactly the kind of vulnerability to price spikes that we are now observing.

Ofgem's energy price cap mechanism, introduced after the 2021-2022 energy crisis, is a political solution that provides short-term consumer protection while doing nothing to address the underlying supply-side dysfunction. The cap limits what suppliers can charge per unit, but it cannot conjure cheaper gas out of thin air. When wholesale prices rise — as they have, driven by global factors including Middle East instability, LNG market tightness, and oil price movements — the cap is adjusted upward, passing costs to consumers on a quarterly basis. This is a price-smoothing mechanism, not a price-reduction mechanism. Households are shielded from the worst monthly volatility but still bear the underlying cost increase — they just experience it later and more gradually.

There is a more fundamental structural point about the interaction between net-zero policy and energy security. The UK's Climate Change Committee and successive governments have operated on an assumption that the transition from fossil fuels to renewables would, over time, reduce energy costs — that the sun and wind, once the capital investment is made, provide free fuel. This is true in a narrow technical sense, but it abstracts away several complications: the cost of grid balancing and storage to manage intermittent supply, the vulnerability of wind and solar to weather-driven output variability, and the period of transition itself during which the old baseload capacity has been retired before new dispatchable capacity is ready.

The specific irony of the current inflation spike is that it is being driven largely by gas prices in an economy that has been told for a decade that gas is a declining fuel. The message has been consistent: gas will become less central to UK energy. Investors have rationally reduced capital allocation to North Sea production in response to that signal. That rational response to policy signals has made the UK more dependent on imported gas precisely as domestic gas infrastructure ages and closes. This is not a market failure in the conventional sense; it is a policy-induced investment drought.

The historical analogy that comes to mind is California's electricity market in the late 1990s and early 2000s, where a mixture of deregulation and environmental constraints on new generation investment produced severe price spikes and rolling blackouts. The lesson drawn — both correctly and incorrectly — was that markets fail at energy provision. The more precise lesson was that partial deregulation in a market with significant regulatory constraints on supply produces the worst of both worlds: market-level prices without market-level supply responses.

For the Bank of England, the dilemma is genuine. Energy-driven inflation is less responsive to rate increases than demand-driven inflation. But if the Bank signals that it will tolerate above-target inflation because it is "supply-driven," it risks anchoring inflation expectations at a higher level, which has its own costs. The credibility cost of persistent above-target inflation is cumulative, and central banks that lose inflation-fighting credibility find it very expensive to recover.

## What to watch

Watch the Ofgem price cap announcement for Q4 2026 — the trajectory of the cap will determine whether energy inflation persists into the autumn or stabilises.

Watch Bank of England rate decisions and forward guidance. Governor statements about whether this inflation is "transitory" or persistent will signal the MPC's read of the structural situation.

Watch North Sea licensing decisions. Whether the Burnham government approves or blocks new extraction licences is the single clearest indicator of whether it is willing to prioritise short-run energy security over long-run net-zero optics.

Watch household energy debt data. Citizens Advice and similar organisations publish regular data on arrears; a significant increase would signal that the affordability crisis is moving from uncomfortable to acute.
