The United States national debt [has passed $40 trillion](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gp8252ejo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — a figure that represents a doubling in roughly a decade and that [NPR noted](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/19/nx-s1-5937552/the-u-s-debt-tops-a-record-shattering-40-trillion-yes-with-a-t) crossed the threshold quietly, with little of the ceremony that might accompany a milestone of such magnitude. This comes against a backdrop of [rising global borrowing costs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c1k1x1zdywdo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) driven by persistent inflation, AI infrastructure spending, and oil price pressures — meaning the cost of servicing that debt is rising simultaneously with the debt itself. The Congressional Budget Office has long projected that interest payments will consume an ever-larger share of federal revenues. We are now living inside those projections.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream economic commentary on the $40 trillion figure will tend toward one of two responses. The more dovish will note that debt-to-GDP ratios are the relevant metric, not the nominal dollar figure, and that the US retains reserve currency status that makes its borrowing capacity uniquely large. Japan has maintained a debt-to-GDP ratio above 200% for years without a crisis; the comparison is instructive, the argument goes, because sovereign debt crises are ultimately about rollover risk and confidence, not arithmetic triggers. The more hawkish mainstream view acknowledges the concern but tends to frame it as a long-run structural problem — something to address through revenue increases, entitlement reform, or both, preferably after the next election. Both framings share a common assumption: that the situation, while suboptimal, is manageable with competent policy.

Meanwhile, the political conversation in Washington has been notable chiefly for its avoidance of the subject. The Trump administration passed a budget framework earlier this year that contained no serious fiscal consolidation. The Democratic opposition has not made debt a central issue. The political incentives, on both sides, run toward spending and away from restraint.

This is the received wisdom, presented in good faith.

## A different read

The trouble with the "manageable in principle" framing is that it abstracts away the political conditions required for management. Yes, a technically competent government with genuine fiscal will could stabilise the debt trajectory — through a combination of entitlement reform, revenue adjustments, and reduced discretionary spending. But we do not inhabit a world of technically competent governments exercising fiscal will. We inhabit a world in which the bipartisan consensus against fiscal discipline has become so entrenched that even raising the subject is treated as politically eccentric.

Consider the trajectory. The $20 trillion mark was crossed in 2017. The $30 trillion mark in 2022. Now $40 trillion in 2026. In nine years, the debt doubled. This was not primarily the product of extraordinary crises requiring emergency spending — though COVID certainly contributed. It was the product of structural deficits that persisted through economic booms as well as busts. The 2017 tax cuts reduced federal revenues. The 2021-2022 spending packages expanded the baseline. No administration in this period made a serious attempt to close the gap between revenues and expenditures.

The interest payment dimension is particularly important and underreported. When interest rates were near zero, carrying $30-40 trillion in debt was expensive but manageable in cash-flow terms. [Rising global borrowing costs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c1k1x1zdywdo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) change that calculus. The Federal Reserve's rate increases to combat inflation have dramatically increased the government's annual interest bill — estimates place it above $1 trillion per year, making it the single largest line item in the federal budget, ahead of defence and Medicare. Unlike those programmes, interest payments cannot be cut through legislation; they are the unavoidable consequence of prior borrowing decisions.

The Japan comparison, often invoked to reassure, deserves scrutiny. Japan's debt is overwhelmingly held domestically, by Japanese savers and institutions, which creates a fundamentally different rollover dynamic than US debt, which relies substantially on foreign holders — including China and other nations whose disposition toward continued accumulation of US Treasuries may not be permanent. Moreover, Japan's experience has not been cost-free: two decades of ultra-low growth, persistent deflation, and diminished fiscal space to respond to crises. If that is the "manageable" outcome, it is not obviously desirable.

The more historically resonant comparison may be the late-Roman fiscal situation, or — less dramatically — the trajectory of several European welfare states in the 1970s-80s that eventually required IMF interventions and painful structural adjustments. The British experience in 1976, when the Callaghan government had to accept an IMF bailout and impose severe public spending cuts, is a reminder that fiscal crises can arrive faster than comfortable projections suggest, and that when they arrive, the adjustment is not managed by technocrats in an orderly fashion — it is imposed by market pressure and produces genuine social pain.

None of this means the US faces an imminent 1976-style crisis. Reserve currency status is real and consequential. But it is a cushion, not an exemption. And the rate at which the cushion is being depleted has accelerated significantly in the past decade. The $40 trillion figure is a landmark not because it triggers an automatic crisis, but because it represents a political failure of the first order — a bipartisan unwillingness to make the choices that fiscal sustainability requires, choices that will eventually be made either voluntarily or under duress.

## What to watch

Watch the next Congressional Budget Office long-range projection — the 10-year baseline will indicate whether current spending trajectories imply a stabilisation or continued increase in debt-to-GDP.

Watch the Federal Reserve's rate path. If rates remain elevated — as persistent inflation and global borrowing costs suggest they might — the annual interest bill will continue to grow, crowding out other federal spending.

Watch foreign Treasury holdings. A significant reduction in Chinese or Japanese purchases of US Treasuries would be an early warning signal of the kind of confidence crisis that manageable debt becomes unmanageable debt.

Watch whether any political figure — in either party — makes debt a primary electoral issue in the 2026 midterms. The absence of such a figure would itself be diagnostically important.
