Two unmanned drones were spotted over a German military base in Mechernich, near Bonn, on Thursday evening, the Bundeswehr confirmed — a facility reported to house components for US-made Patriot air-defence systems. The incident came days after an explosive-laden drone was found on a runway at Leipzig/Halle airport, used by NATO, the German military, and Ukraine's Antonov Airlines. German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt told the newspaper *Bild*: ["We're not at war, but we are the daily target of hybrid warfare."](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwyeg1ljp2eo) The Bundeswehr has declined to comment on origins. Russia has consistently denied any involvement in a [pattern of drone sightings](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/9/germany-warns-of-daily-hybrid-warfare-following-suspected-drone-attack) across European NATO states that accelerated in 2025. Germany is currently investigating how the Leipzig drone — laden with explosives — reached a runway used by NATO logistics.

## The received wisdom

The liberal internationalist reading of these incidents focuses on de-escalation optics. Any direct attribution of drone attacks to Moscow, the argument goes, risks an escalatory spiral that could tip the conflict beyond Ukraine's borders. Better to manage the incidents quietly through intelligence channels, strengthen defensive measures, and avoid giving Russia the confrontation it allegedly seeks. The emphasis falls on resilience — civil defence upgrades, multi-agency coordination, a European "drone wall" air defence network discussed at EU level — rather than retaliation or explicit deterrence postures. The corollary is that dramatic public accusations fuel Russian information operations by making Europeans feel besieged and vulnerable, which is precisely what Moscow wants.

This is not a frivolous position. Ambiguity about attribution is real: not every drone sighting is Russian in origin, and some European governments have been embarrassed by premature attributions. Denmark's announcement that a "professional actor" was behind drone incursions over its airports was careful not to name Russia explicitly.

## A different read

But the case for managed quietude is wearing thin. The pattern is now too consistent and the targeting too precise to be coincidental. Leipzig/Halle is not a randomly chosen civilian airport — it is one of the key logistics hubs for weapons shipments to Ukraine. Mechernich houses Patriot system components, the same air-defence batteries Ukraine has been desperately requesting and which President Zelensky warned this week are running low on interceptor missiles. The targeting logic, whoever is executing it, is coherent: degrade the supply chain that sustains Ukrainian air defence.

The historical parallel worth reaching for is not 2014 or even the Cold War. It is the 1930s doctrine of "plausible deniability" that powers have long used to wage war beneath the threshold of formal conflict. The Soviet Union ran sabotage campaigns against Western logistics infrastructure during the Cold War without triggering Article 5. China wages persistent cyber and economic coercion campaigns today that fall below kinetic thresholds. Russia has, since at least 2015, operated a playbook of hybrid attack — assassination attempts, infrastructure sabotage, election interference, arson — that is explicitly designed to be deniable. The Salisbury poisonings, the Nordstream pipeline sabotage (attribution still contested), the 2024 fires at European arms factories: the pattern is a pattern, even if individual events retain uncertainty.

Germany's response illustrates the structural problem democracies face in countering this. [Dobrindt's public statement](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwyeg1ljp2eo) naming "hybrid warfare" is itself a departure — previous German governments were fastidiously careful not to escalate public rhetoric. But naming the problem is not the same as solving it. Military police were deployed to Mechernich; the matter was then handed to local police. This is not a counterintelligence response; it is a crime-scene response. The institutional gap between what the threat requires — sustained counterintelligence, hardened perimeters around critical logistics nodes, active counterdrone capabilities — and what Germany's peacetime legal framework allows is enormous.

The Trump administration's inconsistency on Patriot supply adds another layer of fragility. Trump apparently offered Ukraine a licence to manufacture Patriots in early July, then denied any such agreement the following week. Whatever the internal White House dynamics, the mixed signalling is strategically damaging: it tells Moscow that Washington's commitment to sustaining Ukraine's air defences is uncertain, which lowers the cost of targeting those supply chains.

The EU's proposed "drone wall" is the right directional instinct, but a continental air-defence architecture will take years to build and billions to fund — and the attacks are happening now, this week, at Leipzig and Mechernich. In the interim, NATO members need to treat their logistics infrastructure for Ukraine as a theatre of active conflict, not a domestic law-enforcement matter. That means hardened perimeters, shoot-down authority for military-grade drones, and rapid attribution protocols that can move faster than news cycles.

## What to watch

- The Leipzig airport investigation's findings: whether forensic evidence links the explosive drone to Russian or Russian-proxy supply chains would be a significant public escalation
- Germany's review of rules-of-engagement for military-base drone threats — currently handled by civilian police rather than Bundeswehr authority
- Zelensky's latest warnings about Patriot interceptor shortages, and whether the Trump administration produces a clear policy response
- The EU's timeline for a continent-wide drone-defence framework, and whether the Leipzig incident accelerates funding discussions
