The United States and Denmark [signed an agreement on Tuesday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9dr74g32g8go) to build two American military bases in Greenland, formalising what had been months of pressure, negotiation, and considerable diplomatic turbulence. Al Jazeera confirmed the deal as a [US "Arctic security" arrangement](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/22/us-signs-tremendous-arctic-security-deal-with-denmark-greenland) with both Denmark and Greenland's autonomous government as parties. Trump called it "tremendous." Greenland's Prime Minister — representing an island whose population is under 57,000 but whose geostrategic weight grows heavier by the year — had earlier this year resisted what many framed as a unilateral American annexation drive. The resulting deal represents something different: a negotiated security arrangement within NATO's broader framework, formalising US military presence without ceding sovereignty. The distinction is worth taking seriously.

## The received wisdom

The liberal critique of the Greenland deal runs roughly as follows: Trump's interest in Greenland was always fundamentally about acquisition and dominance rather than collective security. By applying economic and rhetorical pressure, Washington essentially coerced a smaller NATO ally into an arrangement it might not have chosen freely. The precedent — that the US can leverage alliance relationships to extract strategic concessions from partners — is troubling for Western cohesion. The argument further notes that any expansion of US military footprint in the Arctic is likely to be read by Moscow as provocative, potentially triggering a counter-escalation in a region that has so far remained relatively stable. The humanitarian and environmental concerns of Greenland's indigenous Inuit population, critics add, barely feature in a geopolitical calculus driven by great-power competition.

## A different read

The liberal critique has genuine force but misreads the underlying strategic reality in a way that matters. The Arctic is not stable, and the fiction of its stability has served mainly to defer necessary choices about how the West secures its northern flank.

Consider the geography. The Arctic Ocean is warming at roughly [four times the global average](https://www.bbc.co.uk/weather/articles/cmvgy4nv9d62o), opening shipping lanes and resource access that were science fiction a generation ago. Russia has been [systematically militarising its Arctic perimeter](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck5yw8npxzrlo) for more than a decade — rebuilding Cold War-era bases, deploying hypersonic missile systems, and conducting exercises designed to contest NATO's northern approaches. Lithuania's Prime Minister told the BBC this week that her country is preparing evacuation plans in the face of Russian threats. The arc from the Baltic to the High North is under real, measurable pressure, not hypothetical tension.

In this context, the Greenland deal is less an act of American imperial overreach and more a belated rationalisation of a gap in Western defence architecture that has been obvious since at least 2014. The US maintained bases in Greenland throughout the Cold War — Thule Air Base, now Pituffik Space Base, has been continuously operational since 1952. What changed is the strategic context, not the basic logic. What is being built is an expansion of an existing posture, not a novel incursion into a virgin strategic space.

The sovereignty question deserves a sharper look. Greenland's autonomous government was a party to the agreement, not simply overridden. This matters. The Trump administration's earlier, more nakedly acquisitive language — floating outright purchase — was categorically different from a negotiated basing arrangement within an existing alliance framework. The fact that Denmark and Greenland extracted what appear to be genuine concessions (the two-base limit, explicit Danish and Greenlandic participation in the deal's terms) suggests a negotiation rather than a diktat. Compare this to Russia's approach in the Arctic — or to China's approach to its contested maritime claims — and the moral distinctions are not trivial.

Where the critique lands more squarely is on process. Trump's personal style — the annexation rhetoric, the economic threats, the "tremendous" self-congratulation — generates diplomatic costs that outlast any individual deal. Allies who are coerced rather than persuaded store up resentment that surfaces in future crises. If the Greenland deal becomes a template for how the US manages its relationships with smaller partners, the longer-term damage to NATO's political cohesion will exceed any short-term security gain. The challenge for those who support the deal's strategic logic is to be honest about what it cost in diplomatic capital, rather than simply declaring victory.

The Arctic is going to be contested regardless of what any particular administration does or says. The real question is whether the West builds a coherent posture for that contest on a foundation of genuine solidarity, or cobbles together a patchwork of bilateral arrangements secured through pressure. Tuesday's deal is a step toward the former but was achieved in a manner more consistent with the latter.

## What to watch

- Whether the Greenlandic parliament ratifies the deal without significant political pushback, which would indicate local elite consensus.
- Russian reaction — specifically whether Moscow uses the announcement to justify further Arctic militarisation or escalatory rhetoric toward Scandinavia.
- The second base's proposed location: a site closer to the Russian Arctic shelf would signal a more aggressive posture than one focused on transatlantic shipping lanes.
- Whether Canada and Norway seek parallel basing arrangements, which would suggest the deal is being treated as a positive model rather than a grudging one-off.
