Andy Burnham, in his first weeks as Prime Minister, has announced that regional mayors will receive a share of income tax revenue, framing the move as giving power to "every postcode" in Britain. [The announcement](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c4gdzlzprv1o) came alongside a broader devolution plan and a budget date set for October, with [Chancellor Healey pledging](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjd4yz35x8go) to move "money and power out of Westminster." Critics, however, [noted the plans lack detail](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c4gdzlzprv1o), and a parallel political story intruded: Burnham [told Trump that the UK would be "pragmatic" about North Sea oil](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gr75xzg5o), signalling a possible softening of the previous government's energy transition posture. The Greater Manchester mayoral by-election, triggered by Burnham's own elevation to Number 10, [was won by Labour's Bev Craig](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/jul/31/labour-wins-decisive-victory-in-greater-manchester-mayoral-byelection) but with the lowest turnout on record, offering a muted rather than triumphant political debut for the new PM's signature region.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for Burnham's devolution agenda is compelling and has been rehearsed effectively for years: Britain is one of the most centralised states in the developed world, with Whitehall controlling fiscal decisions that would in Germany, Canada, or the United States be made regionally; this centralisation produces a democratic deficit, impedes local economic development, and breeds the resentment of "left-behind" communities that has fuelled populist politics since 2016. Burnham's "Manchesterism" — the idea that [the northwest city's model of devolved governance](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyl3z2xld8o) can serve as a template for the whole country — represents a serious attempt to address that structural problem. Giving mayors a share of income tax, rather than just block grants, creates a genuine fiscal incentive for local economic growth: mayors who grow their regional economy collect more revenue, aligning local political incentives with national economic goals in a way that central grants never can.

## A different read

The Burnham devolution argument is the right answer to the right question, which makes the political execution all the more important — and all the more concerning to scrutinise closely. The details matter enormously in tax devolution, and the BBC's reporting that "critics say the plans lack detail" is not merely Opposition carping. The precedent of Scottish devolution is instructive: the Scotland Act 1998 gave Holyrood significant tax and spending powers, but the revenue-assignment mechanism — how Scottish income tax interacted with the Barnett formula — took fifteen years of acrimonious negotiation to partially resolve, with fiscal disputes still unresolved today. English mayoral areas are more diverse in economic profile than Scotland, and the distributional consequences of income tax devolution — which benefits already-prosperous city regions with high median incomes more than post-industrial areas with low tax bases — could produce regional inequalities that are precisely the opposite of what the policy intends.

The Greater Manchester by-election result deserves particular attention. [Bev Craig won](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/jul/31/labour-wins-decisive-victory-in-greater-manchester-mayoral-byelection) in what should be the most favourable possible conditions for a Labour candidate — the most popular Labour politician in the country just vacated the role — yet turnout was the lowest on record for a Greater Manchester mayoral election. Low turnout in a high-profile, high-emotion political moment is not simply a measurement of public apathy; it is data about the gap between the metropolitan political class's enthusiasm for devolution as a governance philosophy and ordinary people's lived experience of local democracy as a mechanism for changing things that matter to them. If Burnham's "Manchesterism" is to work as a national template, it needs to produce visible improvements in housing, transport, and public services that motivate people to vote — and to vote for the devolution architects, not against them.

The North Sea oil comment is the most politically revealing development of Burnham's first weeks. His assurance to Trump that the UK would be "pragmatic" about North Sea resources — [telling the US president he couldn't ignore the basin](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gr75xzg5o) while people struggled with energy costs — represents a significant departure from his predecessor's rhetoric and from the positions he held as a Greater Manchester Mayor. Whether this represents genuine policy evolution in response to the Iran-driven energy shock, or a tactical softening designed to avoid trade friction with Washington, will become clearer when the October Budget sets the actual capital allowance and licensing framework for North Sea development. The [sale of BP's North Sea assets](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/jul/31/bp-north-sea-oil-and-gas-business-up-for-sale) — ending six decades of production — suggests industry has already reached its own conclusions about the basin's future, whatever the government says.

Burnham's political authenticity and his genuine track record in Greater Manchester are real assets that his predecessors in Number 10 lacked. But authenticity is not a fiscal framework. The October Budget will be the true test of whether Burnham's "Manchesterism" is a coherent governing philosophy or a set of politically appealing slogans dressed in the language of economic geography.

## What to watch

- The October Budget: the specific revenue-assignment mechanism for income tax devolution, the fiscal rules, and whether capital investment spending is genuinely devolved or remains Treasury-controlled.
- North Sea policy: whether the government issues new licensing rounds or modifies capital allowances in a way that reverses the basin's decline — and how this is reconciled with climate commitments.
- Reform UK's performance in devolution areas: if Reform can win in areas that Burnham's agenda is supposed to benefit, the political case for "Manchesterism" nationally becomes much harder to make.
- The Anas Sarwar appointment: [Sarwar moving to a cabinet role](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce970y1n7plo) represents a politically significant elevation of a prominent Starmer critic. Whether this is genuine reconciliation or managed absorption of internal opposition will shape the government's coherence over the next 18 months.
