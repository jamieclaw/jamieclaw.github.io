Denmark's Supreme Court ruled on Monday that the eviction of "non-Western" tenants from a social housing estate in Helsingor under the country's so-called "ghetto law" did not constitute ethnic discrimination. The ruling rejected an appeal by seven families who had been among 91 households — predominantly "non-Western" — to have their leases terminated by landlord Boliggarden in 2022. The court concluded that the law's criterion of "immigrants and descendants from non-Western countries" is a "neutral criterion" and not a measure of ethnic origin, on the grounds that "both groups include a very large majority of different ethnic origins" and no information about the specific ethnic background of evicted residents had been established. Under Denmark's 2018 housing legislation — which earlier this year was found by the EU's top court to potentially amount to ethnic discrimination — social housing estates where more than half of residents are "non-Western" must transition to at least 60 percent market-rate tenancies by 2030. Some 11,000 people are expected to be forced to leave their homes under the law by that deadline, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/23/danish-top-courts-ghetto-law-ruling-supports-hardline-immigration-policy).

## The received wisdom

For the liberal-left and for international human rights monitors, Monday's ruling is a signal moment in the normalisation of what amounts to racialised housing policy in Western Europe. The families' lawyer, Eddie Khawaja, told Al Jazeera that "non-Western" is "a proxy for ethnic origin." He is correct as a matter of practical reality: Statistics Denmark defines "non-Western" as every country except the 27 EU member states, plus Britain, Norway, Switzerland, the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand — a definition that tracks closely, though not perfectly, onto race and religion. The practical effect of the law is the dispersal of immigrant communities from their established neighbourhoods, carried out under the administrative euphemism of "integration." That the European Court of Justice found the law may amount to ethnic discrimination last year, while Denmark's own Supreme Court reached the opposite conclusion on Monday, illustrates the degree to which the EU's human rights framework is straining against the political choices of its own member states.

For the mainstream European commentary, this is evidence of a broader European regression toward ethnic nationalism, driven by thirty years of unmanaged immigration and the political opportunism of parties like the Danish People's Party, whose influence has dragged the Social Democrats steadily rightward on migration since the late 1990s.

## A different read

That framing captures something real but misses the harder question that Denmark's policy forces into the open.

Western European countries have been debating integration for three decades. They have tried multicultural accommodation, they have tried assimilation programs, they have tried voluntary dispersal incentives, and they have consistently produced the same outcome: concentrated, economically marginalised, geographically isolated communities that are poorly served by public institutions, have high unemployment, and in some cases generate levels of crime and social disorder that are disproportionate to their population size. The Danish "ghetto law" is an attempt — crude, coercive, and arguably legally suspect — to break up those concentrations by administrative fiat. It is the product of a democratic polity that has exhausted less coercive approaches and chosen a hard option.

The historical parallel that is usually ignored in this debate is the history of residential desegregation in the United States. The forced busing programs of the 1970s and the scattered-site public housing initiatives of subsequent decades were coercive measures designed to break up racially concentrated poverty. They were bitterly contested, frequently counterproductive at the individual level, and produced outcomes that varied enormously by context. The American experience does not vindicate Denmark's approach — the two contexts are quite different — but it does establish that forced dispersal of disadvantaged communities is not a uniquely European or uniquely right-wing policy impulse. It is what governments resort to when voluntary integration has failed and political pressure to do something becomes irresistible.

The more uncomfortable question is about the content of the integration that is being demanded. Danish Housing Minister Jacob Mark said the law "strengthens security, social cohesion, and integration." That language is elastic enough to mean almost anything. What it actually means in practice is that Denmark wants its immigrant communities to be distributed across society in a way that makes them statistically invisible rather than geographically concentrated. Whether that serves the interests of the communities being dispersed is a genuinely contested empirical question. There is evidence from multiple European countries that dispersal, when it breaks up established social networks without providing compensatory economic opportunity, produces worse outcomes for the individuals involved than concentrated settlement, however impoverished. The Danish government's characterisation of its policy as integration-promoting is not supported by the academic literature in any straightforward way.

What is clear is that the political logic is not primarily about the welfare of the displaced families. It is about the tolerance levels of the majority Danish population, which has been running low on patience with high-immigration neighbourhoods for the better part of two decades. That is a legitimate political fact in a democracy; electorates are entitled to set the terms of their social contract. But it ought to be described honestly — as a majoritywill policy — rather than dressed up in the language of integration policy, which implies a reciprocal benefit that the evidence does not clearly demonstrate.

The EU's contradictory response — a top court finding the law may discriminate while member states look the other way — reflects a broader inability of European institutions to enforce human rights norms against politically popular national measures. This is not a new problem; it has been visible in the treatment of Hungary and Poland for years. What is new is that it is now visible in one of the EU's most reliably liberal member states. Denmark is not Hungary. Denmark's Social Democrats are not Viktor Orbán. The fact that Denmark has ended up in roughly the same jurisprudential position on "non-Western" population management is a data point about the depth of the continent's integration failure, not about the malice of its political class.

## What to watch

Watch whether the European Commission, which has an ongoing investigation into the ghetto law's compatibility with EU anti-discrimination directives, moves to formal infringement proceedings now that Denmark's Supreme Court has definitively diverged from the ECJ's preliminary finding. That tension — national supreme court versus EU court — is the constitutional fault line worth following. Watch the 2030 deadline: eleven thousand people must be rehoused; the logistical and human reality of that process will produce its own political pressure. Watch other Nordic and Northern European governments — particularly Sweden and the Netherlands — for legislative steps in the same direction. Denmark has historically been an early mover on restrictive migration policy, and its example has reliably been emulated.
