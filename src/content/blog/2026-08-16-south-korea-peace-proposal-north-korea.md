South Korean President Lee Jae Myung used the country's Liberation Day ceremonies on Saturday to propose formal talks with North Korea aimed at officially ending the Korean War — a conflict that has remained technically active since an armistice was signed in 1953. Speaking at a ceremony marking the peninsula's liberation from Japanese colonial rule, Lee called for a "multi-layered security system" to reduce tensions along the demilitarised zone (DMZ) and offered discussions on Pyongyang's nuclear programme. He said the two nations should "sit face-to-face for peaceful coexistence and mutual growth." [North Korea had not responded](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8en2z9jp2xo) by the time of writing; state media reported Kim Jong Un conducting Liberation Day commemorations with a wreath-laying and guard of honour, with no reference to the South's offer. Days earlier, North Korea had fired a ballistic missile into the sea — its eleventh suspected test this year — shortly before a scheduled US-South Korean joint military exercise.

## The received wisdom

Lee's offer will be welcomed in progressive foreign-policy circles as a long-overdue attempt to replace the hawkish escalation of his impeached predecessor Yoon Suk Yeol with pragmatic engagement. The argument runs: North Korea's nuclear programme is the product of insecurity, and insecurity can be reduced by credible diplomatic signals. A formal peace treaty would remove a legal pretext for continued military confrontation, reduce the risk of accidental escalation along the DMZ, and create space for the gradual economic integration that has, historically, accompanied normalisation on other divided peninsulas. Lee's election after Yoon's extraordinary attempt to declare martial law last year restored civilian democratic norms, and part of restoring those norms is replacing confrontation with diplomacy.

There is also a Trump factor here. Lee has explicitly backed the idea of a deal between Trump and Kim Jong Un to freeze nuclear production. With Trump having expressed continued interest in meeting Kim again, and with the Iran war absorbing significant US attention, a Lee-brokered framework could in theory give Washington a diplomatic opening it might otherwise not take.

## A different read

The problem with this analysis is not that it is wrong about the potential value of diplomacy. It is that it consistently underweights the structural reasons why Pyongyang has rejected engagement at every turn, and what those refusals reveal about North Korea's strategic calculus.

[The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8en2z9jp2xo) that in January 2024, Kim Jong Un formally announced that unification with the South was no longer possible, amended the North Korean constitution to designate South Korea a "primary foe and invariable principal enemy," and said that if war broke out, the North should aim to "occupy, recapture, and incorporate" the South. This was not a diplomatic signal designed to extract concessions. It was a doctrinal shift — a deliberate foreclosure of the reunification frame that has defined inter-Korean relations since at least the 1972 Joint Communiqué.

Understanding why Kim made that shift is essential. North Korea declared itself a nuclear power in 2022 and vowed never to surrender its weapons. It is estimated to possess dozens of warheads and, by Lee's own account, produces 15 to 20 more annually. This is not a programme that exists to deter invasion in the manner South Korea or Japan might deter a conventional threat. It is a strategic insurance policy against the regime-change playbook that Pyongyang saw applied to non-nuclear states — Libya and Iraq — in the 2000s. No paper peace treaty changes that calculus. A peace declaration does not dismantle the nuclear arsenal; it simply removes a rhetorical justification for keeping it.

There is a deeper problem for Lee specifically. His political coalition came to power partly in opposition to Yoon's hawkishness, which means he is under domestic pressure to produce diplomatic results. But the price Kim would demand for any meaningful negotiation — a halt to US-South Korean joint exercises, delays or cancellations of the nuclear submarine deal, loosening of sanctions — would each damage the alliance architecture that makes South Korean security viable. Lee is trying to thread a needle that previous South Korean leaders, including the architect of the Sunshine Policy Kim Dae Jung, found impossible to keep threaded once Pyongyang concluded that engagement gave it what it needed without requiring reciprocal restraint.

The regional picture is also complicated by the Iran war, which has drawn US carrier capacity toward the Middle East and raised legitimate questions about alliance commitments in Northeast Asia. [South Korea's Incheon Airport is reportedly now the busiest in the world for global air traffic](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/13/south-korea-incheon-airport-iran-war-middle-east), a direct consequence of the Iran conflict rerouting aviation. That tells you something about the strategic environment Lee is navigating: a stretched American ally, a resurgent Chinese relationship he is also cultivating, and a nuclear-armed North that has just test-fired its eleventh missile of the year. The peace offer is politically understandable. Whether it will move Pyongyang is another question.

## What to watch

Watch, first, whether North Korea issues any official response to Lee's proposal within the next two weeks — Pyongyang's silence will itself be a signal. Second, whether Trump's State Department endorses or distances itself from Lee's framework, particularly on the question of joint exercises. Third, whether China uses the Liberation Day moment to publicly back engagement, which would tell us something about Beijing's interest in managing the Kim-Trump dynamic as US attention remains on the Middle East. Fourth, the next North Korean missile test — if another comes before any diplomatic response, that will answer the question of whether Pyongyang sees any merit in Lee's offer.
