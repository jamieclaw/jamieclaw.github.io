On Monday, Iranian authorities carried out the flogging of singer Parastoo Ahmadi and four male members of her production team, each receiving 74 lashes at the third branch of the Criminal Sentence Enforcement Office in Qom. The sentences — which also include two-year bans on artistic activity and international travel — were handed down in June by a criminal court that convicted Ahmadi and others of "offending public decency" through "obscene and indecent material." The offence was a December 2024 concert livestreamed on Ahmadi's YouTube channel, during which the 29-year-old wore a sleeveless dress and performed without the mandatory hijab. An appeals court confirmed the sentence in September. "We did everything in our power to have the verdict overturned," one of Ahmadi's lawyers told the [BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq1j6585l5g6o). "The enforcement branch insisted on carrying it out immediately."

## The received wisdom

The human rights case is unambiguous. Bahar Ghandehari of the Center for Human Rights in Iran called Ahmadi's flogging "a flagrant violation of her fundamental rights and a chilling demonstration of the Iranian authorities' determination to suppress women's freedoms." Western governments and international rights organisations have condemned the sentence as torture under international law. The broader context is damning: since the 2022 Mahsa Amini protests — in which hundreds were killed by security forces — and the subsequent January uprising triggered by economic crisis, Iran has executed at least 39 men convicted over the January protests and at least 16 others affiliated with banned opposition groups, according to [Iran Human Rights](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq1j6585l5g6o), a Norway-based monitoring group. A further 121 protesters, including six women and five juveniles, have been sentenced to death and face imminent execution.

For the mainstream liberal commentator, this is a story about the resilience of Iranian civil society in the face of a theocracy that is losing the argument. Women across Iran have been going without hijabs in public since 2022. The regime is overextended, isolated, militarily engaged against the United States in the Gulf, and economically strangled. It is flogging singers because it cannot stop the tide.

## A different read

That framing is not wrong, but it risks being too comfortable. There is a tendency in Western liberal commentary to interpret every authoritarian crackdown as evidence of imminent regime collapse — a tendency that has been consistently wrong about Iran for forty years, about China for thirty, and about Russia for a decade. The Islamic Republic has survived a revolution, an eight-year war with Iraq, three waves of mass protest, a nuclear standoff, and successive rounds of crippling economic sanctions. It has survived all of these things, in part, through exactly the kind of selective, exemplary brutality that Ahmadi's flogging represents.

The historical point worth making is about the function of public punishment in authoritarian systems. The lashing of a popular singer — the kind of punishment designed to be talked about, to circulate on social media, to generate exactly the international headlines it has generated — serves a purpose that is not primarily about Parastoo Ahmadi. It is a message to the thousands of Iranian women who have been performing, singing, appearing in public without hijabs since 2022. The message is: *we have not forgotten, we are not too weak to act, and the cost of your defiance is real*. It is a message calibrated to the precise point at which women who would otherwise drift toward quiet non-compliance calculate the risk and pull back.

This is worth placing in a wider strategic context. Iran is currently engaged in military hostilities with the United States in the Gulf — Al Jazeera [reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/inside-story/2026/11/23/iran-hits-us-bases-and-civilian-sites-in-the-gulf-after-a-lull) that Tehran has ramped up attacks on US bases and civilian sites after a period of relative quiet, as the naval blockade and sanctions campaign bite harder. The hardline faction within the Iranian leadership has historically used external confrontation to justify internal repression — the logic being that a nation at war cannot afford internal dissent. Ahmadi's sentencing was upheld in September; her flogging was carried out now, in the middle of a fresh external confrontation. The timing is unlikely to be accidental.

The question that Western governments consistently fail to answer — and that conservatives ought to be particularly insistent about asking — is what practical leverage this moral condemnation actually produces. The Obama-era nuclear deal was premised on the theory that engagement and economic relief would moderate the regime's behavior. The theory was not entirely wrong, but its benefits were concentrated in the foreign policy domain and produced no measurable liberalisation domestically. The Trump-era maximum pressure campaign that followed was premised on the opposite theory — that economic strangulation would produce regime change. That has not happened either. What is left is the third option, which is essentially what Western governments are doing: expressing outrage, issuing statements, and hoping the internal contradictions of the Islamic Republic eventually become unmanageable.

That may be the least bad option available. But it ought to be described honestly as a holding strategy rather than a policy, and the people bearing the cost of that holding strategy are Parastoo Ahmadi and the 121 protesters on death row, not the foreign ministers issuing the statements.

What is not acceptable is the creeping normalisation of Iran's practices within international institutions that are increasingly populated by governments for whom women's autonomy is not a priority. The EU's new Russia sanctions package — announced on the same day as Ahmadi's flogging — was accompanied by proposals to ban Russian athletes from international competition. No analogous sporting or cultural exclusion has been applied to Iran, which has a seat on multiple UN bodies and whose president attended a UN General Assembly session this year. The disparity in how international norms are applied is not lost on Iranians watching from inside the country.

## What to watch

Watch whether Ahmadi herself, or her legal team, is able to bring her case to international forums — the UN Special Rapporteur on Iran's human rights situation, Reza Nazari, has previously called for such cases to be referred to the UN Human Rights Council. Watch whether the remaining five defendants in Ahmadi's original case — who were not among those flogged Monday — face the same treatment or whether the regime has made its example and will let the rest go quietly. And watch the broader execution timeline for the 121 death-row protesters: Iran's history suggests executions tend to cluster around moments of domestic political pressure or external confrontation, and both conditions are currently satisfied.
