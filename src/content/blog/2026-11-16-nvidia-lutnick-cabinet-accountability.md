The Nvidia crisis took a new turn this week as US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly called for the company to bring in an outside leader, while simultaneously criticising Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick for what the Treasury described as a failure to act decisively on the national security implications of Jensen Huang's alleged relationship with a suspected Chinese intelligence operative. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/15/us-treasury-chief-calls-for-outsider-to-lead-embattled-tech-firm-nvidia) the Treasury chief's remarks, and the BBC noted that [pressure on Lutnick was growing in the fallout from the Nvidia scandal](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqvg2d1eg7zro). Nvidia has [hired a major US law firm to investigate the alleged spy affair](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck1wvwwl3x0go), and the BBC reported that the [ex-Nvidia CEO has been summoned to testify before Congress](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmq5vd1wdgz6o). Interim CEO Colette Kress is now managing a company whose share price has fallen sharply, whose largest customer relationships in China are under new scrutiny, and whose core export-control compliance is being reviewed by multiple federal agencies simultaneously.

## The received wisdom

The story as it is typically told in the financial and technology press is a corporate governance failure with national security implications: a brilliant but reckless founder who allowed personal relationships to override professional judgment, a board that failed in its fiduciary duty, and a government that was too dazzled by Nvidia's strategic importance — the company's chips underpin most of the AI infrastructure on earth — to scrutinise its security culture adequately. The Commerce Department, which is responsible for semiconductor export controls, emerges as the villain of institutional complacency. This reading makes Lutnick's discomfort politically legible: he inherited a brief that was already on fire and is now being blamed for not having the fire service ready.

## A different read

That framing is accurate as far as it goes, but it systematically underweights two deeper structural problems that the Nvidia scandal has laid bare.

The first is the fundamental tension in US semiconductor policy between treating chip technology as a strategic national asset and allowing the companies that make chips to operate as normal market actors. Since the 2022 CHIPS Act, Washington has made enormous investments in domestic semiconductor manufacturing on the explicit premise that chips are as strategically important as munitions. If that premise is correct — and it almost certainly is — then the governance standards applied to Nvidia and its peers should look more like the governance standards applied to defence contractors than those applied to consumer technology companies. Defence contractors undergo continuous security reviews of executive relationships, foreign contacts, and personnel with access to classified programmes. Nvidia, whose A100 chips were [reportedly found in Russian military equipment in Ukraine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqvg2d1eg7zro), operated until recently without anything resembling comparable oversight.

The Treasury-Commerce friction exposed this week is partly personal — Bessent and Lutnick are reported to have a contentious relationship — but it is also structural. The Commerce Department controls export licences; the Treasury controls financial sanctions; the Justice Department runs espionage prosecutions; the NSC coordinates intelligence; the Pentagon has its own equities through DARPA and the defence industrial base. No single agency owns the "chip security" problem. This fragmentation is not new, but it is newly dangerous in a world where a single company's chips power both the world's AI systems and China's military modernisation programme.

The second structural problem is about the political economy of tech regulation more broadly. The Trump administration came to office with a broadly deregulatory philosophy toward tech, and several of its key figures — including Lutnick — have strong personal and professional ties to Silicon Valley. The reluctance to move decisively against Nvidia is not simply a failure of one cabinet secretary; it reflects a White House that was ideologically committed to the proposition that the tech sector is a national champion to be championed, not a risk to be managed. The scandal forces a reckoning with that proposition. If Nvidia cannot be trusted to self-regulate its national security exposure, what other assumptions about tech self-governance need to be revisited?

The [Guardian noted](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/13/nvidia-scandal-sparks-us-national-security-concerns-china) that the scandal has sparked national security concerns specifically about the depth of Chinese intelligence penetration of Silicon Valley's executive class. This is a problem that predates Jensen Huang's particular situation and will outlast it. The question is whether Washington can build the institutional architecture to address it without strangling the innovation economy it depends on — a genuinely difficult balance that neither the deregulatory right nor the regulatory left has fully thought through.

## What to watch

The congressional testimony will be the most watched near-term event, but the more consequential developments will be the structural ones: whether the administration moves to establish an intelligence-sharing framework between Commerce, Treasury, and NSC specifically for semiconductor companies; whether Nvidia's export licences for China-adjacent markets are modified; and whether Colette Kress, as interim CEO, is given a mandate for genuine security reform or is simply a holding figure while the board searches for a permanent replacement. Watch also for any signal from the White House on whether Lutnick retains full confidence — his public humiliation by the Treasury secretary is not something that typically happens to cabinet members who remain in good standing.
