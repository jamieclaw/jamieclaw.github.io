The number of asylum seekers housed in hotels in the United Kingdom halved in the year to June 2026, falling from more than 32,000 in June 2025 to more than 16,000, according to [Home Office data published by the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3relr4xx27o). Asylum claims fell by a fifth to 86,000 in the year to June — still elevated by historical standards — and small boat arrivals across the English Channel fell in both absolute numbers and as a trend. The number of people removed from the UK rose 8%, reaching 40,000 in the year to June 2026, the highest removal rate for sixteen years. Work visas fell 18% year-on-year and are 62% below the peak in December 2023. Student visas fell 11% and are 41% below the June 2023 peak. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood said the figures showed that "policies to control migration were having an effect," while acknowledging there was "more we must do" on small boats. Some 38,000 illegal arrivals were detected in the year to June — 33,000 of them on small boats — against a backlog that peaked at 175,000 pending asylum decisions in June 2023 and has now fallen to 40,000.

## The received wisdom

The progressive critique of the government's migration policy argues that the headline hotel figures obscure more than they reveal. The number of asylum seekers in all Home Office-funded accommodation actually rose slightly, from 65,000 at end-2024 to 69,000 in June 2026 — people have moved from hotels to houses and military bases, not out of public housing entirely. The shadow home secretary's line about Labour "moving illegal immigrants out of hotels and into flats in your building" was cheap but captured a genuine point: the reduction in hotel use is partly an accounting change in how asylum seekers are accommodated, not purely a reduction in the caseload. Critics also note that the 33,000 small boat arrivals in a single year remains a very large number by any historical comparison, and that the refugee family reunion restrictions — which a High Court has challenged — deny genuine refugees the chance to rebuild their lives in order to produce marginal deterrence effects. The humanitarian cost of those restrictions falls on some of the most vulnerable people in the system.

## A different read

All of that is true, and yet the consistent refusal of progressive commentators to acknowledge the scale of the improvement — and the policy work that produced it — is its own form of bad faith that undermines the credibility of the humanitarian critique.

The backlog reduction from 175,000 to 40,000 is a genuine administrative achievement. The decision by the incoming Starmer government in July 2024 to order faster processing of claims — rather than allowing the case to age while the applicant remains in expensive hotel accommodation — addressed a structural dysfunction that had been building for years. A faster system is both cheaper and fairer: people who have a genuine right to stay know sooner; people who do not are removed faster. The removal rate of 40,000 in a year — an 8% increase over the previous year and double the 2021 rate — represents a real operational effort, even if it falls short of what critics demand.

The work visa figures are also striking for a different reason. A 62% reduction from the 2023 peak, driven in large part by tightening of health and care worker visas and restrictions on family reunion, represents a significant shift in the composition of migration. The health and care worker route — which fell 90% following progressive restrictions — had been used at enormous scale under the Johnson government's liberalisation, producing a pattern in which tens of thousands of overseas workers filled NHS and social care gaps that UK domestic workforce policy had created. The reduction in that route has not been accompanied by either a domestic workforce strategy or a serious reckoning with the social care staffing crisis. Cutting the visa without fixing the underlying demand is the immigration policy equivalent of treating a fever with cold water.

The political dynamics are worth examining separately from the policy outcomes. Reform UK's home affairs spokesman declared Britain at "breaking point" in response to figures showing the fastest removal rate in sixteen years and a halved hotel population. That reaction tells you something important: the migration debate in Britain is no longer primarily about numbers. It is about cultural anxiety, the pace of demographic change, and the sense that established institutions — government, courts, the civil service — are consistently working to frustrate public preferences. The Lib Dem suggestion that Labour should "join the EU migration pact" as the solution, meanwhile, demonstrates that the centre-left has not yet internalised the political cost of being seen to outsource domestic policy to Brussels. The EU pact is not obviously more restrictive than current UK policy; it is simply managed through a different institutional framework.

The honest assessment is this: the Burnham government has made genuine progress on the metrics it can most directly control — processing speed, hotel numbers, removal rates, visa volumes. It has made less progress on the metric the public cares most about — small boat crossings — because small boats require either a durable deterrence arrangement with France or an offshore processing regime, neither of which is politically easy or legally straightforward. The Rwanda scheme of the previous Conservative government was litigated to death before it processed a single applicant. An alternative arrangement has not yet emerged. Until it does, the political gap between the data (improving) and the public perception (broken) will persist.

## What to watch

- The next quarterly migration statistics, due in late November, which will be the first to show whether the improving trends are sustained or whether recent political developments — including the early release scheme and its associated coverage — have affected public confidence and irregular migration patterns.
- High Court rulings on the refugee family reunion restrictions: if the courts strike them down, the government will face a significant political and operational challenge in managing the family reunion caseload without the deterrence effect those restrictions were intended to provide.
- The small boats question ahead of winter: crossing numbers typically fall in October-March due to weather, then rise again. The government's approach to the 2027 crossing season will reveal whether any new operational arrangements with France have been reached.
- The Labour Party conference in late September, where the migration figures are likely to feature as one of the few clear positive announcements in an otherwise difficult economic environment.
