Israeli judges have [recommended dropping the most serious charges](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cxj05mv723r2o) against Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in his long-running corruption trial, a development Netanyahu himself has called long overdue, [publicly declaring that the trial "must end"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/25/netanyahu-says-corruption-trial-must-end-as-court-suggests-dropping-case). The case — a sprawling set of allegations involving regulatory favours to media proprietors in exchange for favourable coverage, alongside gifts from wealthy benefactors — has shadowed Israeli politics for the better part of a decade, surviving elections, coalition collapses, and a full-scale war in Gaza. The judges' recommendation stops well short of full exoneration; charges relating to fraud and breach of trust remain on the table, and the attorney general must still decide how to proceed. But the development materially changes the political and legal calculus for a prime minister whose survival strategy has been intertwined, in critics' view, with his interest in remaining in office to prevent conviction.

## The received wisdom

The liberal democratic critique of Netanyahu has been consistent and is not without foundation: that a sitting leader facing serious criminal charges lacks the moral standing to govern, that his coalition's judicial reform agenda was partly motivated by self-interested desire to defang the courts, and that the conflation of his personal legal interest with state security policy — including, in some readings, prolongation of the Gaza war — represents precisely the kind of institutional corruption that constitutional democracies are designed to prevent. This critique is widely shared across European capitals, in significant sections of the American foreign policy establishment, and within the Israeli opposition. It draws on a genuine tradition of liberal constitutionalism: that no one, however powerful or however great their claim on national security grounds, should be immune to legal accountability, and that the pressure a sitting PM can exert on the institutions prosecuting him is itself a corrupting influence regardless of the underlying guilt or innocence.

## A different read

The case for taking the judges' recommendation seriously — not as a political outcome but as a legal one — rests on the procedural integrity of the courts themselves.

It is worth noting what the judges actually said: not that Netanyahu was innocent or that the charges were fabricated, but that the most serious charge — bribery — did not meet the evidential threshold the law requires for conviction. That is a substantive legal finding, not a political one, and it deserves to be treated as such. Israeli courts have demonstrated considerable institutional courage throughout this period: they have repeatedly rejected government attempts to limit judicial review, they have ruled against the government on Gaza policy matters, and their professional integrity has been consistently rated highly by comparative legal scholars even as the political pressure on them intensified. The fact that the same court system is now recommending a partial dismissal is therefore not obvious evidence of capture; it may simply be evidence that the specific charge struggled on its legal merits.

The broader rule-of-law concern cuts in multiple directions. Those who argue that Netanyahu must be convicted to demonstrate that Israeli democracy functions are, perhaps inadvertently, articulating a standard under which the courts must reach a particular political outcome regardless of the evidence — which is precisely the opposite of judicial independence. The rule of law is upheld not by ensuring that politically inconvenient defendants are convicted, but by ensuring that the same evidentiary and procedural standards apply regardless of the defendant's identity or political power. If the evidence for the bribery charge was insufficient, dismissing it is the correct judicial outcome even if Netanyahu is the defendant.

None of this rehabilitates the judicial reform project, which was a genuine assault on institutional independence and rightly provoked mass protests. Nor does it resolve the deeper question — raised with particular sharpness by observers like [Anshel Pfeffer](https://www.haaretz.com/israel-news/2024-10-09/ty-article/.premium/netanyahu-at-75-the-rise-fall-and-rise-of-israels-most-controversial-prime-minister/00000192-5e6e-d360-a3ff-dee7efff0000) at Haaretz — of whether a leader whose domestic legal survival has become entangled with wartime executive power is capable of making decisions purely on national security grounds. That is a political question the Israeli electorate must ultimately answer. But the legal question — whether the evidence supported a bribery conviction — is a different question, and conflating the two is the kind of motivated reasoning that weakens the very constitutional culture it purports to defend.

The remaining charges (fraud, breach of trust) are still alive, and they carry their own significant moral weight. A leader found to have systematically abused regulatory power for personal gain, even if acquitted of the most dramatic charge, has still failed the standard of conduct democracy requires. The partial dismissal is not exoneration; Netanyahu would do well not to treat it as such.

## What to watch

Watch the attorney general's decision on whether to pursue the remaining charges with the same vigour or treat the judges' recommendation as a signal to negotiate a plea arrangement. Watch whether the development alters Netanyahu's calculus on ceasefire negotiations in Gaza — the theory that he prolonged the conflict for legal reasons loses some force if the most serious charge is gone, but the politics of the far-right coalition partners who would punish any deal remain unchanged. Watch the Israeli protest movement's response: the judicial reform battle may be re-energised if the partial dismissal is perceived as the courts retreating under political pressure rather than ruling on evidence. And watch the coalition's next legislative move on judicial appointments — the structural fight over who controls the courts is far from over.
