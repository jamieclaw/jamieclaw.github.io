The leaders of the United States, China, and Russia convened in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the APEC summit and emerged with what their spokespeople called a historic "peace declaration." [Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c623j4ln97x7o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) stood together for photographs that will be reproduced in textbooks for decades, whatever the declaration ultimately proves to be worth. The joint communiqué called for "dialogue over confrontation," pledged respect for "national sovereignty and territorial integrity," and urged a "rules-based international order" — all phrases each power applies selectively when convenient. Notably absent from the text, as [The Guardian confirmed](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/19/china-russia-us-agree-peace-statement-no-mention-ukraine-iran-wars), was any mention of the war in Ukraine or the still-smoldering conflict with Iran. European leaders, who were not in the room, were left to describe themselves as "ready for talks" on Ukraine while simultaneously processing the uncomfortable optics of their most powerful ally smiling alongside the man shelling Kyiv.

## The received wisdom

For the foreign policy establishment that has dominated Western thinking since the Cold War ended, the Shenzhen summit is either a diplomatic triumph or a dangerous charade, and opinion divides roughly on partisan lines. Those sympathetic to the Trump administration argue that only direct superpower engagement can unlock the stalemate in Ukraine — that Zelensky cannot negotiate his way to peace without Washington's blessing and Moscow's willingness, and that bringing China into the tent creates accountability Beijing has so far avoided. The conventional multilateralist view, expressed forcefully by [Al Jazeera's inside-story panel](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/inside-story/2026/11/19/will-the-shenzhen-summit-safeguard-world-peace?traffic_source=rss), is more cautiously optimistic: even a vague declaration signals a floor below which relations will not fall, reducing the probability of miscalculation. And serious liberal internationalists acknowledge that the UN framework has been paralysed by great-power vetoes; some triangular summit mechanism, however imperfect, is better than the silence of mutual antagonism. These are not frivolous points, and they deserve honest engagement.

## A different read

The document's silences are more instructive than its text. A peace declaration that refuses to name the two ongoing wars its signatories are most implicated in is not a peace declaration — it is a press release. And the specific architecture of what was left out suggests something more calculated than diplomatic vagueness.

Consider what the omission of Ukraine achieves for each party. For Putin, it allows him to stand in a photograph evoking superpower solidarity while his forces continue to advance incrementally along the Donbas line and while he simultaneously [vows "far more painful" responses](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm879dqn3e1jo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) to Ukrainian strikes on the Crimean Bridge. The declaration asks nothing of him that he has not already ignored in a dozen previous diplomatic formulations. For Xi, the summit cements China's role as indispensable broker — the essential third party without whose participation no durable settlement is possible — without committing Beijing to any position that might anger Moscow or cost it the economic leverage it currently enjoys over both sides. For Trump, the photographs deliver exactly what his political brand has always required: imagery of strength, of deals being made, of the president of the United States once again seated at the table that matters. The declaration's emptiness is a feature, not a bug, because it permits each leader to return home claiming victory without conceding anything.

This is a pattern with deep historical precedent. The [Helsinki Accords of 1975](https://www.osce.org/mc/39501) are frequently cited as a model for triangular diplomacy — an agreement that normalized Soviet borders while extracting human-rights commitments the USSR would spend the next decade violating and dissidents would spend the same decade invoking. Helsinki was not worthless: the rights language mattered eventually, however slowly. But Helsinki's value depended on sustained Western pressure and civil society networks within the Soviet bloc that do not have obvious analogues today. The analogy the Shenzhen summit's architects seem to have in mind is more flattering and less rigorous.

What is genuinely worrying is the European position. [BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckzd9nln5e6go?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) confirms that European leaders were not party to the Shenzhen process and are now in the uncomfortable position of having to welcome a framework they had no hand in designing. The language of "Europe ready for talks" is diplomatically polite for "Europe was sidelined." The continent that bears the highest direct cost from continued war in Ukraine — through energy prices, refugee flows, defence expenditures, and the persistent risk of escalation — is now a spectator at the negotiations that may determine whether the continent's eastern flank remains stable for the next generation.

There is also a structural asymmetry that optimists about the declaration are eliding. The United States can afford a symbolic gesture because it has ocean buffers; Russia and China each gain legitimacy from the format; but Europe takes all the downside risk if the declaration proves an alibi for continued aggression dressed in the language of peace. That asymmetry is not incidental. It is a reasonable description of how American foreign policy has approached Europe since at least 2017.

## What to watch

Watch whether the European Union formally requests observer status or a parallel track in any follow-on summit process — and watch how Washington responds. If the answer is no, or evasive, the declaration is a bilateral arrangement between the US, Russia, and China dressed in multilateral costume. Watch the detail of any Ukraine-specific framework that may emerge: does it involve territory freezes, referenda, or simply a ceasefire line? The difference between those options is the difference between a frozen conflict and a durable settlement. Watch Putin's battlefield behaviour in the weeks after Shenzhen — declarations extracted under diplomatic pressure tend to be tested immediately by the party most willing to defect. And watch whether Xi uses the summit as leverage in the trade negotiations simultaneously underway with Washington, or whether the two tracks are genuinely separate.
