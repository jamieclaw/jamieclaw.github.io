France's high-school students have been setting their own schools on fire. What began as blockades and sit-ins in the Paris suburbs last week has, by 1 October 2026, grown into a nationwide insurrection involving roughly [900 demonstrations, blockades, and related incidents](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/01/france-school-protests-escalate-pm-crisis-cabinet-meeting) outside schools across the country. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu called an emergency crisis cabinet meeting and ordered ministers to cancel travel plans. The government warned of "urban violence" and said many schools would shift to online classes to protect staff and pupils. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/1/school-protests-spread-as-fires-blockades-deepen-unrest-in-france) that fires were set at French schools as students protested nationwide. Students cite overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, and crumbling facilities. The government's response has been hesitation followed by a crisis meeting — which is to say, the standard French repertoire.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic reading — and there is genuine substance to it — is that French education is in crisis. Teacher salaries have stagnated. Class sizes in lycées in suburban *banlieues* are often 35 or more. The physical infrastructure of many French secondary schools was built in the 1970s, has not been adequately maintained, and would shame a middle-income country. Students who have spent six years in these conditions, and who watched their parents' generation protest the pension reform in 2023 and accomplish nothing, have understandably concluded that conventional channels are deaf. The blockade tradition in French student politics is old and even, in a certain light, honourable — this is a country that takes its history of civil contestation seriously.

The progressive analysis adds that the French Republican model of universalism has failed its most vulnerable students: those in *zones d'éducation prioritaire* who face structural disadvantages but are offered the same rigidly centralised curriculum as pupils in bourgeois arrondissements. Reform, on this reading, requires more resources, more flexibility, more local autonomy — all reasonable prescriptions.

## A different read

Reasonable prescriptions, however, are not what is being prosecuted on the streets. Students are not blockading schools with position papers on pedagogy. They are setting buildings alight and blocking teachers from entering classrooms. The *form* of the protest is not incidental to its meaning.

France has a recurring problem that the left-liberal consensus tends to euphemise. The French state, having expanded relentlessly since the Fifth Republic, has simultaneously become less capable of doing its core jobs: educating children, policing streets, maintaining infrastructure. The contradiction is not accidental. When a state promises everything — universal education, universal healthcare, universal pensions — and then delivers bureaucratic mediocrity at each junction, it trains its citizens to regard its institutions with contempt. The students burning their schools are not revolutionaries with a programme; they are expressing, in the most visceral available idiom, that they do not believe the institutions they inhabit will serve them.

The Élysée and Matignon have, for three years, managed a rolling series of reform panics: the pension crisis, the immigration legislation, the agricultural protests, and now this. What unites these episodes is not a single malcontent constituency but a broad erosion of institutional trust. [The BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6d080d1e3l4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that fires broke out at French schools as students protested nationwide — a detail that many metropolitan commentators have quietly buried under the more telegenic images of banner-waving protesters.

The historical parallel worth invoking is not May 1968 — that was an ideological rupture with a coherent (if wrong) programme — but the banlieue riots of 2005, which began as an expression of specific grievances and became a generalised expression of abandonment. France eventually suppressed the 2005 disorder without addressing its causes. The same response is being mobilised today. The difference is that in 2005 the rioters were predominantly young men from the margins of the Republic; today the protests are drawing from a wider pool, including pupils at mainstream lycées in provincial cities.

A government with genuine reforming instincts would use this crisis as leverage for real structural change: greater school autonomy, a genuine audit of the *baccalauréat*'s fitness for purpose, and merit-based teacher compensation that rewards effectiveness rather than seniority. These reforms have been on every French policy table for twenty years and been killed by the same combination of union resistance and political cowardice. Lecornu's crisis cabinet meeting will produce the usual communiqué of constructive dialogue. The schools will reopen, the fires will be investigated, and in three years another generation will blockade another set of crumbling institutions.

## What to watch

- Whether the government makes any concrete concessions on teacher pay or class sizes that go beyond symbolic gesture — a real budget commitment would be a signal of genuine reform intent, but the fiscal position makes this unlikely.
- The role of teachers' unions: if they support the students (as some have already signalled), the government faces a structurally much harder confrontation than a student revolt alone.
- Whether the disorder spreads to universities, which have their own grievances about funding and precarity — France's history of student–worker coalitions in protest cycles is long.
- The political beneficiary: the Rassemblement National has consistently outpolled the centre among young voters in outer-ring cities. A prolonged sense of institutional failure feeds their narrative.
