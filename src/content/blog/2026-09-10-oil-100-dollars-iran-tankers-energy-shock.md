Brent crude oil crossed $100 a barrel on Wednesday for the first time since July, [according to the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cyvznqypz0yo), after the United States destroyed five Iranian oil tankers in the latest round of strikes connected to the ongoing US-Iran conflict. UK petrol prices rose by 5p in a single week, with a litre of unleaded reaching 167.17p — the largest weekly increase since April, [BBC Business reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj4jwlx77lro). The milestone matters not just as a commodity number but as a visible transmission of geopolitical risk into household budgets across the industrialised world. Meanwhile, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/09/g-s1-142496/up-first-newsletter-iran-us-uk-israel-palestine-lonnie-bunch-smithsonian) that the US military confirmed it destroyed the five Iranian vessels as part of its continuing operations against Iranian energy exports.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream case for the US strikes on Iranian tankers goes something like this: Iran has spent years financing proxy warfare across the Middle East — funding Hezbollah, the Houthis, Hamas, and a network of militias that have killed American service members and destabilised American allies. Economic pressure, including targeting the oil revenues that pay for those proxies, is a legitimate and proportionate tool. If Iran's tankers are carrying oil that funds rockets fired at Israel, then intercepting that revenue is not warmongering — it's supply-chain counterterrorism. The $100 oil price, the argument continues, is the market's rational response to supply uncertainty, and should be laid at Tehran's door, not Washington's. Besides, the US is now largely energy-independent, so the blowback to American consumers is more limited than it once would have been.

That reading is serious, not silly. It deserves to be engaged before it is questioned.

## A different read

Here is what the $100 oil price actually measures: the cost of a military campaign being waged without a formal declaration of war, without a clear political objective, and apparently with an expiry date calibrated to the US midterm elections rather than to strategic logic.

On that last point, Al Jazeera reported this week that [President Trump told supporters at a Republican convention in Dallas](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/9/trump-says-iran-war-will-end-immediately-after-us-midterm-elections) that the war with Iran "will end immediately" after the November midterms. He reportedly added that the US is "not looking to restart negotiations." Taken together, that is a remarkable admission: the timeline is electoral, not military. The war ends when the voting stops, not when any objective is achieved.

This should alarm anyone who takes seriously the proposition that wars ought to have strategic rationales. History offers a dispiriting archive of conflicts whose architects believed they could be switched on and off for domestic political advantage, and whose duration and costs were ultimately determined by the enemy rather than the initiator. The Korean War's initial "police action" framing, or the assumption in Lyndon Johnson's inner circle that a few months of pressure would bring Hanoi to the table — the pattern is durable: leaders who define wars as temporary tactical tools tend to find the tools developing a life of their own.

The $100 oil price is not just an inconvenience. In Britain, the 5p weekly rise in petrol costs falls hardest on working households in areas without reliable public transport — the very demographic that has been drifting toward populist parties and away from mainstream politics for the better part of a decade. In Germany and the rest of the eurozone, oil above $100 reignites the inflationary pressures that central banks have only recently brought under partial control. The Guardian's Australian coverage noted this week that the [Reserve Bank of Australia is struggling to contain inflation](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/10/the-rba-hoped-this-years-three-interest-rate-rises-would-tame-inflation) precisely because the "breakdown of the US-Iran ceasefire" and rising oil prices are working against its rate rises.

There is a second-order problem too. The US decision to attack Iranian tankers rather than Iranian territory is, in the language of coercive theory, a "denial" strategy: cut off the money, degrade the capacity, avoid the full escalation of strikes on the homeland. It is in principle less dangerous than bombing Tehran. But it also lacks the finality that either a negotiated settlement or a decisive military defeat would produce. Instead, it creates a sustained, low-level campaign with diffuse costs — costs paid not by the US Treasury but by motorists in Birmingham, retirees in Rotterdam, and farmers in Queensland — while the political benefits of "toughness" accrue to the decision-makers in Washington.

There is an older conservative tradition that has always been suspicious of wars prosecuted for political rather than strategic ends. Edmund Burke worried about wars of ideology; Russell Kirk worried about "democratist" crusades; more recently, figures like Pat Buchanan argued that American military adventurism invariably enriched insiders while taxing the common man. The right-wing case for the Iran campaign rests on deterrence theory and the genuine malevolence of the Iranian regime's proxies. The conservative critique is not that Iran is blameless but that a war with an electoral exit date and no clear terminal objective is not deterrence — it is theatre, and theatre has a ticket price.

## What to watch

- **Oil price trajectory**: Whether Brent holds above $100 or retreats will determine how much political damage flows to Western governments in autumn budgets and by-elections.
- **Midterm dynamics**: If Trump's "war ends after midterms" framing becomes widely reported, watch for Republican primary challengers or dovish voices inside the party to exploit the admission that the war's timeline is political.
- **Iranian response modality**: Tehran's likely counter-moves — accelerated uranium enrichment, proxy escalation through the Houthis, or attempted tanker interdiction in the Strait of Hormuz — will determine whether the $100 floor holds or becomes a ceiling.
- **European central bank guidance**: The ECB and Bank of England will face renewed pressure on rates if energy costs sustain the current level; watch for September policy meetings to reflect or deflect on this.
