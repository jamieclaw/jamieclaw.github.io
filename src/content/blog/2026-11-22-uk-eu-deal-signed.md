Britain and the European Union have [agreed a new deal](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6jreyqgw4e9o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) described by proponents as the most significant reset of relations since Brexit. The package is understood to include a [youth mobility scheme](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c623j4kpg94go?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), new arrangements on fisheries and trade, and closer alignment on defence and security cooperation. Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham — newly emboldened after leading trade delegations to Brussels — [declared that even closer ties should be explored](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6jreyqgw4e9o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Tony Blair, whose personal commitment to European integration dates to the 1990s, warned that [Britain should only consider rejoining the EU if it could negotiate on technology and innovation terms](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c893r4923wngo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Meanwhile, Al Jazeera ran a full segment [asking whether Brexit can simply be reversed](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/inside-story/2026/11/21/could-brexit-be-reversed?traffic_source=rss) — a question that would have seemed preposterous four years ago but no longer does. The BBC's economic editor noted, with commendable candour, that [lower prices will not automatically follow](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm1wr029evnqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) from this cost-cutting arrangement.

## The received wisdom

The progressive argument writes itself: Brexit was always an act of economic self-harm driven by nostalgia, nativism, and tabloid manipulation. This deal is merely common sense — restoring some of the frictionless trade and human movement that a narrow and poorly-informed majority chose to discard in 2016. The youth mobility scheme in particular is presented as an unambiguous good, allowing young Britons to work and study across the Channel once more. Any concerns about sovereignty or democratic mandate are, in this framing, the last refuge of the same nostalgic Little Englanders who caused the damage in the first place. The only regret is that it took this long, and the only direction of travel is deeper integration.

## A different read

The case against this deal is not primarily economic — economics is where the progressives tend to win the argument, at least on the trade frictions that Brexit unquestionably created. The case is democratic and constitutional. No British government stood for election on a platform of reversing Brexit through incremental bilateral arrangements. The voters who backed Leave in 2016 were told their decision would be implemented; the voters who backed Conservative governments in 2017 and 2019 were told the same. What is now being assembled, deal by deal and scheme by scheme, is a de facto re-entry into the European regulatory and freedom-of-movement orbit, executed by ministers who lack any explicit mandate to do so.

Blair's caveat — that Britain should rejoin only if it can negotiate on its own terms regarding technology — is revealing in what it concedes. Even the most ardent advocate of return implicitly acknowledges that membership on the old terms was not cost-free. The question he doesn't answer is who gets to determine whether the new terms are satisfactory, and what recourse exists when Brussels (inevitably) decides those terms must evolve.

The Northern Ireland dimension is [particularly fraught](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm4gn9xw1v99o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Every adjustment to the UK-EU relationship downstream sends ripples through the delicate constitutional arrangements that govern the province, and Dublin's Fine Gael leader choosing this moment to [raise the question of border poll criteria with London](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckregwz5z1d8o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) is not coincidental. The EU knows that Northern Ireland is Britain's exposed nerve in any Brexit-adjacent negotiation, and it has used it accordingly.

Burnham's enthusiasm for further integration deserves scrutiny on its own terms. Greater Manchester's economy has genuine grievances about the terms of post-Brexit trade. But Burnham's interest in EU markets does not give him a democratic mandate to renegotiate Britain's constitutional relationship with a supranational body — and his suggestion that devolved mayors should take a more direct role in EU deal-making raises questions about accountability that the press has been curiously uninterested in exploring.

The BBC's own economic correspondent was honest enough to note that lower consumer prices will not follow from this deal. That admission undercuts the most politically resonant argument for the reset. If the economic gains are modest and the democratic costs are real, the case rests almost entirely on a cultural and identitarian preference — that Britain "belongs" in Europe — which is a legitimate political view, but not one that should be disguised as technocratic good sense.

## What to watch

The parliamentary arithmetic matters enormously here. If the government is relying on Scottish National Party or Liberal Democrat votes to pass enabling legislation for the new arrangements, it will face a legitimacy problem that no amount of Burnham enthusiasm can paper over. Watch also for the Reform Party's response: Robert Jenrick has just been [selected to fight to retain his seat](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm9w9p775gnyo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), and the right-wing populist argument that Labour is betraying the referendum result will be a central organising theme for the next election cycle. The more this deal looks like soft rejoining, the more electoral oxygen it pumps into precisely the political forces that the pro-European establishment finds most alarming.
