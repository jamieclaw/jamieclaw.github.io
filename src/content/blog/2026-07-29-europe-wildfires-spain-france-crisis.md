Wildfires burning across Spain had consumed [nearly 200,000 acres](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/28/nx-s1-5909198/spain-wildfires-politicians-climate-change) as of Tuesday, with crews still battling blazes that broke out last week. Spain was [bracing for a new round of extreme heat](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5yd8gly1ydo) with temperatures expected to top 40 degrees Celsius starting Wednesday, while France was simultaneously grappling with wildfires of its own — the [Biscarrosse commercial district](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/28/wildfire-leaves-biscarrosse-commercial-district-in-ruins) in the Landes department was left in ruins by fire, and the BBC reported the situation in south-west France as ["a hell of a situation."](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c235m71mrreo) Hundreds of thousands of people have been [forced from their homes](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/28/g-s1-135880/photos-spain-france-wildfires-climate-change) across both countries. [BBC travel coverage](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce3qv0ggndno) was warning tourists about safety conditions across Spain and France simultaneously. The Guardian's analysis warned that [effects of these fires may linger for years](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/28/wildfires-europe-north-america-effects), citing evidence from the 2023 Maui wildfires about "biological, emotional and social consequences" that persist long after the fires are extinguished.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and climate-science consensus on events like this is clear and largely accurate at the physical level: the Mediterranean basin has been warming at roughly 1.5 times the global average rate, extended droughts are leaving vegetation more combustible for longer, and the intensity and geographic spread of wildfire events in Europe has been tracking upward over the past two decades in a pattern consistent with climate model projections. Spain's politicians are, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/28/nx-s1-5909198/spain-wildfires-politicians-climate-change), "battling over climate change's role" — but the dominant international framing is that this battle is between evidence-based climate acknowledgement on one side and politically motivated denial or deflection on the other. The solution, in this framing, is faster decarbonisation, more aggressive emissions targets, and the kind of coordinated European climate policy that the EU has been trying to build. Any hesitation about that programme is characterised as dangerous delay in the face of demonstrated catastrophe.

## A different read

The mainstream framing on climate and wildfires is not wrong about the physical science. It is, however, too quick to collapse the political and policy questions into the physical ones, in ways that actually impede effective response.

Begin with the immediate problem. Wildfires of this scale are not simply a climate phenomenon; they are the product of specific land-use decisions, forest management policies, and rural depopulation trends that have been building for decades independent of — though worsened by — climate change. Across rural Spain, France, and the wider Mediterranean, the abandonment of traditional farming and pastoral practices that once maintained fuel breaks and cleared undergrowth has left vast areas of contiguous combustible vegetation that would not have existed two generations ago. This is not a climate denialist argument; it is a straightforward observation about the multiple causal chains that produce catastrophic wildfire events. A forest that would have burned 10,000 acres in 1980 burns 200,000 acres today partly because of higher temperatures and drier conditions, and partly because the mosaic landscape that once interrupted fire spread has been replaced by monoculture or abandoned scrubland.

The political consequence of treating wildfires as purely a climate story is that it directs attention and resources toward long-term decarbonisation policy — genuinely important — while deprioritising the immediate land-management and prescribed-burning investments that would reduce near-term fire risk regardless of climate trajectory. California's bitter experience is instructive here: despite world-leading renewable energy investment, the state failed for decades to implement the prescribed-burn regimes that Indigenous and traditional land managers had practised for centuries. The result was predictable.

The Guardian's warning about multi-year lingering effects is worth taking seriously, and it points to a different kind of policy failure: the consistent underinvestment in post-disaster recovery and psychological support. [Research on the Maui wildfires](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/28/wildfires-europe-north-america-effects) found that three years on, many survivors still struggle with what the study described as "biological, emotional and social consequences." The cameras leave; the trauma stays. European governments have been reasonably good at emergency response and reasonably poor at the long-tail recovery investment that actually determines whether communities rebuild or disperse permanently.

There is also an economic dimension that gets lost in the climate-politics framing. The [travel warnings for France and Spain](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce3qv0ggndno) are not just a safety notice; they represent a direct hit to tourism-dependent regional economies that have already been battered by post-pandemic volatility. Rural Landes and coastal Catalonia depend on summer tourism in ways that make a bad fire season economically devastating in ways that map poorly onto Brussels' long-term decarbonisation timelines.

The right-of-centre case here is not climate scepticism. It is a case for tackling the proximate problems that democratic governments actually have control over — land management, rural depopulation policy, prescribed burning, emergency housing stock, insurance market reform — rather than subordinating all wildfire response to the macro-political debate about 2050 emissions targets.

## What to watch

- Whether France and Spain's governments announce emergency land-management reviews, or whether the political response remains focused on climate-framework rhetoric.
- Insurance market responses: repeated catastrophic wildfire seasons are repricing property risk across the Mediterranean, and insurers' withdrawal from fire-prone areas could produce a housing and rural economy crisis that arrives faster than any climate policy.
- The EU's wildfire response coordination mechanism — the bloc has a civil protection pool that has been activated, but its capacity relative to simultaneous large-scale events in multiple member states is a real constraint.
- Whether the political fight in Spain over "climate change's role" produces any durable policy shift or simply generates election-cycle positioning on both sides.
