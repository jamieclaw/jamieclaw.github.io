The International Monetary Fund used the margins of the UN General Assembly to deliver a message that fiscal authorities in Washington, London, and Brussels have been skilled at ignoring: [advanced economies must "bring debt down" as borrowing costs rise](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx305ymq4ldqo). The warning landed in a week when the UK recorded an [unexpected surge in government borrowing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c68049m18435o) that analysts described as adding to "pre-Budget pressure on the chancellor," and as the US Congress continued to operate without a long-term fiscal framework. The IMF's message is not novel — versions of it have been issued annually since at least 2010 — but the context has changed. Interest rates have been structurally higher for two years, the pandemic-era expansion of balance sheets has not been fully unwound, and the defence spending demands generated by the security environment in Ukraine, the Middle East, and the Pacific are creating new fiscal pressures that show no sign of abating.

## The received wisdom

The Keynesian-adjacent mainstream argues that this is not the moment for fiscal consolidation. The global economy remains fragile; pre-emptive austerity risks triggering precisely the recession that consolidation is meant to prevent. The lesson of 2010-2012 — when European austerity programmes contributed to a prolonged second dip — is that cutting too soon, too fast, destroys the tax base you need to eventually close the deficit. Moreover, the progressive version of this argument adds, debt servicing costs matter less in nominal terms than in relation to GDP growth: if the right public investments generate sufficient growth, the debt ratio improves without explicit consolidation. The IMF itself, this reading notes, has revised its earlier hawkishness on fiscal policy multiple times since 2010, and its warnings should be read in that light.

## A different read

The post-2010 critique of austerity was substantially correct in its own historical moment but has metastasised into a blanket immunity from fiscal discipline that is no longer intellectually defensible.

The argument that interest rates normalised to zero — and would stay there indefinitely — was the foundational assumption beneath a decade of deficit expansion that stretched from the Obama era through the Biden administration and its European equivalents. That assumption is now definitively broken. The Bank of England, the Federal Reserve, and the ECB have all maintained higher-for-longer postures that are producing exactly what the IMF is warning about: a structural rise in debt servicing costs that crowds out productive expenditure. [Trump's own hand-picked Fed chair raised interest rates](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/c54g7wg1l3d3o), defying the president's demands, because the inflation fundamentals left no alternative. The era of free money is over, and the fiscal frameworks built on the assumption that it would continue indefinitely are now visibly straining.

The UK case is instructive in ways that go beyond British parochialism. Andy Burnham's government faces a budget in weeks against a backdrop of borrowing figures that have already exceeded forecasts, a public sector that absorbed enormous wage increases in 2023-2024, and a defence commitment that is rising toward 2.5% of GDP. The [unexpected borrowing surge](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c68049m18435o) is not a technical anomaly; it is the fiscal expression of promises that were made without a credible plan for how to pay for them. The Liberal Democrats, meanwhile, are [promising tax cuts through single market re-entry](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cj62e1wrpx73o) — a policy whose fiscal arithmetic depends on growth assumptions that may not materialise.

The deeper structural problem is demographic and political rather than technical. Advanced democracies face an ageing population that has accumulated claims on public spending — pensions, healthcare, long-term care — that compound faster than the tax base can grow under any realistic scenario. The Keynesian growth escape route requires growth rates that no major advanced economy has consistently achieved in the post-financial-crisis era. [Gen Z is already planning for life without a state pension](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8e2yp1gg37o) — a cultural signal that public trust in the fiscal compact is eroding even as the political class continues to expand commitments.

Niall Ferguson has been arguing for years that the fiscal trajectory of Western democracies is the primary long-term threat to their strategic capacity — more significant, in the end, than Russian military adventurism or Chinese economic competition, because it undermines the resource base from which all strategic responses must ultimately be funded. The IMF's annual warnings are easy to dismiss as a technocratic ritual. The structural data underlying them is harder to argue with.

## What to watch

- The UK Autumn Budget: whether Burnham's chancellor opts for genuine consolidation or another round of revised fiscal rules that defer the reckoning.
- US Congressional budget negotiations — particularly whether any serious deficit reduction passes before the November midterm cycle makes it politically impossible.
- Bond market reaction in the UK and US: if long-term yields rise significantly on the back of borrowing data, markets may impose the discipline that politics has refused.
- Defence spending demands: if European NATO members approach 3% GDP targets, the fiscal squeeze will intensify sharply and political resistance to consolidation will harden.
