On the evening of 23 October 2026, a gunman opened fire near Paris, killing at least one person in what President Emmanuel Macron [condemned as a "racist terrorist attack."](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6ev38dj1g8zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) French authorities launched a manhunt as the city prepared for further public protests; a football match between PSG and Lyon was [moved](https://www.bbc.co.uk/sport/football/articles/cw8rz3eyn26po?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as Paris braced for disorder. The attack comes as France has been grappling with a prolonged period of social tension, intermittent riots, and a political class that oscillates between rhetorical condemnation of violence and structural paralysis in addressing its roots. Macron's prompt use of the word "terrorist" — with the qualifier "racist" — signals a deliberate framing choice that will itself become a subject of political contestation in the days ahead.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-progressive reading of events like this is well-rehearsed and, at its best, genuinely important. Terrorism of any ideological colour is terrorism. The state has an obligation to protect all citizens equally, and the impulse to draw broad cultural or demographic conclusions from individual atrocities leads historically to collective punishment of innocent communities. France's republican tradition, the argument goes, requires a firm distinction between criminal individuals and the communities from which they emerge. Macron's immediate invocation of "racism" signals that the left-wing reading — that France's structural inequalities and exclusions breed the alienation that occasionally detonates into violence — will be central to the official response.

There is real weight to this framing. France's banlieues have been systematically underinvested for decades. The republican promise of equal citizenship has been honoured more in constitutional text than in employment statistics or police encounter rates. No serious analysis of French social violence can omit these facts.

## A different read

The problem is that "racism" as the master explanatory category for this attack — if that is indeed how authorities proceed — carries its own evasions. France has now cycled through versions of the same conversation so many times that the very predictability of the response has become part of the crisis. A terrible act occurs. Political figures issue statements. The left emphasises structural causes; the right emphasises cultural ones. Reform commissions are announced. Nothing changes at the level of the banlieues, and nothing changes at the level of border and integration policy either. The conversation has become a ritual substituting for governance.

The right-of-centre argument is not, as its caricaturists insist, that all immigrants are terrorists or that Islam is uniquely violent. It is the more modest and empirically defensible claim that France's specific model of mass settlement — combined with a republican civic culture that was always better at assimilation in theory than in practice — has produced durable parallel societies in ways that other immigration models (Canada's points-based system, Germany's early guest-worker integration programs, even Britain's community-specific settlement patterns) have handled with varying but generally greater success. France's problem is not immigration per se; it is the specific form of French state ideology that simultaneously insists on colour-blind universalism while producing colour-coded outcomes in housing, schooling, and employment.

Macron [has himself acknowledged](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/23/macron-condemns-racism-after-one-killed-in-terrorist-shooting-near-paris) that France is in crisis. What he has never convincingly done is connect the rhetorical acknowledgment to a coherent policy programme. The same centrist technocrats who have managed French social policy for thirty years remain in their positions, applying the same approaches that have demonstrably failed. The irony of the progressive-technocratic response to recurring French violence is that it is, in its own way, profoundly conservative: it conserves existing institutional arrangements, existing funding models, existing approaches to policing and social work, while projecting an image of reformist seriousness.

The deeper issue is what social theorists have called "exhausted liberalism" — the condition in which liberal democratic governments have lost the vocabulary, and often the will, to make the kinds of normative distinctions (between acceptable and unacceptable cultural practices, between functional and dysfunctional community norms) that any workable integration policy requires. When "naming" a problem's cultural dimensions is itself treated as a racist act, the political system loses its capacity to address that problem at the level at which it actually operates. That is not a recipe for harmony. It is a recipe for the recurring cycle France has been living through for two decades.

## What to watch

- The identity and precise ideology of the perpetrator, which will determine whether Macron's "racist terrorist" framing is sustained or complicated by the evidence.
- Whether the French government uses the attack to advance any specific legislative or policy response, or whether it follows the now-familiar pattern of condemnation followed by institutional inertia.
- The electoral implications for Marine Le Pen's party and for the broader French right, which will frame this as evidence of policy failure regardless of the specifics.
- Any European-level response — particularly whether the EU revisits border management frameworks or treats this, as it has previous episodes, as a purely domestic French matter.
