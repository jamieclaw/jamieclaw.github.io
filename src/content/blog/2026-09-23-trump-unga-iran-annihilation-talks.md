At the United Nations General Assembly on Tuesday, Donald Trump told the gathered heads of state that he faced "a big decision to make" about Iran, adding — [eleven minutes into a forty-minute address](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/22/donald-trump-unga-speech) — a remark so blunt it made delegates visibly flinch: a threat to annihilate a fellow UN member state. Hours later, US officials confirmed that American and Iranian delegations had held [three hours of talks on the sidelines](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/22/trump-says-us-officials-met-with-iranian-delegation-for-three-hours-2), with Trump himself calling the meeting "very good." The G7, convening ahead of the Assembly, had already [called on Iran to stop arming the Houthis in Yemen](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwm2qpmepmlzo). The juxtaposition — existential threat at the podium, constructive dialogue in the corridors — defines the peculiar grammar of the Trump second term.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading is alarm, and not without reason. Threatening the annihilation of a UN member state from the General Assembly floor represents a breach of diplomatic norms so jarring that commentators reached for Cold War analogies, invoking the ghost of [Nikita Khrushchev's shoe-banging theatrics](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/22/donald-trump-unga-speech). Critics argue that such rhetoric is reckless: it emboldens hardliners in Tehran, undermines America's allies in the region who need diplomatic cover, and corrodes the already fragile international rules-based order. The liberal case runs that serious diplomacy — the kind that might actually prevent conflict — requires a predictable, credible interlocutor, not one who oscillates between annihilation threats and "very good" bilateral talks in the same afternoon. The White House press pool's [recent suspension of major news outlets](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/22/nx-s1-5977768/trump-tv-media-ban-white-house) only deepens the opacity around what signals are actually being sent to whom.

## A different read

The received critique assumes that diplomatic coherence is the only path to de-escalation. History suggests otherwise, and the Trump UNGA performance may be more strategically legible than it first appears — though not necessarily more reassuring.

Start with the historical record of maximalist rhetoric enabling negotiations. Nixon's "madman theory," whatever one thinks of it, was deliberately designed to convince adversaries that the president was unpredictable enough to use overwhelming force — a posture that created negotiating space the Eisenhower era's measured language had not. Reagan called the Soviet Union an "evil empire" while simultaneously preparing to meet Gorbachev at Reykjavik. The rhetorical extreme and the diplomatic track have never been as mutually exclusive as the liberal consensus assumes.

Second, consider Iran's actual position. The Trump administration launched [military operations against Iran in February](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/22/donald-trump-unga-speech) and has maintained a posture of maximum pressure since. Tehran's willingness to sit for three hours of talks — however informal — suggests the pressure is producing some movement. The G7's demand that Iran cease arming the Houthis is not merely rhetorical: [the Houthis have opened new territorial fronts in western Yemen](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/22/g-s1-144261/yemen-houthis-saudi-iran-humanitarian-crisis), displacing more than 100,000 people, and the UK has publicly committed to [helping refuel Saudi jets countering Houthi attacks](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqlykpe4dk0yo). The stakes are real.

Third, the UNGA speech needs to be read as a domestic communication as much as a foreign one. Trump's base does not want diplomacy for its own sake; it wants results. The threat to annihilate Iran communicates resolve to an American constituency that is deeply sceptical of the "forever war" cycle in the Middle East while simultaneously wanting any eventual deal to look like unconditional victory. The madman framing gives Trump the political cover to negotiate without appearing weak.

None of this means the strategy will work. The risks are substantial. Iranian hardliners — who have their own domestic audiences to manage — may read the UNGA remarks not as a negotiating signal but as a sincere statement of intent, ruling out the very talks that Trump subsequently praised. The margin for miscalculation is enormous: the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi missile capabilities, and Israeli calculations all interact in ways that no presidential speech can fully control. There is also a deeper problem: annihilation rhetoric from a democratic leader sets precedents that outlast any single administration. The norms that constrain state behaviour are not self-enforcing; they require constant rhetorical reaffirmation, and Trump is systematically depleting that reserve.

The question is whether the three-hour sidebar tells us something the UNGA speech deliberately obscured. If so, the performance may be working. But "working" and "wise" are not the same thing. A strategy that relies on projecting irrationality places enormous demands on every subsequent actor in the system to correctly decode that irrationality as performance rather than intent. States — and their miscalculation rates — do not always comply. The Cuban Missile Crisis, the closest historical parallel to a nuclear-adjacent standoff managed through brinkmanship, came to a peaceful resolution partly by luck. The luck variable is not something that appears in any president's strategy document.

## What to watch

- Whether a follow-up US-Iran diplomatic channel opens in the coming weeks, or the sideline talks remain a one-off performance.
- The Houthi front: further territorial gains in western Yemen will test whether G7 pressure on Iran translates into anything operational.
- Congressional reaction — particularly from Republican hawks who have been ambivalent about the Iran conflict's costs.
- Whether Saudi Arabia, now publicly supported by British air-refuelling operations, shifts from a defensive to an offensive posture.
