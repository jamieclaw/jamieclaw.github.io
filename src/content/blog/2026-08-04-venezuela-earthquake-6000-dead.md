Venezuela's government confirmed on Monday that the death toll from the magnitude 7.2 and 7.5 earthquakes that struck the country on 24 June has risen to 6,125, with 61,000 people treated in hospitals. [National Assembly President Jorge Rodriguez announced the figures](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/3/death-toll-from-venezuela-earthquakes-passes-6000) on Telegram, adding that 16.5 percent of earthquake debris had been removed. The coastal state of La Guaira and the capital Caracas were the hardest hit. A World Bank assessment has placed physical damage from the earthquakes at nearly $20 billion. The Venezuelan government, now headed by interim President Delcy Rodriguez following the abduction of former President Nicolás Maduro by US special forces in January, faces mounting criticism over shortcomings in the disaster response. International pledges of support were made in the days after the quakes, but the actual flow of reconstruction funds remains unclear and contested.

The US retains significant control over Venezuela's economic circumstances: some sanctions from the Maduro era remain in place, and advocates have called for their loosening to facilitate reconstruction funding. Chevron, whose Venezuela operations were previously protected even through nationalisation under Hugo Chávez, has been a beneficiary of Trump's post-Maduro opening of Venezuelan oil production.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian-progressive reading of the Venezuela catastrophe is, in its essentials, correct: a country already devastated by years of economic collapse, hyperinflation, and mass emigration has been struck by a catastrophe of historic proportions, and the international community has failed to respond adequately. The death toll of 6,125 — if accurate — would make this one of the deadliest natural disasters in Latin American history. The continued US sanctions are a legitimate target of criticism: when a country's financial system is crippled by external pressure, disaster-relief dollars cannot easily flow in. The World Bank's $20 billion damage estimate should be read against Venezuela's devastated productive capacity and near-total collapse of public services over the preceding decade. On this view, Washington bears particular responsibility: having helped engineer a change of government in January, it now has an obligation to enable Venezuela's recovery, and maintaining sanctions that impede relief funding is a moral and strategic failure.

This reading has force. The pattern of sanctioning governments out of office and then failing to invest in reconstruction is not unique to Venezuela — it is a recurring feature of US-led regime-change adjacent policy.

## A different read

And yet several realities complicate the straightforward blame-America narrative. Start with the governance context. The earthquake struck a country whose public infrastructure had been systematically gutted not by US sanctions alone but by two decades of socialist mismanagement under Chávez and Maduro — a system that nationalised industries, expelled foreign expertise, diverted oil revenues into politically patronage rather than maintenance, and left PDVSA, the state oil company, producing a fraction of its earlier output even before sanctions tightened. Venezuela's building code enforcement collapsed years ago; many of the structures that fell in Caracas and La Guaira were not earthquake-proof by any credible standard. Countries hit by earthquakes of similar magnitude with functional governance — Chile's 8.8 magnitude quake in 2010 killed around 500 people — suffer dramatically different outcomes because the institutional substrate is different. The death toll in Venezuela is not simply a geophysical event; it is a governance event.

Second, the diplomatic complexity around reconstruction is partly self-created. [Venezuela's decision to leave the International Criminal Court](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/27/what-drove-venezuelas-decision-to-leave-the-icc) — taken in the weeks after the earthquakes — removed one of the few neutral international frameworks through which accountability and aid coordination can occur. The interim Rodriguez government, which emerged from the chaos of Maduro's removal, has limited legitimacy both internally and externally. Donors are reluctant to commit funds to a government whose long-term status is unclear and whose accountability mechanisms are absent. This is not an argument against giving aid; it is an argument about why the aid pipeline moves slowly even when political will exists.

Third, the coverage gap itself is telling. Six thousand deaths in Venezuela have received a fraction of the international attention that a comparable disaster in a geopolitically simpler country would command. That invisibility reflects several factors: media fatigue about Venezuela after years of crisis coverage; the complexity of a country that does not fit cleanly into either the "US imperialism" or "socialist failure" narrative; and the absence of a vocal diaspora lobby comparable to those that mobilise attention for disasters elsewhere. [The World Bank estimate of $20 billion in damage](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/3/death-toll-from-venezuela-earthquakes-passes-6000) suggests a reconstruction need that dwarfs anything the current government can finance domestically, but international attention has already moved on. The combination of geopolitical complexity and narrative fatigue is lethal — not metaphorically, but literally, for the people still living in debris.

The question of sanctions deserves a direct answer. Some targeted sanctions — on specific individuals responsible for human rights abuses — can reasonably remain in place and still permit humanitarian aid flows. Broad economic sanctions that affect the financial system as a whole should be suspended for reconstruction purposes; the moral calculus of maintaining them against a government that, in any case, has changed, is not defensible. But loosening sanctions alone will not rebuild Caracas. That requires the harder work of institutional reconstruction, which Venezuela has barely begun.

## What to watch

- **Reconstruction fund disbursement**: Whether the international pledges made after the June earthquakes translate into actual fund flows, or dissipate into diplomatic communiqués.
- **Sanctions review**: Whether the Trump administration uses Venezuela's disaster as an opportunity to lift remaining broad economic sanctions while maintaining targeted individual measures.
- **Interim government stability**: Delcy Rodriguez's authority is fragile; whether the earthquake response fatigue translates into renewed political instability will determine whether any reconstruction plan can actually be implemented.
- **Regional solidarity**: Whether Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico — the largest Spanish-speaking neighbours — step into the leadership vacuum and provide bilateral reconstruction assistance regardless of US policy.
