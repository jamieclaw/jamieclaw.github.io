US consumer prices [rose more than expected in September](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwy8lx3pg4k2o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), the latest in a sequence of data prints that have confounded Federal Reserve projections and alarmed financial markets heading into the midterm elections. NPR reports that the September CPI surprise will [trigger an upward adjustment in Social Security benefits](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/14/nx-s1-6000593/inflation-consumer-prices-cpi-social-security-benefits) — a mechanical consequence of the COLA formula that provides some cushion for retirees but does nothing for working-age Americans who are absorbing higher costs without automatic indexation. On the ground, the picture is familiar and dispiriting: BBC reporters found [Americans across income bands struggling with persistently elevated prices](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c305649p2d6o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) on groceries, rent, insurance, and energy, describing the experience as "rough" and "tight." The Guardian's analysis suggests the question of [whether Trump's economic record will sink Republican candidates](https://www.theguardian.com/community/live/2026/oct/14/us-economy-affordability-midterm-elections-republicans-trump) in key Senate races is now the central variable in November's midterms.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive-media framing attributes persistent American inflation to a combination of corporate greed, supply-chain disruption, and the structural inadequacies of an economic system that concentrates gains at the top while socialising costs downward. There is a secondary argument, more quietly voiced, that the inflation problem is essentially solved — that headline numbers are declining toward target, that the Federal Reserve's gradualism has achieved a soft landing, and that Democratic messaging should pivot to the resilience of the labour market rather than defending an economic record that remains unpopular. The broader implication is that voter discontent reflects a perception gap rather than a real one: people *feel* worse off because media coverage is relentlessly negative, not because they actually are. This is the managerial reading: trust the models, trust the institutions, wait for the data to vindicate the policy.

## A different read

The managerial reading is not entirely wrong on the macroeconomics — headline inflation has, in fact, declined substantially from its 2022 peaks — but it is profoundly wrong on the politics, and increasingly suspect on the economics too.

Consider what "prices higher than expected" actually means for a family earning $60,000 a year. It does not mean a regression coefficient; it means the gap between what the paycheck covers and what the shopping cart costs has widened again, in a month when the experts promised it would not. The Federal Reserve has conducted its most aggressive rate-raising cycle in four decades, and the result is that the same Americans who cannot afford groceries also cannot afford mortgages. The inflation tax fell on everyone; the credit-tightening tax has fallen disproportionately on the young and the aspiring.

There is a deep tension at the heart of modern monetary orthodoxy that this episode exposes. Central bank independence — the institutional arrangement that placed interest rate decisions beyond electoral accountability — was designed, and has largely functioned, as a bulwark against inflationary populism. Politicians cannot, in normal times, simply order the Fed to print money for electoral purposes. That is an enormous institutional achievement with a respectable intellectual pedigree running from Milton Friedman through Paul Volcker to Ben Bernanke. But independence cuts both ways. It also means that when the Fed's models are wrong — when transitory inflation turns persistent, when demand stimulus combines with supply constraint to produce an inflationary surge that monetary policy alone cannot quickly resolve — there is no democratic mechanism for accountability. The chairman gives testimony twice a year; the voters feel the consequences daily.

The right-of-center concern here is not primarily about the Fed's policy stance, which has been, if anything, admirably hawkish given the political pressures. It is about the broader managerial confidence that produced the initial misdiagnosis. A 2021 consensus among treasury officials, academic economists, and financial journalists that inflation was transitory was not merely wrong; it was wrong in a way that delayed the tightening cycle by roughly eighteen months and imposed enormous real costs on ordinary earners. When the IMF's latest assessment of the UK economy — [cited in the BBC this week](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx30eq0vyed4o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — contains "surprise praise," it is worth noting that the IMF also spent years underestimating the structural persistence of post-COVID inflation in Western economies. Expert institutions are not infallible, and treating their pronouncements as beyond scrutiny is itself a form of democratic failure.

The Social Security COLA adjustment is the one element of this picture that functions as intended: an automatic stabiliser for a population with limited political voice. It is also a reminder that most Americans do not have indexed wages, indexed rents, or indexed savings. They have whatever their employer or their landlord decides to offer, in a market that has moved against them. The political consequence of sustained cost-of-living pressure is not primarily a messaging problem. It is a representation problem.

## What to watch

- Whether the September CPI print is an outlier or the start of a second inflationary wave — October's number, released just after the election, will be decisive for monetary policy in early 2027.
- Senate race dynamics in cost-of-living-sensitive states: Arizona, Nevada, and Montana, where inflation has polled as the top voter concern.
- Federal Reserve communications: whether Chair Powell signals any pivot toward accommodation given the political calendar, and how markets respond.
- The UK parallel: the [IMF's upgraded UK growth forecast](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmglw0r21zexo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) alongside warnings about energy prices suggests the transatlantic inflation story is far from over.
