The Trump administration has [threatened to ban American companies from building AI data centres overseas](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmde1lw9y54ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), framing the move as a national security measure to prevent advanced computing infrastructure from falling into adversarial hands. The Guardian reported separately that [Trump threatened "tremendous penalties"](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/20/trump-datacenters-white-house) for US tech firms that build abroad, language that signals intent to use regulatory force rather than gentle persuasion. The announcement arrived in the same news cycle as California's probe into [whether Nvidia chip exports ended up in China](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/20/california-investigates-nvidia-over-whether-chip-exports-ended-up-in-china), and [a broader investigation into Nvidia over Chinese AI chip supply chains](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqn70vxp5p4no?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). The timing is not coincidental: Washington is engaged in a comprehensive effort to prevent China from accessing the computing substrate on which the next generation of artificial intelligence will be built. The question is whether a ban on overseas datacenter construction is the right instrument — or whether it will harm American companies while doing little to slow Chinese AI development.

## The received wisdom

The tech industry's response to this kind of intervention is predictable and not entirely wrong. Building data centres overseas serves American companies' genuine business interests: proximity to European and Asian customers reduces latency, local infrastructure satisfies data residency regulations, and geographic diversification improves resilience. A ban on overseas construction would cede those markets to European, Chinese, and other competitors who face no equivalent restriction. The national security framing, the industry argument runs, is an overreach by an administration that sees strategic threat in every offshore investment rather than distinguishing genuine security risks from normal commercial activity. Silicon Valley will lobby hard, and it will find sympathetic ears in the trade wing of the Republican coalition.

## A different read

The administration's underlying concern is more serious than the tech lobby wants to concede. AI infrastructure is not like a car factory or a call centre. Data centres running frontier AI models are strategic assets in a way that most commercial infrastructure is not. They require export-controlled chips, they process sensitive data, and they represent the physical layer of a capability race that the United States government has correctly identified as the defining technological competition of the century. If American companies build large-scale AI infrastructure in countries with legal systems that require cooperation with authoritarian governments — think of China's national security laws, or the access that many Gulf states' intelligence services expect — the nominal American ownership of that infrastructure provides less protection than it appears.

The Nvidia investigations are the clearest illustration. Advanced semiconductor exports are subject to export controls precisely because US officials have determined that certain chips should not reach Chinese military or intelligence users. If those controls are being circumvented through third-country routing — a pattern that has been documented repeatedly — then allowing American companies to build data centres in intermediary jurisdictions without restriction is strategically inconsistent. You cannot simultaneously restrict chip exports for national security reasons and freely permit the construction of the facilities those chips would power.

That said, the instrument matters enormously. A blanket ban on overseas datacenter construction is a blunt tool that would create genuine commercial damage for little corresponding strategic gain. American hyperscalers building in Frankfurt or Singapore are not feeding Chinese military AI programmes. The more targeted approach — restricting construction in specific high-risk jurisdictions, requiring security reviews for facilities in countries with problematic intelligence-sharing obligations — would achieve most of the security benefit without forfeiting Europe and Asia to competitors.

Trump's preference for maximum leverage ("tremendous penalties") reflects a negotiating style that works in some domains and fails badly in others. In international trade, the threat of sweeping penalties can extract concessions; in technology regulation, it creates uncertainty that causes companies to delay investment decisions and move headquarters to more predictable jurisdictions. If American tech giants begin domiciling European operations in Dublin or Amsterdam to avoid domestic regulatory risk, the national security goal is not served — the infrastructure still exists, it's just owned by a European subsidiary rather than a California parent.

The deeper irony is that American dominance in AI depends precisely on the global deployment of American infrastructure and American platforms. The more of the world runs on AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, the more leverage Washington has over global AI governance. A retreat to domestic-only construction would, over time, create space for Chinese, European, and Gulf-state infrastructure providers to fill — which is exactly the outcome a national security-focused policy should be trying to prevent.

## What to watch

Watch for the specific regulatory language when and if this order is formalised. The difference between "no overseas data centres" and "no data centres in countries designated as AI security risks" is enormous in its practical implications. Congressional pushback will come from both parties — from Democrats defending Silicon Valley, from Republicans defending free markets — and may force a more targeted version of the policy. Watch also for European government reactions: Brussels has been building its own AI sovereignty agenda, and an American withdrawal from European infrastructure investment would accelerate rather than hinder that project. The Nvidia chip investigation resolution is the near-term bellwether; if it results in substantial penalties or export licence revocations, expect a broader crackdown on the entire supply chain.
