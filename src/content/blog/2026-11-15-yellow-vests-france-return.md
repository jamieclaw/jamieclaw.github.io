[More than 100,000 people marched across France](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/more-than-100000-rally-across-france-in-new-yellow-vest-protests) on Saturday in what organisers called a renewal of the Yellow Vest movement — the mass protest phenomenon that paralysed Paris in the winters of 2018 and 2019. Demonstrators chanted against declining purchasing power, the Macron government, police violence, and what they described as deteriorating public school conditions. The marches were the largest since the original Yellow Vest peak, drawing crowds in Paris, Lyon, Toulouse, Marseille, and dozens of smaller cities. The revival comes at a moment of acute economic pressure: French inflation remains above the European Central Bank's target, housing costs have risen sharply, and fuel prices — the trigger for the original 2018 movement — are again at politically sensitive levels. The government's response has thus far been muted, with ministers emphasising fiscal constraints and pointing to European-level energy policy as the principal driver.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic reading of the Yellow Vest revival — the one that dominates European progressive commentary — is that it represents the legitimate fury of a working and lower-middle class being squeezed by forces their government refuses to control. High fuel costs, stagnant wages, and a tax system perceived as indulgent toward the wealthy are real grievances, the argument runs, and a government that lectures its citizens about climate transition while refusing to shield them from the transitional costs deserves the streets it gets. The [protests in Portugal over housing costs](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/tens-of-thousands-march-in-latest-portugal-housing-protests), which have been running for weeks and drew similarly large crowds on Saturday, suggest this is not a French pathology but a European-wide failure of governance. On this view, what is happening is a healthy democratic correction to technocratic overreach.

## A different read

The sympathetic framing deserves engagement before it is complicated. The grievances are real. The political class's detachment from the economic conditions of non-metropolitan France is real. But the Yellow Vest movement — now in its second incarnation — has consistently demonstrated the limits of reactive protest politics: it can destroy governments and policies, but it has shown no capacity to construct an alternative. The first Yellow Vest phase produced roughly two billion euros in emergency fiscal concessions from the Macron government, temporary fuel-tax freezes, and the Grand Débat National — an exercise in participatory democracy that was, by most accounts, earnest in design and modest in outcome. France's structural fiscal position did not improve; indeed, the concessions worsened it.

The underlying pathology is a political economy that has locked France into a simultaneous commitment to high public spending, low growth, and European fiscal rules that resist the deficit expansion populists on both left and right demand. Neither the gilets jaunes of 2018 nor their successors today have offered a coherent answer to this trilemma. What they have offered is a generalised negation — no to this government, no to these prices, no to this police, no to this school — that is emotionally authentic but programmatically incoherent. The comparison with [Chile's current protest wave](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/14/nx-s1-6040144/chile-protests-fuel-kast-students), where fuel prices and student grievances are fusing into a challenge to President Kast's government, is instructive: across the democratic world, rising energy costs are becoming the solvent that dissolves whatever political settlement is currently in place, regardless of whether that settlement is left-wing or right-wing.

There is a conservative critique here that is distinct from, and more interesting than, the reflexive dismissal of the marchers. The French state's energy policy — heavy investment in renewables, neglect of its once-exemplary nuclear fleet, exposure to European spot-market gas prices — is a creation of the same technocratic centre that is now getting pelted with demands to fix energy costs. France mothballed nuclear reactors that were producing electricity at two to three euros per megawatt-hour and replaced the capacity with wind and solar that required market top-ups when the wind didn't blow. The [global energy superpower competition](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw62y2z53pg1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) between China and the United States over clean-energy supply chains is making European energy dependence structurally worse, not better. The Yellow Vests are protesting the downstream price signal of an upstream policy failure that stretches back fifteen years. That is not a complaint government press statements can answer.

The other dimension worth considering is policing. The original Yellow Vest movement was marred by significant police violence — rubber bullets, baton charges, serious injuries — that generated their own protest cycle. The reappearance of "police violence" among the Saturday chants suggests the government has not resolved that grievance either. France's interior-security apparatus has become a political variable in its own right, and the difficulty of reforming it without appearing to capitulate to mob pressure is a genuine governing dilemma, not simply a failure of will.

## What to watch

Watch whether the protests coalesce around a single political figure or demand; the first Yellow Vest wave was specifically leaderless, which made it undefeatable in the streets and ungovernable as a political movement. If a Marine Le Pen successor or a hard-left figure captures the energy, the electoral implications for 2027 become acute. Watch the ECB's response to French fiscal pressure: any government tempted to announce fresh spending concessions will immediately face bond-market discipline that the first round survived only narrowly. Watch energy prices heading into winter; a cold December in Europe is a political accelerant. And watch whether the [German government's own fiscal crisis](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/13/russia-ukraine-talks-kaja-kallas-eu-foreign-policy-dublin-summit) produces parallel street pressure — if France and Germany are simultaneously destabilised, the EU's political centre cannot hold.
