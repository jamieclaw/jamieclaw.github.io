Chancellor John Healey will deliver Labour's first budget in fifteen years next week, and the preliminary numbers are not encouraging. [Inflation and government borrowing have both risen to their highest levels in a year](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6gel2xv4493o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), creating a tighter fiscal envelope than Labour projected when it took office. The budget will reportedly include [a major devolution package](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/oct/24/andy-burnham-john-healey-first-budget-devolution-growth-package) designed to boost regional growth, alongside the government's promised investment in public services. [BBC economics editor Faisal Islam described the combined data as illustrating the chancellor's "Budget challenge"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm3wv9pn22e5o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — a characteristically diplomatic way of saying that the numbers do not add up as comfortably as Labour's manifesto implied.

## The received wisdom

The progressive framing of this budget is that Britain has been living with the consequences of thirteen years of Conservative austerity, and that Healey's task is to reverse the damage: invest in crumbling public services, repair the NHS, end the freeze on public sector pay, and stimulate growth through infrastructure spending and devolution. The Conservatives' record includes a decade of real-terms cuts to everything from local government to court services, and the Truss mini-budget of 2022 demonstrated what fiscal irresponsibility looks like on the right. Labour, on this account, is being fiscally responsible — raising taxes on those who can afford to pay, borrowing for investment rather than consumption, and accepting short-term pain to restore long-run capacity.

The received wisdom also holds that the UK's problem is fundamentally one of underinvestment: infrastructure, skills, housing, research — all the things that compound to determine long-run productivity — have been starved for years. The OBR and the Bank of England have both noted that Britain's supply-side capacity is weaker than pre-2008. Investment spending now, even if it adds to short-run borrowing, creates the productive base that makes future debt service affordable.

## A different read

None of this is false. But several things about the current fiscal picture deserve harder scrutiny than the progressive framing provides.

First, the inflation number. [UK inflation at its highest in a year](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6gel2xv4493o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) arriving immediately before a budget that will involve significant public spending increases creates a genuine risk. Fiscal stimulus into an inflationary environment is not investment — it is acceleration. If Healey borrows substantially and the Bank of England responds by keeping interest rates higher for longer, the growth dividend from investment spending may be offset by higher debt service costs and the chilling effect on private investment. The Bank of England is not a Labour institution; it will respond to its mandate, and its mandate is price stability.

Second, the borrowing number. Conservative governments borrowed heavily — this is true and the left is right to note it. But borrowing headroom is not an abstraction. The UK's gilt market reflects a real-time judgment by bond investors about the credibility of British fiscal management. After the Truss episode destroyed that credibility in 2022 — and Healey's own government has not yet demonstrated it can replace it with something sturdier — the cost of getting that judgment wrong again is not theoretical. A budget that spends heavily, relies on optimistic growth assumptions, and then faces a bond market that prices in higher risk could trigger exactly the kind of fiscal crisis it was designed to avoid.

Third, the devolution package. The principle of devolution is sound: decisions made closer to the communities they affect tend to be better calibrated than decisions made in Whitehall. Andy Burnham's Manchester model has genuine achievements to its name. But devolution without fiscal accountability — which has historically been the British way — creates incentives for regional spending without regional tax-raising. You get the political benefits of handing money to local governments while the Treasury absorbs the fiscal risk. Real devolution, the kind that works in Germany or Switzerland, includes genuine sub-national tax-raising powers. Watch whether Healey's package includes that, or whether it is merely the central government redistributing resources under a different administrative label.

The deeper structural problem is that Britain's productivity puzzle has no easy answer. The country has among the highest rates of economic inactivity in the G7, driven by long-term sickness — a phenomenon that post-pandemic data has concentrated. No amount of infrastructure investment addresses a workforce in which a rising share is unable to work. The NHS backlog that drives long-term sickness requires not just money but reform of how care is delivered — and reform tends to produce the kind of opposition from NHS unions and the medical establishment that Labour governments find politically impossible to sustain. [What is the Budget and what could be in it?](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6x2zrjl7kjno?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) is a question that conceals a harder one: what trade-offs is the government willing to make, and does it have the political will to make them against its own coalition's preferences?

## What to watch

- **OBR growth forecasts**: The Office for Budget Responsibility's independent projections will tell you more about the budget's credibility than Healey's own numbers. Watch for the gap between Treasury and OBR forecasts on growth and debt.
- **Gilt yield reaction**: In the days after the budget, the ten-year gilt yield is the honest market verdict on fiscal credibility. Above 4.5% sustained would signal bond market nervousness.
- **Tax on working vs. capital**: The distributional choices — whether the revenue-raising falls on income, national insurance, capital gains, or wealth — will define Labour's relationship with its middle-class voters for years.
- **NHS reform detail**: If the NHS investment comes without structural reform commitments, it is likely to disappear into the current system's cost pressures without visible improvement in waiting times.
