Saudi Arabia and the United States [conducted joint airstrikes against Iran-backed Popular Mobilisation Forces (PMF) bases in Iraq](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/29/iraq-calls-saudi-us-attacks-flagrant-violation-of-sovereignty), according to reporting from Al Jazeera on 29 July. The Iraqi government swiftly condemned the action as a [flagrant violation of sovereignty](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/29/iraq-calls-saudi-us-attacks-flagrant-violation-of-sovereignty). Simultaneously, Iran resumed missile attacks against US positions in the region, with NPR reporting that [US and Saudi forces struck Tehran-backed militias in Iraq](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/29/g-s1-136063/us-saudi-arabia-iran-iraq) as the cycle of retaliation continued. BBC reporting noted that [Saudi Arabia joined US in strikes on Iran-backed militias in Iraq](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70g6y24d76o), while a separate analysis explored [Saudi Arabia's dilemma as it tries to stay out of the US-Iran war](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyx83l8jv8o) — a dilemma that now appears resolved, at least temporarily, in favour of direct participation.

## The received wisdom

The standard liberal-internationalist reading of these events will emphasise legal and humanitarian concerns. Iraq is a sovereign state. The PMF, whatever their Iranian connections, are formally integrated into the Iraqi security apparatus. Joint US-Saudi strikes on Iraqi soil — absent an explicit UN Security Council resolution or Iraqi government consent — represent a troubling erosion of the post-1945 norms that have kept interstate war in check. The worry, from this perspective, is escalation: that each strike invites retaliation, each retaliation justifies the next strike, and the spiral widens until a regional conflagration becomes unavoidable. Proponents of this view point to the experience of the post-2003 Iraq intervention, which also began with confident assertions of decisive action and ended with two decades of low-grade insurgency and strategic incoherence. There is also a legitimate concern about Iraqi civilian populations caught between competing powers — this is a real cost that ought not be dismissed.

## A different read

The more telling fact, however, is not that the strikes happened but *who* carried them out together. Saudi Arabia joining American military operations against Iranian proxies on Iraqi soil is a strategic event of the first order — one that has been building since the [Abraham Accords](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyx83l8jv8o) framework demonstrated that Arab states could reconfigure their security relationships without waiting for a Palestinian settlement. For decades, the default assumption in Washington and Riyadh alike was that open Saudi military action against Iranian proxies would trigger domestic political blowback inside the Kingdom, embolden Iran's hardliners, and risk destabilising oil markets. All three concerns now appear to have been subordinated to a harder calculation: that Iranian proxy networks, left unchecked, represent a more immediate threat to Saudi territorial and economic security than any of the diplomatic risks involved.

This is not the first time a regional power has concluded that coexistence with Iran's proxy infrastructure is simply not sustainable. Israel has conducted hundreds of strikes in Syria over the past decade on precisely this logic — and the sky has not fallen. The difference is that Saudi participation transforms what had been a bilateral US-Iran confrontation into something that looks, uncomfortably, like a Sunni-Shia regional war conducted through proxies and air power rather than massed armies. History offers cautionary parallels. The internationalisation of the Spanish Civil War in 1936-39 began with similar logic — outside powers intervening to prevent adversary proxies from consolidating — and ended with Europe far less stable than before. The analogy is imperfect, but the structural dynamic is recognisable.

Iraq's position deserves serious attention rather than dismissal. Baghdad has spent years trying to manage its impossible position between Washington and Tehran, maintaining formal alliance with both while controlling neither. Iraqi Prime Minister [al-Sudani's government calling the strikes a "flagrant violation of sovereignty"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/29/iraq-calls-saudi-us-attacks-flagrant-violation-of-sovereignty) is not mere theatrics — it reflects the genuine erosion of Iraqi state authority over its own territory, a problem that predates these strikes and will persist long after. The harder right-of-centre question is whether a Baghdad government that cannot or will not disarm Iranian-directed militias operating from its territory retains the full moral claim to sovereignty protection. The answer is uncomfortable in both directions: yes, sovereignty matters as a principle; but sovereignty also requires the will and capacity to exercise it, including against non-state actors using your soil to kill people.

What the combined strikes most likely represent is not a considered long-term strategy but an improvised response to Iran's resumed missile attacks. [Trump threatening Iran strikes after attack on a US base in Jordan](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/29/trump-threatens-iran-strikes-after-attack-on-us-base-in-jordan) suggests the administration is operating in reactive mode, responding to each provocation with the next rung up the escalation ladder, without an evident theory of how the conflict ends. This is the classic failure mode of limited wars: the means are tactical, the objective is deterrence, but deterrence requires the adversary to want the same off-ramp you do. Iran's current leadership has historically shown that it does not always share that preference. Whether the new post-Khamenei leadership calculates differently remains the critical unknown.

## What to watch

- Whether Iraq's formal protest of the sovereignty violation escalates into demands for US forces to leave — which would hand Iran a strategic windfall without firing another missile.
- Saudi Arabia's domestic political management of the strikes: the Kingdom's religious establishment has long been wary of direct confrontation with Shia Islam; how clerical voices respond will matter.
- Iran's next move: resumed missile attacks suggest Tehran has not found its off-ramp yet; watch for any signal from Oman or Qatar back-channels, which have historically served as the de-escalation corridor.
- Oil prices: [Saudi Arabia's dilemma](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyx83l8jv8o) is partly about protecting energy revenue; if Hormuz freight costs spike again, the Saudis have strong incentives to pull back.
