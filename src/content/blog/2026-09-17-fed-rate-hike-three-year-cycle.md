The United States Federal Reserve [raised interest rates for the first time in three years](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw4gmlyvj422o) on Wednesday, a move that marks the end of an extended pause that monetary policymakers had maintained through a period of war-driven commodity volatility. The decision had been widely anticipated after months of energy-cost inflation stemming from the Iran conflict and its knock-on effects on global oil markets. US borrowing costs, which [had already reached their highest level since 2007](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw804154z90ko) in recent weeks as bond markets moved ahead of the Fed, are now formally on an upward path. The hike arrives as the US government is simultaneously absorbing elevated defence expenditures and as households cope with higher fuel costs — a combination that creates a narrowing corridor for policymakers.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive-Keynesian reading of a rate hike at this moment would be one of cautious endorsement wrapped in anxiety. The Fed, on this view, is finally doing what markets have demanded — acknowledging that inflation is not purely transitory, that energy supply shocks have embedded price pressure across the economy, and that a credible central bank must act. The worry from the centre-left is distributional: rising rates hurt borrowers, make mortgages more expensive, and tend to disproportionately squeeze lower-income households who carry variable-rate debt. The call from economists like those at the Roosevelt Institute has been to address inflation through supply-side investment and strategic reserves rather than demand suppression. There is a humanitarian dimension too: higher US rates tend to strengthen the dollar, which exports financial stress to emerging economies that borrow in dollars and import commodities priced in them. On this reading, the Fed is performing a necessary but socially costly act.

## A different read

The more uncomfortable truth — uncomfortable because it implicates decisions made by both parties over decades — is that the Fed's options are now genuinely constrained in ways they were not during the 2010s. The [bond market's move ahead of the Fed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw804154z90ko), pushing borrowing costs to their highest since 2007 before the central bank had formally acted, is the tell. Markets are not merely pricing in an expected rate path; they are pricing in fiscal risk. The US federal debt load has grown substantially through pandemic spending, the Ukraine support packages, and now the costs associated with the Iran war footing. When markets demand higher yields before the central bank acts, they are sending a signal about sovereign credibility, not just inflation expectations.

This dynamic has a historical precedent that the current commentary often fails to engage with directly. In the late 1970s, Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker faced a Fed that had lost the confidence of bond markets after years of accommodating deficits and energy inflation. His eventual response — the so-called "Volcker shock" of aggressive rate hikes in 1979–1981 — broke inflation but at the cost of a severe recession and, notably, a cascade of sovereign debt crises in Latin America, where dollar-denominated debt became suddenly unpayable. The parallel is not exact: today's global financial architecture is different, central bank communication is more sophisticated, and the starting level of rates is lower. But the underlying logic — that a credible commitment to price stability requires willingness to impose short-term pain — holds.

What makes the current situation particularly thorny is the fiscal side of the ledger. The Fed can raise rates, but it cannot cut spending. The [BBC's analysis of rising bond yields and their impact on American consumers](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/cwz0zpyzrlr1o) notes that higher borrowing costs feed through to mortgage rates, auto loans, and credit card debt with a lag — meaning the full impact of Wednesday's hike will not be felt for months. Meanwhile, the federal government continues to spend at wartime-adjacent levels. The combination of tight monetary policy and loose fiscal policy is the macroeconomic equivalent of driving with one foot on the accelerator and one on the brake — it can work for a while, but the car runs hot.

There is a second-order argument that the right should make more forcefully than it typically does: the rate hike is partly a consequence of the energy price inflation that followed from strategic decisions — specifically, the failure over years to secure sufficient domestic energy production and to build strategic reserves adequate to absorb a major Middle East disruption. The Iran war did not cause inflationary pressure in a vacuum; it activated a latent vulnerability created by years of policy that prioritised green transition speed over energy security redundancy. The rate hike is, in part, the bill coming due for that priority ordering.

The global spillover deserves attention too. Al Jazeera's [coverage of the Fed decision](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/16/us-fed-raises-interest-rates-as-inflation-weighs-on-economy) contextualises it within broader inflation pressures. Dollar strengthening following a rate hike typically compresses growth in emerging markets by raising the cost of their dollar-denominated debt servicing. Countries in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia that are already stressed by food and fuel price inflation now face an additional headwind from tighter US monetary conditions. The Fed does not set policy for the world, but it effectively does.

## What to watch

Watch the next inflation print — if energy costs moderate as the Iran situation stabilises, the Fed may pause after one or two hikes, avoiding the worst of the Volcker-parallel scenario. Watch the ten-year Treasury yield: if it continues rising even after the rate hike, that suggests markets want more, and the Fed is still behind the curve. Watch Congressional action on the deficit — or rather, the continued absence of it — as the most important long-run variable. And watch the political response from the Burnham government in the UK, which faces its own [Budget pressures](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwp93jp4p7xpo) and whose chancellor must now navigate a more expensive dollar environment for its own gilt issuance.
