Russia has [escalated its strikes on Ukraine's rail network](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/01/russia-targets-ukraines-rail-network-as-war-enters-deadly-new-phase), killing six railway employees on Tuesday in what Ukrainian officials describe as a new and deliberate phase of infrastructure warfare. The same day, a [sixth consecutive night of drone attacks on Kyiv](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/01/russia-kills-sixth-night-drone-attacks-ukraine-kyiv) killed at least twelve civilians, including two children, with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky stating publicly that Moscow is capitalising on a critical shortage of US-made Patriot interceptors to suppress air defences over the capital. The [German government simultaneously attributed a drone attack on Leipzig airport](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/01/germany-blames-russia-drone-attack-leipzig-airport) to Russia, describing it as part of a pattern of hybrid attacks designed to cause damage across European infrastructure. Authorities in Germany were also investigating [reports of explosives near a Brandenburg power substation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/sep/01/ukraine-russia-strikes-attacks-odesa-zelenskyy-putin-eu-defence-europe-latest-news-updates). The pace and breadth of Russian operations has intensified markedly in recent days.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western progressive-internationalist reading frames these events as a straightforward moral and strategic imperative: Ukraine must be supported with whatever it needs, Western democracies are in a battle for their values against authoritarian revanchism, and any suggestion that NATO or European hesitancy bears responsibility for Ukrainian suffering is a form of appeasement. That reading is not wrong in its essentials. Russia did invade a sovereign nation; the Ukrainian people did not ask to be the laboratory for Putin's imperial nostalgia. The targeting of civilian rail workers and residential buildings in Kyiv is a war crime by any definition, and the drone campaign against German civilian infrastructure represents the kind of aggression that a serious alliance cannot simply absorb without response. The moral clarity here is real.

The Patriot interceptor shortage is particularly alarming from this vantage point. If Zelensky is correct that Moscow is deliberately exploiting gaps in Ukrainian air defences created by Western supply constraints, then every drone that reaches Kyiv is partly a consequence of NATO's failure to sustain the commitments it made. The case for urgently resupplying Patriot batteries is overwhelming on humanitarian grounds alone.

## A different read

The rail targeting strategy is analytically significant and worth unpacking. Ukraine's rail network has been the logistical backbone of its war effort — moving troops, ammunition, and humanitarian supplies across a country the size of Texas. Russia's decision to systematically [target rail infrastructure](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/01/russia-targets-ukraines-rail-network-as-war-enters-deadly-new-phase) signals a shift in Moscow's operational theory: having failed to break Ukrainian will through mass civilian drone strikes, and having suffered severe attritional losses in ground offensives, Russia appears to be reverting to a strategy of logistical strangulation. This is historically a more effective approach. The Allied strategic bombing campaigns in World War II achieved their most decisive results not from bombing German cities — which, like Ukrainian cities today, proved remarkably resistant to morale collapse — but from targeting oil refineries and transport networks.

The Patriot shortage, meanwhile, deserves a harder look than it typically receives in Western commentary. Zelensky has been diplomatically explicit about it — almost certainly a deliberate political signal to Washington and European capitals. [UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham has reportedly offered to share missile blueprints](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8zxqn57z3o) so Ukraine can build its own long-range missiles — a creative workaround that speaks to the genuine constraints in Western production capacity. But it also reveals something important: three years into the largest European land war since 1945, the Western alliance has not surged its defence industrial base to anything approaching the scale the situation demands. Germany is attributing drone attacks to Russia while running budget debates about whether it can sustain its own defence commitments.

This is the deeper structural problem. The West's strategic posture toward Ukraine has been characterised throughout by a peculiar combination of moral clarity and material hesitancy — strong rhetoric paired with carefully metered supply of the weapons Ukraine needs to win. There is a conservative case, distinct from the isolationist one, for being more deliberate about this calculus: if the West is going to commit to Ukrainian victory, it should commit to it seriously, with a surge in production and supply that makes Russian escalation less, not more, likely. The Kissingerian counter-argument — that pushing too hard risks nuclear miscalculation — is serious and should not be dismissed, but it has been used to justify a gradualism that has extended the war without resolving it.

Russia's hybrid attacks on German infrastructure add a dimension that should concentrate European minds. The Leipzig drone incident and the Brandenburg substation investigation suggest Moscow is probing the threshold at which NATO's Article 5 commitment becomes actionable — not pushing hard enough to trigger collective defence, but hard enough to impose costs and erode public support for Ukraine aid. This is a sophisticated coercive strategy, and it is working in the sense that it is creating political friction in multiple capitals simultaneously.

## What to watch

Watch whether the Patriot shortage prompts a genuine NATO resupply commitment or remains a talking point. Watch whether Russia's rail campaign disrupts Ukrainian logistics enough to affect the front lines before winter — the window of autumn ground firmness is narrow. Watch the Modi-Putin dynamic: [India's prime minister has been urging Putin to end the war](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2zy3wdqqeo), and if Delhi is acting as an intermediary, that channel matters more than the public posturing suggests. Watch Germany's domestic political response to the Leipzig attack — if it hardens German public opinion toward Russia, it could unlock Bundestag support for greater military spending.
