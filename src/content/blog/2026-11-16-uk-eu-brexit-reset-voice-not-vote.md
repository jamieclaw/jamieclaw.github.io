The British government has confirmed that its ongoing Brexit reset negotiations with the European Union will include a formal arrangement under which the UK gains a "voice but no vote" on the EU's food safety and agricultural regulations — the so-called sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) rules that have been among the most contentious post-Brexit trade irritants. [BBC reporting confirmed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6wy4l8nl42wo) that Prime Minister Andy Burnham's team has accepted this formulation as the price of reduced border friction for British food exporters. Mayor — now Prime Minister — Burnham also told the BBC that a [win-win deal can be agreed between the UK and EU](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm2l5n07l87vo), while his party's internal politics showed signs of strain, with [Burnham's push for closer EU ties causing ripples](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr78251892z7o) in both his own party and among other political groupings. The Guardian separately reported that a [UK-EU breakthrough will allow airport-style fingerprint checks at the Eurotunnel](https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2026/nov/14/eurotunnel-drive-in-fingerprint-trial-folkestone-calais-border) to reduce border chaos — a practical improvement that arrives with its own costs for travellers accustomed to seamless movement.

## The received wisdom

The majority liberal reading of this development is straightforwardly enthusiastic: after years of Brexit self-harm, a pragmatic Labour government is quietly undoing the worst damage while maintaining the fiction of departure for domestic political consumption. The SPS alignment is the economically rational choice — British food exporters, particularly in agriculture and processed foods, have faced disproportionate costs from the introduction of the new UK-EU border. The Eurotunnel fingerprint arrangement reduces one of the most visible Brexit irritants for ordinary travellers. And a UK government that is willing to accept rule-taking in exchange for market access is, on this reading, simply being an adult about the realities of geography and economic gravity. The alternative — continued insistence on full regulatory autonomy while suffering the trade costs — was always a posture that served ideology more than national interest.

## A different read

There is much in that reading that is correct. The SPS friction has been genuinely costly, and the original decision to exit the single market in its entirety, rather than pursuing a Norway-style arrangement that would have preserved regulatory alignment, was arguably the worst single decision of the 2016-2020 Brexit process. The Burnham government is cleaning up a mess it did not make.

But the "voice not vote" formula deserves more scrutiny than it has received in the generally sympathetic coverage.

The critical question is not whether regulatory alignment with the EU is good for British food exporters — it probably is. The question is what institutional mechanism accompanies that alignment. Under the arrangement as described, Britain will be consulted on EU food safety and agricultural regulation changes, but will have no formal vote and no formal veto. In practice, this means that every time the EU updates its SPS regulations — and it updates them constantly, as a function of the world's most elaborate food safety bureaucracy — the UK will face a binary choice: adopt the new standard and maintain access, or refuse the standard and face renewed border friction. That is not a stable bilateral arrangement; it is a ratchet that slowly pulls Britain back into regulatory orbit without the political accountability that comes from having actual representation in the institutions that make the rules.

Brexiteers are, in general, wrong about most things. But they were not entirely wrong when they observed that the distinction between being outside the EU while following its rules and being inside the EU while helping to make its rules is not trivial. Norway has been making exactly this complaint about its EEA relationship for thirty years. The Norwegian government estimates that it has adopted approximately three-quarters of EU legislation with zero formal input into its content. The UK is now heading toward something structurally similar in the food sector, with the difference that it arrived there through negotiated bilateral agreement rather than a formal treaty framework.

There is also a domestic political dimension. The BBC's coverage of [Burnham facing internal Labour rebellions](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c95y100nrm41o) suggests that the EU reset is not cost-free within his own party. Some Labour MPs represent constituencies where Brexit was driven by genuine concerns about regulatory sovereignty and democratic accountability — concerns that the "voice not vote" formula specifically fails to address. The government's challenge is to explain why accepting rule-taking in this sector is better than the alternative, without inadvertently making the case that rule-taking everywhere is the natural endpoint of its European policy.

The honest version of Burnham's argument is this: Britain is too economically integrated with the continent to maintain full regulatory independence in sectors like food and agriculture without paying costs that outweigh the benefits of independence. That may be true. But it has implications for other sectors — financial services, pharmaceuticals, vehicles — that the government has not yet addressed. The "voice not vote" formula applied to SPS is a pilot project for a broader question about what Brexit actually means in practice, and the answer emerging in late 2026 looks considerably different from what either the Leave or Remain campaigns promised.

## What to watch

The key legislative test will be whether the SPS alignment arrangement requires primary legislation — in which case a parliamentary vote becomes a flashpoint — or can be implemented through statutory instrument. Watch also for the reaction from the devolved governments: Scotland in particular has strong agricultural export interests in the EU, and the Scottish government will be watching whether the "voice" mechanism includes any sub-national representation. And track whether the Eurotunnel fingerprint arrangements, once operational, reduce the political salience of border friction enough to give Burnham room to extend alignment to other sectors.
