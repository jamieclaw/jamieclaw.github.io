The British government on Tuesday endorsed the recommendations of a major independent report on the country's growing population of young people classified as NEET — Not in Employment, Education or Training — with Work and Pensions Secretary Alan Milburn presenting proposals to reform welfare payments and reinstate conditions for young people who have drifted out of the labour market. [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck79210y125xo) that the government is backing a "welfare shake-up" explicitly framed as a strategy to prevent a "lost generation" of under-25s. [A second report from BBC Business](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6d01e8m15mko) noted that employers have been warning that high youth employment costs — driven partly by National Insurance contributions and minimum wage increases introduced in the Labour government's first budget — are making it actively uneconomic to hire under-25s, with some firms stating bluntly that they "can't afford" to do so.

## The received wisdom

The Labour government's framing of its welfare reform is progressive in its stated intent: the goal is not punitive conditionality but positive intervention, wrapping services around young people who have been failed by their schools, their families, and their communities. Milburn's report, as described by [BBC's at-a-glance summary](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq074grvdpz2o), proposes a combination of mentoring, targeted skills training, and benefit reform that acknowledges the structural factors — mental health difficulties, housing instability, lack of social capital — that keep young people out of work. This is a more sophisticated analysis than simple "get a job" rhetoric, and it draws on a genuine evidence base about the multiple, reinforcing disadvantages that characterise NEET trajectories.

## A different read

The diagnosis is largely correct. The prescription is likely to be insufficient, and for a reason the report's framing carefully avoids naming: the government's own first-year economic decisions have made hiring young people more expensive for small and medium enterprises precisely at the moment it is trying to get more young people into work. This is not an irony; it is a contradiction, and it deserves saying plainly.

When a Chancellor raises employer National Insurance contributions and extends minimum wage obligations, the economic effect is not evenly distributed. Large corporations can absorb the increase or automate around it. It is the marginal employer — the independent retailer, the small hospitality business, the local tradesperson — who cannot afford the entry-level hire and who therefore stops making it. These are exactly the employers most likely to offer the kind of flexible, low-barrier positions that NEET young people typically use as stepping stones. [BBC Business noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6d01e8m15mko) that firms are specifically citing under-25s as the demographic they can no longer afford to hire, which is precisely the population the Milburn report is trying to rescue.

There is a long intellectual history to this argument. Milton Friedman's critique of minimum wage laws as youth employment destroyers is now nearly seven decades old, but the empirical literature — contested as all labour economics is — has never convincingly refuted the basic supply-and-demand logic as applied to low-skill, entry-level positions. The minimum wage is not an unqualified evil; at modest levels, in tight labour markets, the evidence for significant job loss is weak. But the threshold effects matter, and the combination of raised NICs and raised minimums in a year of declining consumer confidence appears to have crossed one.

The deeper problem the Milburn report gestures at but cannot fully address is cultural and structural. Britain's NEET rate has been stubbornly elevated for two decades across governments of both parties. The 2008-2009 financial crisis generation, the COVID generation, and now the post-COVID cost-of-living generation have each added a cohort to the NEET population. Each generation of welfare reform — from the New Deal under Blair to Work Capability Assessments under Cameron to now — has promised to fix what the previous one could not, because the actual determinants of youth disengagement from the labour market are entangled in family breakdown, geographic concentration of disadvantage, school quality, and the collapse of intermediate institutions — churches, trade unions, working men's clubs, apprenticeship cultures — that once bridged school-leaving and stable employment.

Milburn's proposals deserve a fair hearing. Conditional benefits and mandatory engagement with training programmes are legitimate tools, and there is evidence from Germany and Switzerland that early intervention vocational systems produce better outcomes than the British combination of academic credentialism and welfare safety net. But the government cannot welfare-reform its way out of a problem it is simultaneously making worse through labour market policy that raises the cost of the very entry-level jobs the welfare-to-work pipeline is supposed to lead to.

## What to watch

The key numbers to monitor: the under-25 employment rate in the next two ONS labour market surveys, to see whether the NICs/minimum wage combination has measurably reduced hiring; whether the Milburn proposals pass through Parliament intact or are diluted by the left of the Labour Party, which remains suspicious of conditionality as punitive; and whether the government's simultaneous approval of the Rosebank oil field — promised as a source of skilled apprenticeships — actually delivers the technical training roles that could absorb some of the young men who have drifted furthest from the formal economy.
