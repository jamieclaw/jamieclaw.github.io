Michigan on Saturday [reported cyberattacks on nine of its water systems](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/1/michigan-joins-minnesota-in-reporting-cyber-attacks-with-fbi-investigating), days after Minnesota disclosed breaches across some thirty sites. The FBI confirmed it was investigating both incidents and said at least seven states had reported similar events — though as of Saturday only Minnesota and Michigan had been publicly identified. The FBI, CISA, and allied agencies had issued an advisory earlier in the week warning that Iranian hackers had been targeting water and wastewater systems and other critical infrastructure. No culprit has been publicly confirmed. Michigan's Department of Environment, Great Lakes and Energy said all systems continued to operate safely. Minnesota IT Services said most confirmed attacks involved technology used to remotely monitor and control equipment, with some brief requests for residents to modify water usage; those requests were subsequently lifted.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream read is essentially a governance story with a partisan valence: federal government capacity has been hollowed out by the Trump administration's cuts to CISA and related agencies, leaving the country more exposed to exactly the kind of state-sponsored cyber intrusion that adversaries — Iran most prominently — have been developing for years. Minnesota Governor Tim Walz made this case directly, saying that Trump's cuts to federal employees had made the US more vulnerable and that Trump "knows exactly who is responsible." The broader liberal consensus holds that cybersecurity is a public good that requires robust federal coordination, and that the current administration's scepticism of administrative agencies and its record of staffing cuts has created a dangerous gap. That argument draws on a real body of evidence about the vulnerability of municipal water systems, which tend to run older operational technology with limited IT security budgets.

## A different read

The partisan framing is convenient on both sides, which is the first reason to be sceptical of it. President Trump, asked about the Minnesota attacks, said "I think Minnesota is behind it" — a transparently political deflection that tells us more about his reflex to discredit Democratic governors than about any actual intelligence assessment. Walz, symmetrically, used the attacks as a vehicle for a budget argument he would have made regardless. Neither response is a serious security posture.

The more important story is structural, and it predates the current administration by many years. Local water utilities in the United States have been operating on aging SCADA — supervisory control and data acquisition — systems that were designed for reliability, not cybersecurity. The [FBI advisory](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2026/malicious-cyber-actors-targeting-water-and-wastewater-sector-internet--facing-programmable-logic-controllers-causing-operational-disruptions) specifically identified internet-facing programmable logic controllers as the attack surface. These are industrial control devices that municipal systems began connecting to the internet in the 2000s and 2010s for convenience; many were never designed to be internet-accessible and have never been patched for modern threats. This is not a problem the Trump administration created, though the current administration's reductions in CISA capacity have made it harder to remediate.

The Iranian dimension matters, and Walz is likely right that Iranian actors were involved — the pattern matches previous attribution by US and allied agencies, and Federal law enforcement has previously indicted Iranian hackers for targeting water infrastructure. But Iran's motive here is not apocalyptic. Tehran is practising coercive disruption at the low end of the escalation ladder: demonstrating capability, exploiting the US-Iran tensions that have been running hot since the strikes earlier this year, and avoiding a conventional military response by staying below the threshold of kinetic attack. This is the doctrine of "grey zone" operations, and Iran has been refining it for more than a decade, including the 2021 Oldsmar, Florida water treatment incident and the subsequent 2023 attacks on water infrastructure that CISA attributed to Iranian-linked actors.

There is a conservative case here that goes beyond simple hawkishness: resilience and subsidiarity. Local infrastructure should not depend on federal agencies for its first line of defence. The attacks would have been far harder if individual utilities had implemented basic segmentation — air-gapping their operational technology from internet-facing systems — which is a relatively straightforward, inexpensive hardening measure that many have simply not implemented. Part of the reason they haven't is that federal mandates to do so are weak and unenforced; part of the reason they're weak is that the utilities lobby, in both Republican and Democratic states, has resisted the compliance costs. This is a story about deferred maintenance and regulatory capture dressed up as a partisan fight about federal staffing.

There is also a geopolitical signal embedded in the timing. The attacks come at a moment of maximum US-Iran tension following this year's strikes, and they appear calibrated to impose costs without triggering an Article 5-equivalent response. Iran is, in effect, conducting a demonstration: showing that even as its conventional military position is weakened, its cyber arm can reach into American civilian infrastructure. The correct response is not primarily diplomatic theatrics but a sustained, funded, technically specific programme of infrastructure hardening — which neither party has shown the appetite to deliver.

## What to watch

Watch whether the White House issues a formal attribution in the coming days, and if so, how Iran responds — either by escalating or by using a denial to maintain deniability. Watch also whether Congress, following recess, uses this episode to advance any of the dormant water-sector cybersecurity bills that have been sitting in committee for two years. The bipartisan framing of infrastructure security is one of the few remaining areas where legislation is theoretically possible; whether it actually happens is a test of whether anyone in Washington is still capable of governing past the news cycle. Finally, watch the other five unnamed states: if more go public, the scope of this incident becomes significantly larger than the current reporting suggests.
