The Trump administration has asked the US Supreme Court to allow restrictions on mail-in voting that have been blocked by lower federal courts, [Al Jazeera reported Monday](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/27/trump-asks-us-supreme-court-to-allow-mail-in-voting-restrictions). The request comes less than 100 days before the November midterm elections. The restrictions in question — the specifics of which have not been fully detailed in the public reporting — have already drawn a legal challenge from Wisconsin, where [prediction market company Kalshi separately sued the state over its ban on election betting](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5905360/kalshi-wisconsin-election-betting-prediction-markets), suggesting Wisconsin is shaping up as a major battleground over election administration more broadly. The Democratic Party, meanwhile, is confronting serious internal difficulties: the [Guardian reports that DNC chair Ken Martin](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/27/dnc-chair-ken-martin-scrutiny) faces growing scrutiny over a cash-flow crisis and an internal HR complaint after a heated incident with a junior aide.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive critique of Republican-initiated voting restrictions is by now well rehearsed: limitations on mail-in voting are a form of voter suppression, disproportionately affecting low-income voters, elderly voters, and minority communities who rely on mail ballots. The fact that the administration has chosen to escalate this legal fight less than 100 days before a midterm election looks, on this reading, like a strategic attempt to reduce Democratic turnout at the margins — an admission, essentially, that the Republicans cannot win on policy alone. The Supreme Court, with its conservative supermajority, becomes the instrument of this strategy. The fact that Wisconsin — a perennial swing state — has already resisted a related election-law push from a major business interest (Kalshi) reinforces the narrative of an administration running roughshod over state sovereignty when it suits them.

This critique has genuine force. Mail voting expanded dramatically during the 2020 pandemic and, despite the flood of litigation that followed, courts consistently found that the expansion was lawfully administered. Any restrictions imposed weeks before an election carry real risk of disenfranchisement through voter confusion alone, regardless of the merits of the underlying legal question.

## A different read

The deeper constitutional argument is more interesting than the political optics suggest, and it is worth engaging with seriously even if one ultimately disagrees with the administration's position.

The bedrock claim is about the Elections Clause of the US Constitution, which grants Congress — and by extension, federal authority — the power to regulate the "Times, Places and Manner" of federal elections. The progressive era and the New Deal largely normalised the idea that election administration is a state function; the practical consequence is a system of 50 different sets of rules, varying deadlines, and inconsistent chain-of-custody requirements for mail ballots. The conservative legal argument — not a frivolous one — is that this patchwork creates real risks of error and manipulation, and that federal standardisation is not only constitutional but desirable.

There is a legitimate conversation to have here. Countries with unified national election systems — Germany, the United Kingdom, France — tend to have simpler, less litigation-prone elections than the American federal patchwork. When critics note that the US system is uniquely vulnerable to claims of fraud — not necessarily because fraud is more prevalent, but because the decentralisation makes it easier to construct plausible-sounding narratives of irregular counting — they are identifying a real structural problem.

The problem with this administration pursuing that argument is one of timing and credibility. Reforming election administration 90 days before a midterm that the administration has a strong interest in winning does not look like principled constitutional housekeeping. It looks like exactly what the critics say it is. Even someone sympathetic to the underlying legal argument should want it made in a context where the self-interest is not so nakedly obvious — or at minimum, in a way that does not deprive voters of access they have relied upon.

The Supreme Court has been notably cautious about late pre-election intervention since its 2020 shadow docket rulings generated significant backlash. Whether the current Court's majority will apply that caution here — or whether it will defer to the administration's statutory argument — is genuinely unclear. The stakes are high enough that the Court may want to punt, either by denying the application or by crafting a narrow ruling that changes little before November.

Meanwhile the DNC's internal crisis is its own story. An embattled chair, a cash-flow problem, and internal divisions about party direction less than 100 days from an election that will determine the balance of Congress: the Democrats appear to be organising a response to a motivated, disciplined opponent with a degree of institutional chaos that should concern anyone who wants competitive elections to remain competitive.

## What to watch

- The Supreme Court's timeline: any ruling must come fast enough to matter, which means either a quick denial (preserving the status quo) or an emergency order within weeks.
- Whether Wisconsin and other states with significant mail-vote infrastructure mount coordinated legal resistance, as they did in 2020.
- The DNC fundraising numbers: if the party enters October with a significant cash disadvantage, the mail-vote restriction fight may be largely academic — you can't mobilise voters you can't reach.
- Whether the Kalshi election-betting lawsuit produces any ruling before November: prediction markets claiming a First Amendment right to take bets on election outcomes is a genuinely novel legal theory that deserves careful scrutiny, not just a political reaction.
