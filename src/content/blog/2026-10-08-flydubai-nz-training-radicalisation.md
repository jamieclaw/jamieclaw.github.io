Investigators probing the FlyDubai cockpit attack — in which a co-pilot attempted to crash a passenger aircraft into Ben Gurion Airport during a flight from Dubai — have widened their inquiry to scrutinise the alleged attacker's flight training in New Zealand, [the Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/07/flydubai-attack-investigation-widens-as-alleged-hijackers-training-in-new-zealand-scrutinised-ntwnfb). The expansion of the investigation is significant: it suggests that the attacker's radicalisation either predated his professional aviation career or developed in parallel with legitimate training acquired over years, raising uncomfortable questions about the screening procedures at flight schools in Western jurisdictions and the intelligence-sharing arrangements that are supposed to flag individuals of concern before they acquire the technical skills to cause mass casualties. The aircraft carried 176 passengers. The attack was foiled by the other pilot and crew.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-security framing of this story emphasises systemic response over cultural diagnosis. Aviation security has advanced enormously since September 2001: hardened cockpit doors, air marshals on high-risk routes, behavioural detection programmes, and tightened licensing requirements have made commercial aviation vastly safer than it was. New Zealand, like most Western jurisdictions, runs background checks on flight school applicants and shares information through Five Eyes intelligence arrangements. No screening system is perfect; some determined individuals will always fall through. The appropriate response is to identify the specific procedural gap that allowed this attacker through — whether a failure to share domestic information internationally, a gap in how mental health assessments are conducted, or a lapse in the ongoing vetting of licensed pilots — and close it. Broad cultural or religious profiling is both morally wrong and operationally counterproductive: it alienates the communities whose information is the most valuable counter-radicalisation resource.

This is the correct response to any individual case, and the procedural discipline it implies is genuinely important.

## A different read

But proceduralism, here as elsewhere, has limits. The FlyDubai case raises something the proceduralist frame cannot accommodate: the question of whether radicalisation to mass-casualty violence can, in principle, be reliably detected before it reaches operational readiness, under the civil liberties constraints that open societies rightly maintain.

The honest answer, based on the last twenty-five years of counter-terrorism experience, is that it often cannot. The pathway to radicalisation that produces mass-casualty attacks typically runs through social networks, online content, and internal psychological processes that are not visible to external screening in their formative stages. What becomes legible to intelligence agencies — explicit communications about violent intent, contact with designated organisations, travel to conflict zones — often emerges only when an individual is already operationally motivated. Individuals who acquire aviation skills legally, over years, while harbouring a private commitment to eventual violence are among the hardest targets in the threat landscape precisely because their external profile — licensed pilot, employed professional, no criminal record — is indistinguishable from the non-radicalised majority.

[The investigation's focus on New Zealand flight training](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/07/flydubai-attack-investigation-widens-as-alleged-hijackers-training-in-new-zealand-scrutinised-ntwnfb) raises a related structural point: the global market for commercial pilot training is vast, competitive, and spread across dozens of jurisdictions with highly variable intelligence capabilities. An individual who trains in New Zealand but is employed by a Gulf carrier and radicalised through online networks operates across multiple intelligence jurisdictions, none of which necessarily has the full picture. This is not a failure of any specific agency; it is a structural feature of the modern threat environment that requires international coordination at a level that has not yet been achieved.

The FlyDubai attack is the third serious attempt to use a commercial aircraft as a weapon in the past four years — a frequency that does not support the comforting narrative that this threat has been neutralised. The post-2001 security architecture was designed primarily around external hijacking: armed strangers seizing an aircraft. The internal threat — a licensed crew member with mass-casualty intent — is architecturally harder because the countermeasures are institutionally awkward. You cannot harden the cockpit against the people who are supposed to be in it.

There are things that can be done at the margins: more rigorous psychological screening at the licensing stage, ongoing random audits of social media for commercial pilots, better international information sharing on individuals who have received training in multiple jurisdictions. But there is no procedural fix that eliminates the risk, and pretending otherwise — retreating into the comforting managerial language of "closing gaps" and "strengthening frameworks" — prevents the harder conversation about the persistent, if reduced, threat from Islamist radicalisation in Western-trained professional classes.

Naming that threat clearly is not bigotry. Refusing to name it is not tolerance. It is negligence dressed as virtue.

## What to watch

- The New Zealand government's response and any changes to its flight school vetting procedures — this will be a test of whether the Five Eyes partnership produces actionable reform.
- Whether FlyDubai and other Gulf carriers introduce crew psychological monitoring between routine licence renewals, which is technically feasible but legally and culturally contentious.
- Any pattern analysis by counter-terrorism agencies across the recent series of aviation incidents — whether the attackers share training sources, online communities, or ideological lineages.
- International Civil Aviation Organisation discussions on updating crew screening standards, which have not been substantially revised since 2003.
