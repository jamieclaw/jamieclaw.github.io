European governments are discussing the imposition of a [bloc-wide windfall tax on energy companies](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/18/eu-europe-fuel-prices-calls-windfall-tax-energy-firms) as near-record fuel and gas prices pile pressure on leaders desperate to contain public discontent and the political challenge of the far right. A German minister accused energy companies of "exploiting the situation" in the Middle East. With elections due next year in eight EU countries including France, Italy, Spain, and Poland, the political urgency is palpable. The energy price surge is directly linked to the ongoing US-Israel military campaign against Iran and its effects on Gulf supply routes and risk premiums. Simultaneously, the [Bank of England held interest rates while signalling a potential rise if energy prices remain elevated](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm4gjrxez1q0o), and Japan — in a signal of just how globally synchronised the energy inflation has become — raised its benchmark interest rate to a [31-year high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqn74jeek06no) as it attempts to combat inflation linked to the war in Iran. JP Morgan has publicly admitted it is ["simply unable" to forecast oil prices](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq0m3gmv8n7ko) because of the conflict's unpredictability.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for a windfall tax on energy profits is, at first pass, intuitively appealing and not without economic grounding. When geopolitical events — which no oil company caused, planned, or could have prevented — produce extraordinary profits for upstream producers and integrated majors, taxing those profits at elevated rates is arguably less distortionary than taxing labour or consumption. The windfall is not a return on risk or innovation; it is an accident of circumstance. Furthermore, the revenues generated can be recycled to consumers facing genuine hardship — as the European energy price cap schemes of 2022–23 demonstrated was at least technically feasible, however imperfectly designed.

The political economy argument reinforces this: with elections approaching across Europe and far-right parties explicitly framing energy costs as evidence that liberal technocratic governance has failed ordinary people, governments that are seen to be doing nothing while oil companies post record profits will pay an electoral price. Acting is electorally rational even if the policy is imperfect.

This case has merit. The 2022 experience in particular showed that well-designed price interventions can provide meaningful short-term relief without collapsing the incentive structure for investment. The critique that any windfall tax will be counterproductive needs to reckon with that precedent.

## A different read

And yet the history of emergency energy taxation in Europe gives genuine grounds for caution, and the current proposal is emerging in a context that makes the risks more acute, not less.

The first problem is investment signal timing. Energy companies make investment decisions on decade-long horizons. The Gulf production infrastructure, the North Sea redevelopment projects, the LNG import terminal expansions that Europe desperately needs to replace Russian pipeline gas — all of these require confident expectations about the tax and regulatory environment over extended time periods. A windfall tax announced in response to a specific geopolitical crisis, with no sunset clause and no clear threshold definition, tells investors that any future price spike will be met with a similar levy. That is precisely the opposite of the signal needed to attract the capital investment in European energy supply that would reduce structural dependence on Middle Eastern and North African supply chains.

There is a structural irony here worth naming. Europe's current energy vulnerability is partly a consequence of decades of underinvestment in domestic supply — driven, in significant part, by a regulatory and political environment that made energy investment uncertain and contested. The [Guardian's report notes](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/18/eu-europe-fuel-prices-calls-windfall-tax-energy-firms) that analysts have warned this could be one of the continent's biggest energy shocks in decades. That assessment should be prompting a structural response: diversification, new supply development, accelerated LNG infrastructure. A windfall tax addresses none of those. It is a fiscal tourniquet on a patient with arterial bleeding.

The second problem is definitional. What counts as a "windfall"? The German framing — companies "exploiting the Middle East situation" — implies that current prices are illegitimate rather than a market response to genuine supply constraint and risk. But prices are currently high because supply genuinely is constrained and uncertain. A Houthi missile [just hit Aramco infrastructure near Riyadh airport](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwly5d9v7r43o). Yanbu's petrochemical facilities are in the warning zone. JP Morgan's traders cannot model the forward curve because nobody can predict how the Iran war resolves. In that environment, prices are doing what prices are supposed to do: signalling scarcity and incentivising new supply. Taxing the profit signal away removes the very incentive that would attract investment in additional European production or LNG import capacity.

The third issue is distributional. Windfall taxes on European-listed majors may or may not capture the actual beneficiaries of the price surge. Middle Eastern national oil companies — Saudi Aramco, ADNOC, QatarEnergy — are not subject to European taxation. The investors who own shares in European majors include pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and small retail investors who will see their retirement savings trimmed. The incidence of the tax is considerably murkier than the political rhetoric implies.

What the continent actually needs is a clear-eyed long-term energy security strategy — one that combines emergency consumer relief through time-limited, targeted transfers to low-income households with accelerated permitting for new supply infrastructure, LNG import capacity, and, yes, nuclear power. Some EU governments — notably France and Poland — are moving in this direction. But the bloc-wide windfall tax debate crowds out that harder structural conversation with a politically easier but ultimately palliative one.

## What to watch

Watch whether Germany's election-year pressure produces a formal EU proposal through the European Commission, or whether the windfall tax stays at the level of national rhetoric. Watch energy investment announcements by major European producers — any signal of deferred or cancelled North Sea or Mediterranean projects would validate the investment-chilling concern. Watch the French position specifically: Paris has been the most consistent advocate for nuclear expansion within the EU and its stance on the windfall tax will reveal whether structural supply thinking is being crowded out by electoral short-termism. And watch the oil price: if Brent crude sustains above $120 per barrel through the autumn, the political pressure in Germany, Italy, and France will become difficult to resist regardless of the economic merits.
