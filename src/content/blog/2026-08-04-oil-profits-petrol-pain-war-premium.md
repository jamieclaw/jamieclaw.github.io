Chevron reported its highest quarterly profit in six years on Friday, posting adjusted earnings of $6.06 per share, or $12 billion, as tensions around the Strait of Hormuz kept global oil prices elevated. ExxonMobil reported $9.2 billion in quarterly earnings — its best in four years — and Valero Energy posted its highest-ever second-quarter profit at $3.7 billion. [President Trump responded by publicly berating Chevron CEO Mike Wirth](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/8/3/petrol-prices-strain-us-households-as-oil-giants-chevron-exxon-profits-soar), calling the company's earnings "too much money" and accusing Wirth of failing to credit the Trump administration for industry gains. For American consumers, the picture is starkly different: the average national petrol price stands at $4.09 per gallon, up from $3.82 a month ago and from $2.98 when the US and Israel first struck Iran in late February. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve this week hit its lowest level since 1983, falling to 304.8 million barrels after a draw of 2.8 million barrels. A Bank of America analysis published in April found that consumers were spending 4.2 percent of their income on petrol — with households in the lowest income bracket spending more than 10 percent of monthly income on fuel.

## The received wisdom

The progressive critique here is familiar but not without substance. Oil companies are booking record profits from a war that ordinary Americans did not choose, cannot afford, and — according to a Reuters/Ipsos poll cited this week — only a third of them support. The structural mechanism is simple: Chevron has less than 5 percent exposure to Middle Eastern production, so it benefits from higher global prices without bearing the supply disruption costs. Exxon and Valero similarly profit from the refining margin created by global supply tightness. Meanwhile, lower-income households — who spend a larger share of their income on petrol and have less ability to substitute, work remotely, or own fuel-efficient vehicles — absorb a disproportionate share of the war premium. Democratic Senator Sheldon Whitehouse's characterisation of the oil industry's windfall as money "we didn't earn" from a "cartel pricing scheme" may be rhetorically overwrought, but the underlying point — that shareholders are capturing a war dividend while consumers bear the cost — is arithmetically accurate.

The progressive remedy of a windfall profits tax has precedent: the UK imposed one on North Sea oil producers in 2022 after Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent energy prices surging. There is an intellectually coherent case for using the proceeds to fund targeted relief for lower-income households.

## A different read

But the progressive framing contains two important evasions. The first is about price signals and the second is about strategic choice.

On price signals: oil companies are not creating high prices; they are reflecting them. The Strait of Hormuz closure, combined with the new Houthi blockade of the Red Sea, has removed a substantial fraction of global crude supply from the market. Brent crude was [23 percent higher in the second quarter than in the first](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/8/3/petrol-prices-strain-us-households-as-oil-giants-chevron-exxon-profits-soar). Chevron's strong earnings come partly from its minimal exposure to the Middle East — meaning the market is appropriately rewarding a company for prudent geographic diversification. If Chevron had been heavily exposed to Hormuz, it would be posting losses. The outrage is directed at a company for profiting from a global price rise it did not cause. That is not corporate malfeasance; it is how commodity markets function. Imposing a windfall tax does not lower petrol prices — it taxes the symptom rather than addressing the cause.

The second evasion is more consequential: the war premium on petrol is inseparable from the decision to wage the war. Trump began military operations against Iran in February. The Strait of Hormuz closure followed. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve — now at its lowest level since 1983 — has been drawn down to buffer prices that the war itself elevated. This is a policy-created price problem, and the policy is one the administration chose. The irony of Trump publicly scolding oil executives while continuing the war that created their windfall is almost too neat to satirise, but it reflects a genuine structural tension: you cannot simultaneously wage an energy war and demand that energy companies forego the profits that flow from it.

The expert consensus on near-term relief is modest. Bill Drolet of The Post Oak Group told Al Jazeera that suspending state and federal gas taxes — which range from 20 cents per gallon in Texas to 63 cents in California — would be the most administratively feasible consumer relief measure. That is a sensible observation, but it too addresses the symptom. The deeper question — whether the strategic and military objectives of the Iran war justify the ongoing economic cost — is the one that almost no one in Washington is willing to ask plainly.

There is a further irony worth noting: the administration that sold itself on "energy dominance" and domestic production is now presiding over an SPR at its lowest since 1983 and petrol prices that have risen 37 percent since the war began. "Energy dominance" turns out to look quite different when the dominant energy corridor in the world is closed by an adversary who anticipated what American military action would trigger. The SPR was designed precisely for scenarios like this — but drawing it down while the war continues without a clear endgame only defers the reckoning.

The midterm politics are clarifying. A Washington Post/Ipsos poll cited this week found that 54 percent of respondents listed "high prices and the economy" as their chief concern heading into November. The overlap between households paying more than 10 percent of income on petrol and households in competitive congressional districts is not small.

## What to watch

- **Gas tax suspension**: Whether the administration moves to suspend federal and state fuel taxes as a pre-midterm relief measure — a politically cheap but symbolically significant option.
- **SPR draws**: Whether the reserve is drawn further below 300 million barrels, at which point restocking becomes a multi-year project that constrains future crisis options.
- **Congressional pressure on oil executives**: Whether Republicans in competitive seats begin calling for hearings on oil company profits, as happened after the 2022 European energy shock.
- **Hormuz deal and price reversal**: Oil fell 4–5 percent on Monday on talk-of-talks; if a genuine Hormuz arrangement materialises, the profit boom would unwind rapidly — watch whether oil majors are hedging against that possibility.
