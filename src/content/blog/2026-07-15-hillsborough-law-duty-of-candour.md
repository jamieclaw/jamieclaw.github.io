The [Public Office (Accountability) Bill — colloquially the Hillsborough Law](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cz754eey3xeo) — passed the House of Commons this week and is expected to become law by autumn. The legislation creates a legal duty of candour on public bodies and officials: they will be required to tell the truth to inquiries and investigations, with criminal penalties for those who do not. The bill's passage was not smooth — it was [briefly withdrawn in January](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cz754eey3xeo) after the government proposed an amendment allowing intelligence agency heads to opt out of public inquiries on national security grounds, drawing fierce opposition from Manchester Arena bombing families who pointed out that MI5 had given an inaccurate picture of its intelligence on the suicide bomber. That exemption has now been removed. Prime Minister Starmer, who made the bill a personal commitment to Hillsborough families, was joined by incoming Labour leader Andy Burnham in welcoming the passage. The 1989 Hillsborough disaster, in which 97 people were killed in a crowd crush at an FA Cup semi-final, was followed by decades of police fabrication of witness statements, official stonewalling, and institutional character assassination of the victims.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case for this bill — which is also the correct case — is overwhelming. Hillsborough is not a contested story. [The 2016 fresh inquests found conclusively](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cz754eey3xeo) that the Liverpool fans were unlawfully killed and that South Yorkshire Police caused or contributed to their deaths. Senior officers lied to Parliament, altered statements, and coordinated a media campaign — fronted most notoriously by Rupert Murdoch's Sun — to blame the victims. The families who fought for justice did so for thirty-seven years against the organised resources of the state. A law requiring public officials to tell the truth to inquiries is, on these facts, the minimum civilised response. Campaigner Pete Weatherby KC, director of Hillsborough Law Now, noted that the government has "at last accepted that there will be no carve-outs or exemptions for the new duty of candour." Ian Byrne MP, himself a Hillsborough survivor, called it "a lasting legacy for the 97, for the survivors, the bereaved families, and for every person who has suffered at the hands of the state and been denied truth and justice." These are not ideological claims. They are statements of fact about an institutional horror.

## A different read

The passage of this bill deserves genuine celebration, and I offer it. But the celebration should not entirely displace the question that hangs over it: why did it take this long? The Hillsborough disaster occurred in 1989. The cover-up was already a matter of extensive public knowledge by the mid-1990s. The full official vindication of the families came in 2016. The Hillsborough Law — which merely requires that public officials do what most people assume they are already legally required to do — is being enacted in 2026. That is thirty-seven years from the disaster to legislation. The gap is not incidental; it is a feature of how the British state protects itself.

Consider what was required to get here. It took serial public inquiries, a coroner's inquest that ran for two years, a Supreme Court battle, sustained parliamentary campaigning by a survivor-turned-MP, two prime ministers pledging personal support, and the imminent political disruption of a new PM inheriting the issue to finally push the bill through. At every stage, the momentum for accountability was resisted by institutional actors — not always maliciously, but consistently. The intelligence services exemption, inserted by the Starmer government's own officials in January, is the most recent example: even a government formally committed to the Hillsborough Law found reasons to carve out protections for state bodies when it came to the detail.

This pattern matters because Hillsborough is not unique. The Manchester Arena bombing inquiry into MI5 failures, the Grenfell Tower fire inquiry, the Post Office Horizon scandal, the infected blood inquiry: in each case, institutions that failed catastrophically subsequently deployed delay, legal complexity, and document management to impede accountability. The [Manchester Arena families](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cz754eey3xeo) who wrote to Starmer to oppose the MI5 exemption were not being paranoid; they had direct experience of intelligence services withholding information from a public inquiry. The duty of candour, if it is enforced rigorously, could change this dynamic. But enforcement is everything. A legal duty that carries nominal penalties and is policed by the same state apparatus it is designed to constrain is worth considerably less than its advocates claim.

The broader point is one that conservatives and libertarians often make more honestly than the centre-left: the state is not a neutral public servant. It is an institutional organism with interests of its own, primarily the interests of self-continuation, self-protection, and freedom from scrutiny. Every organ of government — the police, the intelligence services, NHS trusts, local councils — has structural incentives to manage information in ways that protect the institution rather than serve the public. The Hillsborough Law addresses this tendency with a legal duty. It will help. But the deeper question is whether British political culture has the will to actually prosecute senior officials who violate that duty, and whether courts will impose sentences that make the duty real rather than aspirational.

## What to watch

Watch the first test of the duty of candour in practice: which public inquiry or investigation will be the first to invoke the new law against an official who withholds information, and whether the Crown Prosecution Service will treat violations as serious criminal matters. Watch whether the intelligence services seek legislative amendments at a later stage to reintroduce national security carve-outs via a different route. Watch Andy Burnham, who is expected to become Prime Minister this autumn: he has been the bill's most consistent political champion, and his government's implementation will determine whether the law has teeth. And watch the civil service's response to the new legal landscape — the cultural change required to make the duty of candour meaningful will take a generation, and it begins now.
