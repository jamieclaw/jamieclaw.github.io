The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to its highest level in 31 years on September 18, 2026, continuing a cautious tightening cycle that began only recently after decades of ultra-loose monetary policy. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqn74jeek06no?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the move was driven by persistently rising prices — a phenomenon Japan had not experienced for a generation — and that the central bank signalled further tightening was possible if inflation remained elevated. The Guardian [confirmed the rate was at its highest since the mid-1990s](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/18/japan-raises-interest-rates-to-31-year-high-rising-prices), noting the context of rising energy costs and a weakened yen that has made imports more expensive for Japanese households. The move comes at a moment when central banks globally are navigating between inflation still running above target and economies showing signs of fragility.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream macroeconomic framing celebrates Japan's rate hike as a necessary and overdue normalisation. For three decades, the Bank of Japan was a laboratory for experimental monetary policy — near-zero and then negative interest rates, yield curve control, vast asset purchase programmes. The argument for these measures was that Japan faced a unique deflationary trap, rooted in demographic decline and post-bubble balance-sheet recession, that required extraordinary measures. Now that inflation has finally arrived, the central bank is doing the textbook thing: raising rates to anchor expectations and prevent an inflationary psychology from taking hold. Progressive economists point out that the old ultra-loose regime failed workers and small savers while inflating asset prices, benefiting the already-wealthy. Tightening is, in this reading, a restoration of monetary sanity.

This narrative is largely correct in its diagnosis. Japan's decades-long experiment in monetary stimulus produced asset price inflation, zombie corporate survival, and very limited wage growth for ordinary workers. The return to positive rates is, in principle, healthy.

## A different read

The more uncomfortable question is whether "normalisation" is actually available to Japan — or to any major economy that spent decades borrowing against the assumption of perpetually cheap money. Normalisation is easy to describe; it is genuinely difficult to execute without disrupting the vast edifice of debt, asset valuations, and carry trades built on the prior regime.

Consider the carry trade specifically. For years, sophisticated investors borrowed in yen at near-zero cost and invested in higher-yielding assets elsewhere. [When the Bank of Japan began its tightening cycle earlier in 2026](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqn74jeek06no?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), the resulting yen strengthening triggered significant volatility in global markets — a preview of what further hikes might produce. The yen carry trade is estimated to be enormous, though its full scale is difficult to measure precisely. Unwinding it is not a smooth process; it tends to happen in disorderly lurches that can destabilise asset markets in surprising locations.

The deeper point is historical. Japan's thirty-year monetary experiment was not an accident or an eccentricity — it was the leading edge of a global problem. The United States, the eurozone, and the United Kingdom all followed Japan into ultra-loose policy after 2008, and then more aggressively after 2020. Japan is now normalising first because its inflation arrived earlier and in a context of yen weakness. But the same pressures — ageing populations, debt levels that constrain fiscal space, asset bubbles built on cheap money — apply everywhere. Japan is not a cautionary tale about uniquely Japanese dysfunction; it is a preview.

The right-leaning critique of the post-2008 monetary regime has always been this: that central banks were substituting cheap money for the structural reforms — labour market flexibility, pension sustainability, industrial competitiveness — that actually determine long-run prosperity. Japan exemplifies the dynamic. Decades of quantitative easing produced a zombie-firm economy in which inefficient businesses were kept alive by cheap credit rather than being allowed to restructure. The Bank of Japan's tightening, welcome as it is, now risks calling in debts that corporate Japan was never forced to address. The resulting credit stress, if it arrives, will be blamed on the central bank's tightening rather than on the decades of forbearance that made the debts unsustainable.

There is a parallel in the United Kingdom, where [the Bank of England is signalling potential rate rises](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm4gjrxez1q0o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) if energy prices remain elevated, even as the economy shows limited growth. The UK, like Japan, spent years treating monetary stimulus as a substitute for supply-side reform. The reckoning has now arrived in the form of inflation that is hard to suppress without causing pain that the political system finds difficult to absorb.

The lesson of Japan's rate hike is not that monetary policy worked and we should celebrate its return to normalcy. It is that the exit from a thirty-year cheap-money regime is never clean, and that the costs of the exit are borne disproportionately by those who were most exposed to the asset-price inflation the old regime produced: mortgage holders, leveraged investors, and governments that financed spending with cheap debt rather than tax revenues or genuine growth.

## What to watch

- **Yen carry trade unwinding**: Significant further yen appreciation will pressure global asset markets, particularly emerging-market assets that were funded by yen carry positions. Watch for volatility in Southeast Asian currencies and equity markets as a leading indicator.
- **Japanese corporate sector stress**: The first wave of corporate credit events — small and mid-sized firms that survived on zero-rate refinancing — will be the tell. Watch Japanese bank non-performing loan ratios in coming quarters.
- **Bank of England following suit**: The BOE's signalled rate rise, if it materialises, will test the UK's heavily mortgaged housing market and its highly leveraged commercial real estate sector in ways that could produce political feedback.
- **US Federal Reserve correlation**: If Japan and the UK are both tightening while inflation remains sticky, the pressure on the Federal Reserve to maintain or extend its own tightening cycle increases. The global monetary regime is more correlated than central bank independence suggests.
