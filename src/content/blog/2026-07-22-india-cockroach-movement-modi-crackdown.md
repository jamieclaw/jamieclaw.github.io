Supporters of India's Cockroach movement — formally the Cockroach Janta Party, or Cockroach People's Party — remained at their sit-in site at Jantar Mantar in New Delhi on Tuesday, vowing to continue their protest the day after police used tear gas and batons to disperse thousands of them as they attempted to march toward Parliament, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/21/g-s1-134722/indias-youth-led-cockroach-movement-vows-to-continue-protest-after-police-crackdown). Delhi police said nearly 180 people were injured in Monday's confrontation, including 118 security personnel and 60 protesters. The movement is demanding the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan over alleged failures in India's examination system, including exam leaks, student deaths by suicide linked to exam irregularities, and systemic unemployment among graduates. The Cockroach Janta Party was founded just two months ago by Abhijeet Dipke and began as a satirical protest before evolving into a nationwide, youth-led movement drawing students, working professionals and families. Prominent activist Sonam Wangchuk, whose three-week hunger strike had become a symbol of the protests, was forcibly taken to hospital by police over the weekend. Opposition leader Rahul Gandhi and dozens of Congress party lawmakers staged a sit-in outside Prime Minister Narendra Modi's residence on Tuesday. Video of police beating protesters and dragging them away spread widely on social media, fuelling further public outrage and drawing additional participants to the movement. A government minister held talks with movement representatives on Monday in what appeared to be the first official engagement after nearly a month of silence; the talks failed to ease tensions. Amnesty International said the police response showed "how peaceful dissent is being suppressed in India."

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of the Cockroach movement — particularly in Western media and among Indian liberals — emphasises the authoritarian drift of Modi's third term. A government that uses tear gas and batons against students peacefully demanding education reform is, on this reading, demonstrating precisely the intolerance of dissent that critics have warned about since the BJP's consolidation of power began. The specific grievances driving the protests are genuine: India's examination system, which determines access to prestigious universities and government employment, has been repeatedly compromised by leaks that undermine the meritocratic promise on which millions of young people have built their aspirations. The National Testing Agency, which administers the key NEET and JEE examinations, has faced multiple credibility crises. Students who have spent years and significant family resources preparing for examinations that were compromised by corruption have legitimate reason for anger. The movement's rapid growth — from a satirical jape to a national movement in two months — suggests that it has tapped into a reservoir of accumulated frustration that goes well beyond any single examination scandal. The presence of Sonam Wangchuk, whose activism in Ladakh on environmental and autonomy issues gave him a national following, broadens the movement's appeal beyond examination grievances to questions of government accountability more generally.

## A different read

The Cockroach movement is unusual in the history of Indian protest politics, and its unusual features are worth examining more carefully than either its cheerleaders or its critics have done.

India has a long tradition of street agitation, and its democratic system has historically shown a capacity — imperfect, uneven, but real — to absorb and eventually respond to popular pressure. Anna Hazare's anti-corruption movement of 2011 created the political momentum that eventually produced the Lokpal Act, however inadequate. The agrarian protests of 2020-2021 forced the BJP to repeal three farm laws over which it had parliamentary supermajority, in one of the most striking reversals of agricultural policy by any government since Independence. The pattern suggests that sustained, broad-based protest has, in India, sometimes compelled even a dominant government to reconsider. Modi's BJP is not structurally immune to this pressure, however much its media management and institutional control might suggest otherwise.

What is different about the Cockroach movement is its social base. The protesters are not, predominantly, from the constituencies that have historically challenged Hindu nationalist politics — not religious minorities, not Dalit organisations, not the leftist student unions of the old Delhi University tradition. They are, [according to BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c98v349r9lro), largely from the educated urban middle class: students preparing for competitive examinations, young professionals who passed those examinations and found the promised careers had not materialised, and families whose investment in their children's education has not yielded the economic security it was supposed to buy. This is the social base that BJP politics has, until recently, been able to count on. When the government uses batons against people who voted for it, or whose parents voted for it, the political arithmetic changes.

The examination grievances, moreover, point to a structural problem that no amount of blame management can resolve. India produces millions of graduates annually for an economy that cannot absorb them at the income level their credentials suggest they deserve. The examination system — intended to be a meritocratic sorting mechanism — has been repeatedly corrupted by leaks, impersonation and institutional capture. The NTA's credibility problems are not a one-off scandal; they reflect systemic pressures that have built over decades of misaligned education supply and labour demand. A government minister's talks with the protest leadership, [as noted by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/21/g-s1-134722/indias-youth-led-cockroach-movement-vows-to-continue-protest-after-police-crackdown), failed because the protesters want structural reform, not assurances — and assurances are, politically, much easier to deliver than structural reform.

Modi's government has handled this badly. The month of silence before the first ministerial engagement, the baton charge that produced footage of crying young women being dragged away by police, the forcible hospitalisation of a hunger-striking activist: all of these are politically costly in ways that a more politically dexterous response could have avoided. Whether the government now recalibrates — as it did with the farm laws — or doubles down on the security response will determine whether the Cockroach movement becomes a footnote or a turning point.

## What to watch

- Whether Pradhan resigns, is reassigned, or survives — his fate will signal whether the government is prepared to offer a concrete concession or simply manage the optics.
- The NTA reform agenda: any credible package of examination system changes — transparent audit mechanisms, independent oversight, revised eligibility criteria — would need to be substantive enough to satisfy movement demands.
- Rahul Gandhi's Congress party role: if the sit-in outside Modi's residence turns into a sustained parliamentary campaign, the movement's energy could be channelled into formal opposition politics in ways that extend its half-life beyond a single examination cycle.
- Social media response: the government's broader attitude toward online dissent — and whether it attempts to restrict coverage of the protests — will be watched by international observers and affects India's reputation among the democratic allies it is courting.
