The White House has blocked Microsoft and Adobe from the H-1B high-skilled foreign worker visa programme, citing fraud allegations. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck5yngl2y4gpo) that the administration barred Microsoft from the programme on Thursday. [Al Jazeera separately reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/8/us-suspends-microsoft-and-adobe-from-visa-programme-amid-fraud-claims) that the US had "suspended Microsoft and Adobe from the visa programme amid fraud claims," with the suspension applying to one of the most widely used pathways for technology companies to hire specialised workers from abroad — particularly from India and China. The exact nature of the fraud allegations has not been fully detailed in public filings available at time of publication. Microsoft has not confirmed the BBC's characterisation of the ban as a "block" from the programme.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and pro-business case against this move writes itself. The H-1B programme is one of the primary mechanisms by which American technology companies recruit specialised engineering and science talent that the domestic education system does not produce in sufficient numbers. Restricting access for companies of Microsoft's scale — a firm that employs hundreds of thousands of people globally and whose research output includes foundational contributions to artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and quantum computing — risks damaging the competitiveness of the US technology sector at precisely the moment when competition with China is most acute. If the AI race is as consequential as Washington claims, then restricting the talent pipeline for leading AI developers is an act of strategic self-harm dressed up as border security. Silicon Valley has made this argument repeatedly, and it is not without force.

There is also the specific question of fraud allegations. If the charges are specific and evidence-based, they should be adjudicated through the existing legal process. Using programme-wide exclusion as a mechanism for fraud enforcement bypasses due process and creates a precedent for using immigration compliance as a weapon against disfavoured companies — a power that can be easily abused.

## A different read

The mainstream business community's defence of the H-1B programme, while economically coherent in the short term, has systematically avoided grappling with the structural questions that make this political moment predictable.

The H-1B programme as designed is a mechanism for employers to hire temporary foreign workers at fixed pay scales that are, in practice, often below market for equivalent domestic workers. This is not a secret: academic studies and Department of Labor data have consistently shown that H-1B wages cluster at the minimum allowed under prevailing wage rules, which are themselves set below median market wages. For companies, the programme creates a pool of workers who are legally tied to their sponsoring employer for the duration of their visa status — a condition that limits their bargaining power considerably. The programme's defenders describe this as importing talent; its critics describe it as depressing wages for domestic technology workers while creating a captive workforce.

The fraud framing — whatever the specifics of the allegations against Microsoft and Adobe — sits alongside a broader pattern in which large technology companies have used the H-1B programme at scale while simultaneously conducting significant domestic layoffs. Microsoft alone announced tens of thousands of job cuts over 2023 and 2024 while continuing to file thousands of H-1B applications. Whether or not this constitutes legal fraud, it constitutes a political problem that the technology industry has failed to address credibly.

The Trump administration's approach is characteristically blunt: use programme exclusion as leverage, create fear among technology companies, and signal to a domestic constituency that the immigration deal is changing. This is politically effective even when procedurally dubious. The relevant historical comparison is to the Reagan administration's use of regulatory and antitrust levers against industries it wished to discipline — an approach that was also often procedurally aggressive, but that forced corporate behaviour changes that litigation alone could not achieve.

The longer-term question for the technology sector is whether it can make a credible case for a reformed H-1B programme that genuinely addresses wage suppression concerns and limits employer lock-in, rather than simply defending the status quo as necessary for competitiveness. The status quo has been politically indefensible for years. The industry has not produced a serious reform alternative, which has left the field to those whose primary interest is restriction rather than reform.

There is also the question of what this signals to foreign-born workers already in the pipeline — students at American universities, workers mid-way through visa transitions, researchers at government-funded labs. Uncertainty about programme stability has measurable effects on the choices talented people make about where to build their careers. Canada, Australia, and Germany have all been systematically marketing themselves to precisely this population. Each restrictive action in the US is a recruitment advertisement for its competitors.

## What to watch

- Whether the specific fraud allegations against Microsoft and Adobe are publicly detailed, and whether they involve systematic abuse of the programme or isolated compliance failures — the difference is material.
- Microsoft and Adobe's legal response; a court challenge to programme-wide exclusion would test the administration's legal authority on an expedited basis.
- Whether other large technology employers are added to the exclusion list, which would indicate whether this is targeted enforcement or a broader programme restriction.
- Congressional reaction; H-1B has defenders on both sides of the aisle, and a serious push to reform rather than restrict the programme could emerge from unexpected coalitions.
