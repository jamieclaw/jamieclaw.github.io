Nicaragua's National Assembly passed legislation on Wednesday formalising a "work plan" to suspend future elections, following months of constitutional changes under President Daniel Ortega that have steadily eliminated competitive political institutions, [according to The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/22/nicaragua-daniel-ortega-congress-work-plan-suspend-elections). The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights formally condemned the decision, calling it an assault on democratic norms and demanding that Ortega reverse course, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/22/un-rights-chief-condemns-nicaraguas-decision-to-abolish-elections). The move completes a trajectory that began with the 2018 suppression of anti-government protests, accelerated through the 2021 jailing of opposition presidential candidates before that year's election, and has now arrived at the explicit institutional abolition of the electoral process itself. Ortega has governed Nicaragua in various capacities since 1979, first as a revolutionary commander, then as elected president, and since 2007 in an increasingly authoritarian consolidation of power that his government continues to describe as the defence of the Sandinista revolution against imperialist subversion.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream liberal response to Nicaragua's democratic collapse is essentially correct as far as it goes: this is a grave regression, it violates the Inter-American Democratic Charter to which Nicaragua is a signatory, and the international community should respond with targeted sanctions, diplomatic isolation and sustained pressure through the Organization of American States and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights. The UN's condemnation is appropriate. The regime's framing — that elections are a tool of imperialist destabilisation — is a bad-faith cover for personal and dynastic power consolidation. (Ortega's wife, Rosario Murillo, is now vice-president, having already been described by observers as co-ruler.) Civil society, the Catholic Church and independent media in Nicaragua have all been systematically dismantled. The argument that this is merely a "different model of democracy" deserves no serious engagement.

That analysis is broadly right. The question is what follows from it — and here the mainstream framing becomes thinner.

## A different read

The international community's response to Nicaragua's democratic collapse has been, by any honest measure, ineffectual.

Ortega has been sanctioned by the United States, the European Union and Canada. The OAS passed resolutions. The Inter-American Court has issued orders. None of it has altered the trajectory by one degree. Nicaragua continues to receive trade access to major markets through CAFTA-DR — the Central America Free Trade Agreement. Ortega's government continues to participate in regional bodies. The sanctions have hurt ordinary Nicaraguans without dislodging the regime. This is not an unusual pattern: targeted sanctions against entrenched authoritarian regimes have a weak empirical track record unless they are accompanied by credible military threat or genuine multilateral economic isolation. Neither is on offer in Nicaragua's case.

What makes today's announcement significant beyond the particular tragedy of Nicaragua is what it reveals about the state of the liberal international order more broadly. The past decade has produced democratic backsliding in Hungary, Turkey, Venezuela, Myanmar, Belarus, and now the explicit abolition of elections in Nicaragua — and in each case, the international response has followed the same pattern: condemnation, targeted sanctions, continued trade and diplomatic engagement, and ultimately accommodation of the new reality. The UN Human Rights Commissioner's condemnation, [reported by Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/22/un-rights-chief-condemns-nicaraguas-decision-to-abolish-elections), is the formulaic step in a predictable process that leads nowhere.

The harder question — which Western governments consistently avoid — is whether the architecture of conditional engagement (trade access, multilateral membership, aid flows) actually reinforces authoritarian durability by giving regimes both the resources they need and the symbolic legitimacy of international participation, without attaching meaningful costs to democratic regression. The Latin American left, which once had a principled commitment to electoral democracy as the antidote to military juntas, has largely failed to condemn Ortega — partly out of ideological solidarity, partly because Venezuela's Maduro has already normalised the behaviour and faced no significant price. The Guardian's reporting on Nicaragua's congressional "work plan" notes the absence of significant regional government reaction, [according to The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/22/nicaragua-daniel-ortega-congress-work-plan-suspend-elections).

There is also a specific migrant and refugee dimension. Nicaragua under increasing autocratic pressure has been a steady source of emigration to the United States — through Mexico and the Darién Gap. Abolishing elections removes the last institutional mechanism for peaceful political change, which reliably increases political persecution, and which reliably increases emigration. The countries most morally outraged about Nicaragua's democratic collapse are the same countries whose migration policies make no distinction between political refugees from autocracies and economic migrants from stable democracies. If the rules-based order is worth anything, the moment of democratic abolition is precisely when it should be capable of imposing real costs. The muted response tells you something about what the order actually prioritises.

## What to watch

Watch whether CAFTA-DR trade privileges for Nicaragua become a legislative issue in Washington — that is the one lever of real economic pressure available to the United States. Watch whether the OAS invokes the Democratic Charter's suspension mechanism, which has been used only once (against Honduras in 2009) and which would give Ortega's isolation real institutional weight. Watch migration data from Nicaragua over the next six months: a new wave of political emigrants would place the humanitarian cost of electoral abolition on the desks of US and Mexican officials in a way that UN resolutions do not. And watch Cuba and Venezuela for whether they formally endorse Nicaragua's move — their explicit solidarity would clarify the emerging bloc of electoral autocracies in the hemisphere.
