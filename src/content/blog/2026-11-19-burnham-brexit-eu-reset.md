Speaking at the Lord Mayor's Banquet in London, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham declared that [the "Brexit we were given has not worked"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crp3e1n5kezpo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — the most explicit acknowledgement from a sitting British Prime Minister that the 2016 referendum settlement has failed to deliver its promised benefits. This comes days before a scheduled EU-UK summit, and follows former Foreign Secretary David Lammy's call for [Burnham to take Britain back into the European Union](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm9q8jx05q3go?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). Burnham subsequently clarified he will [set out options for a future relationship](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwm2j3nyd44ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) alongside his ten-year economic plan — deliberately ambiguous language that keeps maximum political flexibility while signalling a clear directional shift. The backdrop is a [UK energy inflation crisis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmy498kdkx3xo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — typical household bills forecast to rise by £443 per year from January — that is sharpening the economic argument for deeper European trade integration and removing the fiscal headroom that might otherwise insulate the government from its trading losses.

## The received wisdom

The pro-European case is now being made openly and by sitting ministers: Brexit has cost Britain export access, reduced City of London competitiveness, constrained labour supply in key sectors, and left the country outside the regulatory frameworks that determine access to European markets. The numbers, in this reading, are unambiguous. Business surveys, ONS trade data, and independent economic analysis have consistently shown a post-Brexit trade drag, particularly in services and food exports. Lammy's intervention is not a fringe position; it reflects a growing mainstream sentiment that the political cost of reversing course is now lower than the economic cost of staying on the current trajectory. For this view, Burnham's Lord Mayor's Banquet speech is an overdue statement of the obvious, and the question is not whether to reset but how quickly.

This case is not without empirical grounding. The trade data is what it is; the complexity and cost of post-Brexit customs procedures for small businesses have been documented exhaustively.

## A different read

What the pro-European narrative consistently underweights is the political sociology of a re-entry process that is very much harder than the rhetoric of "working more closely with Europe" implies — and the degree to which the technocratic class's confidence in its own diagnosis is itself part of the problem.

Begin with the arithmetic of re-accession, or even of significant reintegration. The European Union has changed since 2016. Its fiscal architecture has evolved through COVID recovery instruments; its regulatory reach has expanded through the Digital Markets Act, the AI Act, and the Green Deal. Rejoining the single market in any meaningful sense now means accepting a considerably more interventionist EU regulatory framework than the one Britain left. For a government already navigating a [welfare reform debate](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck79210y125xo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) on domestic grounds and trying to restore fiscal credibility, absorbing EU state aid rules, procurement directives, and labour market regulations would constrain precisely the economic flexibility that Burnham needs. David Lammy's vision of a smooth return to European embrace does not grapple with what that embrace now costs.

There is also a democratic legitimacy problem that neither Burnham nor Lammy has yet honestly addressed. The 2016 referendum was a 52-48 result; subsequent elections have not produced a clear mandate for reversal. Proceeding with significant reintegration through executive option-setting rather than a fresh referendum treats the electorate as a problem to be managed rather than a constituency to be persuaded. This is precisely the managerial disposition — we know what's good for you and we'll do it on a ten-year plan — that created the conditions for the original Brexit vote. The lesson of 2016 was not that the British public was wrong; it was that a political class that had stopped explaining and justifying its choices lost the argument by default. Burnham risks repeating the error in reverse.

The [rising energy bills](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmy498kdkx3xo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) deserve a note here. The energy price spike is a European-wide phenomenon driven by infrastructure underinvestment and Russian gas exposure — not, primarily, a Brexit consequence. Using it as an argument for EU reintegration is analytically opportunistic. Britain's energy market is tightly coupled to European gas prices regardless of its political relationship with Brussels, and no plausible trading arrangement with the EU would have insulated UK households from the fossil fuel price volatility that stems from decisions made in Moscow and Riyadh.

The right-of-centre position is not that Brexit was managed brilliantly — it was not, and the implementation failures were often self-inflicted. It is that the solution is not to relitigate the constitutional question under economic pressure, but to do the patient work of building bilateral trade agreements, reducing friction in specific sectors through targeted mutual recognition deals, and accepting that the British economy will adapt through enterprise rather than regulatory reintegration. That is slower and less satisfying than the pro-European narrative. It is also more honest about what is actually on offer from Brussels.

## What to watch

- The substance of the EU-UK summit: will it produce concrete mutual recognition agreements in specific sectors (financial services, agri-food), or will it remain at the level of atmospherics?
- Whether Burnham commits to a referendum before any significant reintegration move — the absence of that commitment would be politically explosive.
- The [government's welfare reform](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck79210y125xo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) and energy cost trajectory as a constraint on political capital: a government fighting domestic fires has limited bandwidth for a European constitutional renegotiation.
- Whether Reform UK, now a substantial parliamentary force, frames EU reintegration moves as the decisive political battleground for the next election — turning what Burnham intends as a pragmatic adjustment into a culture war.
