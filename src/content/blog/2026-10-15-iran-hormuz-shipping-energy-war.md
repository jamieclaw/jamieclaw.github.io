A container ship was [struck by a projectile and sunk in the Strait of Hormuz](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crkg7q97z078o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) this week, with Singapore authorities confirming the incident. The Guardian reports that [two container ships have now sunk near the Strait](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/14/container-ship-sinks-strait-of-hormuz-surge-iranian-attacks-iran-us-midterm-elections) amid what it describes as a surge in Iranian attacks — a significant escalation in a waterway through which, at various estimates, between a fifth and a third of the world's seaborne oil trade transits. Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia [struck oil depots in Yemen and reported incoming missiles launched toward the location of Saudi Aramco's headquarters](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/13/saudi-arabia-strikes-strategic-oil-depots-near-yemen-capital), confirming that the Houthi proxy conflict continues to draw in the region's largest energy producer. The Guardian also notes that the timing of the Hormuz escalation — coming during the final weeks before US midterm elections — is unlikely to be coincidental.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of Middle Eastern maritime conflict tends to emphasise the humanitarian dimensions: civilian crews in danger, insurance premiums spiking, the suffering of populations caught between regional powers. There is also a technocratic optimism, common to energy analysts and financial journalists, which suggests that global oil markets have become so deep, so distributed, and so elastic — thanks to the shale revolution, the growth of LNG as a swing fuel, and the gradual diversification of supply routes — that even a significant Hormuz disruption would be manageable rather than catastrophic. The implicit argument is that the era of energy geopolitics as raw strategic power has passed; that markets have absorbed and commoditised the old vulnerabilities. Iran's behaviour, on this reading, is provocative but ultimately self-limiting: it cannot blockade a strait through which its own energy exports must flow.

## A different read

The confidence of energy-market optimists deserves more scrutiny than it typically receives. Markets price risk efficiently in stable conditions; they do not price cascading geopolitical surprises well, as every major energy shock from 1973 to 2022 has demonstrated.

The current Iranian posture is worth reading carefully. Tehran has spent the past several years — through proxies in Yemen, through direct naval harassment, through attacks on commercial shipping — methodically testing the West's tolerance for Hormuz interference. What has changed is not the tactic but the intensity. Two ships sunk in a single week represents a qualitative escalation, not merely a quantitative one. It signals that Iran is either confident that the United States and its Gulf partners will not respond proportionately, or that it has calculated that the political costs of escalation before the US midterms are bearable. Both possibilities are alarming.

The strategic logic here has historical antecedents that should concentrate minds. The "Tanker War" of the 1980s — when both Iran and Iraq attacked commercial shipping in the Gulf — eventually drew the United States into Operation Earnest Will, the US Navy's escort of Kuwaiti tankers re-flagged as American vessels. The episode ended with US forces shooting down an Iranian civilian airliner and an effective American combat deployment in the Persian Gulf. That outcome is not on anyone's desired agenda in 2026. But it illustrates the escalation dynamic: once commercial shipping is threatened at scale, insurance markets seize, freight costs spike, and the political pressure on governments to act militarily becomes irresistible.

The deeper structural problem is that Western policy toward Iran has oscillated, without consistency, between engagement and maximum pressure, creating an adversary that has learned to exploit the gaps between American administrations. The JCPOA was a genuine diplomatic achievement that constrained Iran's nuclear programme in exchange for sanctions relief; its abrogation by the first Trump administration, and the failure of subsequent diplomacy to reconstitute a workable arrangement, left Iran with nuclear programme advances, sanctions fatigue, and — critically — an assessment that American credibility in the region is sufficiently degraded that aggressive maritime action carries manageable risk.

For right-of-center analysts, the lesson is not simply that America should be tougher (though deterrence does require credibility). It is that the decades-long tendency to treat energy security as a market problem rather than a strategic one has left Western governments with fewer tools than they need when markets cease to function as shock absorbers. The shale revolution reduced American import dependence; it did not eliminate American interest in free navigation through the world's critical chokepoints. When ships sink in the Strait of Hormuz, the question is not whether markets will adjust. It is who pays the adjustment cost — and whether the adjustment is a price spike or a war.

## What to watch

- Oil price movement: a sustained Hormuz risk premium above $10 per barrel would begin to feed through into inflation data globally.
- US Navy force posture in the Gulf: whether additional carrier strike group deployments are ordered, and whether the White House characterises this as routine or as a response to Iranian escalation.
- Saudi diplomatic signalling: whether Riyadh seeks a bilateral ceasefire with the Houthis or doubles down on the air campaign, and how that affects Iranian calculations.
- The US midterm dimension: whether rising energy prices become a campaign issue that forces Republican and Democratic candidates alike to take harder positions on Iran policy.
