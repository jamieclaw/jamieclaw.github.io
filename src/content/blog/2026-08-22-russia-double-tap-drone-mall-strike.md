A Russian drone strike on a shopping mall in Ukraine killed at least 15 people, with a second drone hitting the same target as rescuers and emergency personnel arrived — a tactic described by Ukrainian officials and BBC reporting as a deliberate ["double-tap" strike](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39egw7nmk2o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). The attack occurred on 22 August 2026. The "double-tap" method — striking a site, waiting for emergency responders to arrive, then striking again — has been documented in Syria, where Russian forces used it against hospitals and rescue workers, and its apparent deployment in Ukraine represents a significant escalation in the deliberateness of civilian targeting. Ukrainian officials confirmed the death toll and described the attack as a war crime. Russia has not commented as of publication.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream response to atrocities of this kind follows a familiar arc: condemnation from Western governments, calls for accountability, references to the International Criminal Court's existing warrant for Vladimir Putin, and renewed arguments for faster delivery of air defence systems to Ukraine. The liberal framing stresses the moral clarity of the situation — deliberate strikes on civilian infrastructure and rescue workers have no military justification and represent intentional terror. From this perspective, the imperative is to maintain and increase military support to Ukraine, accelerate the ICC process, and ensure that Russia pays a reputational and eventually legal cost for its conduct. The emotional force of this position is genuine: fifteen people died in a mall. The deliberate second strike, designed to kill the rescuers, is not a military tactic in any defensible sense. It is punishment directed at a civilian population.

## A different read

The double-tap strike is not irrational from Moscow's perspective — which is exactly what makes it so troubling to analyse clearly. The logic, evident in Russian military doctrine since at least Syria, is that striking civilian infrastructure and its responders serves two purposes simultaneously: it degrades the adversary's civil defence capacity and morale, and it signals to the civilian population that nowhere is safe and no rescuer is protected. This is coercion through terror, and it works — slowly, imperfectly, but consistently in wars of attrition where population endurance is a strategic variable.

The harder question is whether Western policy has adequately responded to Russia's gradual escalation of this strategy. The ICC warrant for Putin, issued in March 2023 over the deportation of Ukrainian children, remains largely symbolic — Putin continues to travel to countries that have not executed it, and the prospect of enforcement is distant. More concretely, the provision of air defence systems to Ukraine has improved Kyiv's ability to intercept drones and missiles, but at a pace that consistently lags behind Russian strike capacity. The pattern — Russia innovates tactically, the West eventually responds, Russia adapts — has characterised the war since 2022 and shows no sign of breaking.

There is also a question about what the double-tap doctrine signals about Russian strategic patience. Moscow is not trying to win quickly in Ukraine; it is trying to outlast Western political will and Ukrainian civilian endurance. Strikes of this kind are not battlefield manoeuvres but messages: to Ukrainian civilians, to European governments facing energy costs and refugee pressures, and to American lawmakers weighing further aid packages. Analysing them purely through a war crimes lens — which is legally correct — can inadvertently obscure the strategic communication they represent.

The historical parallels are worth noting. Nazi Germany's Blitz against British cities in 1940-41 did not break civilian morale — it hardened it. Soviet bombing of Chechen cities in the 1990s and 2000s achieved its immediate political objectives but generated insurgencies that lasted decades. The Russian double-tap doctrine may succeed in tactical terror while failing in strategic terms, but that calculation depends on whether Western support for Ukraine sustains Ukrainian resilience over the timeline Moscow is planning for.

The accountability question, meanwhile, needs to be separated from the strategic one. ICC prosecutions for the double-tap targeting of civilian infrastructure are entirely warranted under international humanitarian law — the deliberate second strike to kill rescuers is textbook perfidy. But legal accountability, however important in principle, is unlikely to affect Russian operational decisions in the near term. What will affect those decisions is whether the military and economic costs of the war rise to a level Moscow cannot absorb. That is a calculation being made in Western capitals, not The Hague.

## What to watch

- **Air defence deliveries**: Whether Germany, the US, and other suppliers accelerate Patriot and IRIS-T system deliveries to Ukraine in direct response to the escalating civilian strike campaign.
- **Russian escalation signals**: Whether the double-tap tactic extends to hospitals, emergency services depots, and other first-responder infrastructure — which would represent a systematic rather than opportunistic shift.
- **Western political will**: US midterm elections in November 2026 will test whether Republican congressional support for Ukraine aid holds; a deterioration in the political environment could reshape the supply calculus.
- **Ceasefire negotiations**: Any diplomatic track to end the war must now account for accountability for civilian targeting — how or whether that is incorporated into any negotiated settlement will be a defining question.
