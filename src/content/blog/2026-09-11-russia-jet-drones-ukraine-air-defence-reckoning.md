Russia fired its new rapid, jet-powered Geran drones against Ukraine every single day throughout August — the first time such daily attacks have been recorded in Ukrainian Air Force data, according to a BBC Verify investigation published this week. [The analysis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c804d9lyj2jo) found that attacks by the jet-powered variant grew from 350 in June to 1,600 in July and 2,800 in August. The drones cruise at over 300mph — significantly faster than previous models — and their turbojet engines are, according to Ukrainian military intelligence, partly sourced from China. Ukraine's interception rate, which stood at 94 percent for long-range drones earlier this year, has been falling. In five nightly attacks in August, the new jet drones outnumbered older propeller-driven variants. President Volodymyr Zelensky condemned an unfolding "horror" over the skies of Kyiv; on 1 September, a wave of drone attacks killed at least twelve people and injured twenty more, including six railway workers whose depot in Kyiv was struck directly.

## The received wisdom

The conventional analysis of Russia's drone campaign treats it primarily as a terror weapon — a tool of civilian punishment and psychological pressure designed to exhaust Ukrainian morale rather than achieve decisive military effects. By this reading, the escalation in drone volume is evidence of Russian desperation: having failed to achieve battlefield superiority through conventional means, Moscow is lashing out at civilian infrastructure. Western policy circles have largely endorsed this view, arguing that continued military aid to Ukraine will eventually grind down Russia's capacity to sustain such attacks.

The supplementary progressive argument holds that the real culprit for Ukraine's air-defence deficit is the United States and its European allies, who have been too slow to transfer modern interceptor systems. Delays and political hesitancy in NATO capitals are, on this view, directly responsible for Kyiv's suffering. It is hard to dismiss this entirely: the decisions to withhold long-range weapons early in the war, later partially reversed, did cost Ukraine time and tactical advantage.

## A different read

Both framings have merit, but neither fully grasps the doctrinal significance of what BBC Verify's analysis reveals. This is not simply an escalation in volume; it is a qualitative shift in kind.

The key insight from the RAND analyst Michael Bohnert, cited in the BBC's investigation, is that Russia has learned to combine "a greater variety of munitions" — jet drones, decoy drones, and ballistic missiles — in tactically complex swarms that overwhelm layered air-defence systems. The tactical intelligence required to sequence such attacks has improved markedly since 2022. Ukraine's Patriot batteries, already globally scarce due to demand from the US-Israeli campaign against Iran, are being consumed defending against missiles that the newer, cheaper jet drones are now designed to slip past.

John Hardie of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies characterises the new generation of Geran drones as "basically a poor man's cruise missile." That metaphor is important. Cruise missiles require sophisticated, expensive production lines. Drones with turbojet engines can be manufactured at industrial scale in ways that Western planners assumed were beyond Russia's degraded defence-industrial capacity. They were wrong. The new Tsymbulova base in the Oryol region, visible in satellite imagery analysed by BBC Verify, shows dedicated long-rail infrastructure for the jet variants — [a permanent investment, not an improvised workaround](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c804d9lyj2jo).

There is a broader strategic lesson here that Western capitals need to absorb. The arms-race dynamic in drone warfare is compressing rapidly. Ukraine has responded by developing its own interceptor missiles — four Ukrainian companies are now testing systems — and new defence minister Yevhenii Khmara has tasked them to produce "thousands of units" as quickly as possible. But there is an inherent lag between identifying a threat and fielding a counter at scale. In the meantime, Ukrainian civilians are paying the price.

The fact that some of the turbojet engines are reportedly sourced from China adds another layer of strategic complexity. China has maintained a posture of formal neutrality while providing what Western intelligence agencies have characterised as dual-use materiel to Russia throughout the war. Each time this technology appears on a Ukrainian battlefield, it forces a policy question that the Biden and Trump administrations have both struggled to answer directly: at what point does Chinese material support for Russia's war effort constitute a casus belli for tougher action against Beijing's economic interests?

History offers a sobering precedent. The technological escalation in World War One — machine guns, poison gas, the aerial bomb — consistently outpaced both defensive responses and the strategic thinking of the generals commanding armies. The Western response to Russia's drone escalation has been reactive throughout. If Ukraine is to stabilise its skies before winter, when drone attacks on heating infrastructure become existential rather than merely terrible, the pace of counter-development needs to accelerate dramatically.

Moldova's government this week reported that a Russian drone — reportedly jet-powered — entered Moldovan airspace, delaying President Zelensky's plane at Chisinau airport. That is a direct territorial violation of a European state. NATO's silence on this particular incident is conspicuous.

## What to watch

- Ukraine's ability to deploy domestic jet-interceptor missiles at scale before winter: the next three months are critical to civilian infrastructure protection.
- Whether NATO allies use the Moldovan airspace violation as a trigger for tightening air-defence support, or allow it to pass without formal response.
- China's diplomatic position if Western governments formally link Chinese component supply to Russian drone attacks — a confrontation that has so far been avoided.
- Russian production capacity at Tsymbulova and similar bases: the pace of drone manufacture will determine whether August's 2,800 figure is a peak or a baseline.
