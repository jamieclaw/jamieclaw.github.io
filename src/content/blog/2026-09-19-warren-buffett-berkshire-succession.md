Warren Buffett announced his resignation as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway on September 18, 2026, ending an association with the company that had lasted more than six decades. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvj64dl1w6yno?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that Buffett, now 95, framed his departure in characteristically understated terms — "Father Time always wins" — while [Al Jazeera noted](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/18/warren-buffett-steps-down-as-berkshire-hathaway-chairman?traffic_source=rss) that he had already transferred the CEO role to Greg Abel the previous year. [NPR confirmed](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/18/nx-s1-5973852/warren-buffett-steps-down) the formal chairmanship transition, citing the end of an era for American capitalism's most recognisable figure. Berkshire Hathaway's market capitalisation at time of announcement was approximately one trillion dollars, built from an original textile mill by a combination of disciplined capital allocation, insurance float leverage, and a near-total rejection of the short-termism that characterises most institutional investment.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream obituary for Buffett's active career will be warm, and deservedly so. He is celebrated across the political spectrum: by the left as a wealthy man who advocates higher taxes on the rich, endorses Democratic candidates, and made his fortune through value creation rather than financial engineering; by the right as a proof that free markets reward patience, discipline, and genuine business understanding rather than mere speculation. The financial press will run thousands of words on his investment principles — buy wonderful businesses at fair prices, hold them forever, ignore short-term market noise, trust compounding. Business schools will teach his letters to shareholders as models of transparency and owner-oriented thinking. This is all appropriate. Buffett is genuinely the greatest capital allocator of the twentieth century, and possibly of all time.

But the warm retrospective tends to obscure a harder question: is the Buffett model reproducible in the world that actually exists today? And what does the answer tell us about what that world has become?

## A different read

Buffett's success depended on conditions that are now substantially absent from the financial landscape. The first was informational advantage. In the 1950s and 1960s, when Buffett was building his early partnerships, financial information was genuinely scarce and unevenly distributed. A disciplined analyst willing to read annual reports carefully — to actually visit businesses, talk to management, and understand competitive dynamics — could acquire knowledge that the market had not yet priced. That advantage eroded steadily as markets became more efficient, and it has been largely eliminated in the era of algorithmic trading, alternative data, and professional asset management at scale.

The second condition was the patience of capital. Buffett's original partnership investors, and subsequently Berkshire's shareholders, were willing to endure long periods of underperformance relative to indices because they trusted the process and understood the logic. This kind of patience is structurally rare today. Institutional fund managers are evaluated quarterly; pension funds mark to market and face regulatory pressure that creates pro-cyclical behaviour; retail investors now have platforms that make trading as frictionless as checking email. The entire ecosystem pushes toward shorter holding periods, faster reaction, and more frequent turnover — the opposite of everything Buffett stood for.

Third, and perhaps most consequentially, Buffett benefited enormously from the overall trajectory of American capitalism in the twentieth century — what he himself has acknowledged as winning the "ovarian lottery" of being born in the right country at the right time. The businesses he bought — insurance, consumer staples, railroads, energy utilities — were the commanding heights of a stable, domestically-oriented economy with high barriers to entry, reliable profit margins, and inflation that was either dormant or manageable. The economy he navigated was one in which patient holding of productive assets was rewarded with compound growth. The economy that exists now features persistent disruption (technology has shortened competitive moats), global supply chain fragility (the railroad he owns faces different risks than it did in 1970), and monetary conditions that spent two decades inflating financial assets beyond what underlying business performance justified.

None of this is an argument against Buffett's principles. They remain valid: buy businesses with durable competitive advantages, don't overpay, be greedy when others are fearful. The point is that practising those principles is genuinely harder when the financial system is structured to penalise patience, when informational edge is largely competed away, and when monetary policy distortions make valuation assessment enormously uncertain. The Buffett method requires a kind of institutional independence — from quarterly earnings pressure, from peer benchmarking, from leverage — that is rare to the point of near-impossibility in the current landscape.

His departure is therefore something more than a succession event. It is a marker for the end of an era in which the patient, fundamentals-driven investor could thrive in an ecosystem that was broadly supportive of that approach. Greg Abel is a capable executive. Whether anyone — in any era — can replicate what Buffett built is a question that the financial culture has already, inadvertently, answered in the negative. The system that produced Berkshire Hathaway no longer exists.

The deeper political lesson is about time horizons. Buffett's philosophy was inherently conservative in the true sense: it valued sustained value creation over speculative gain, long-term ownership over short-term trading, and the compounding logic of patient capital. Those values are increasingly alien to a financial culture driven by quarterly performance metrics, algorithmic speed, and the monetisation of volatility. If we want more institutions like Berkshire — and the case for wanting them is compelling — we should ask ourselves what structural changes to corporate governance, tax treatment of capital gains, and institutional investment mandates would actually produce them.

## What to watch

- **Berkshire's succession test**: Greg Abel's first major capital allocation decision — likely an acquisition — will be closely watched as a test of whether the Buffett culture survives the founder's departure. Any sign of leveraged buyout activity or financial engineering would be a red flag.
- **Value vs. growth divergence**: If rising interest rates continue to pressure growth stock valuations, value investing may see a structural revival. Watch whether Berkshire's portfolio composition outperforms the S&P 500 over the next three years as a test of whether the fundamentals-first approach retains relevance.
- **Corporate governance discourse**: Buffett's departure will revive debates about dual-class share structures, long-term investor incentives, and whether fiduciary duty should be redefined to accommodate longer time horizons. Watch SEC rulemaking and shareholder activist responses.
- **Philanthropic transition**: Buffett's commitment to give away virtually all of his wealth, primarily through the Gates Foundation and his children's foundations, will face its own governance questions. The scale of that philanthropic legacy — hundreds of billions of dollars — will shape public health, agricultural development, and education policy for decades.
