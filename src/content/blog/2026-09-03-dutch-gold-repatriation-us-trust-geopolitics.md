De Nederlandsche Bank announced on Wednesday that it had moved 86 tonnes of gold reserves from the United States and Canada to London over the period from March to August, citing "increasing geopolitical unrest." The Dutch central bank, which holds a total of 612.4 tonnes of gold valued at approximately €72bn, said the reallocation was designed to improve the "deployability" of its reserves in a crisis. Before the transfer, 31.3% of Dutch gold was held in New York and 19.7% in Ottawa; afterwards, each accounts for 18.5%, while the London share rose from 18.1% to 32.1%. DNB President Olaf Sleijpen said the London market allowed the bank to "[deploy gold more quickly in a crisis situation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/03/netherlands-gold-dutch-central-bank-uk-us-and-canada-geopolitical-unrest)" than reserves held in New York or Ottawa. The bank was careful to say it "assumed it would never need to deploy the gold," but the language of resilience and preparedness was prominent throughout its statement.

## The received wisdom

Financial commentators have largely treated the Dutch move as a technical and prudent portfolio adjustment — the sensible act of a responsible central bank ensuring that its reserve assets are held in the most liquid and accessible venue. London's gold market is, by most measures, the deepest and most active in the world; the Bank of England's vaults are the standard custodial facility for international reserves. On this interpretation, the move reflects rational liquidity management rather than any fundamental shift in confidence in the United States. Paris-based gold market president Laurent Schwartz noted that central banks have been moving reserves around for a decade, and John Plassard of Cité Gestion called it a "fairly one-off move" that would only become significant if others followed. The Bundesbank, conspicuously, has decided not to shift its own New York holdings, with a spokesperson saying the New York Fed "is and remains an important storage site for our gold."

## A different read

The prudent-liquidity framing is accurate as far as it goes. But it does not fully account for the timing or the language. The DNB announcement explicitly cited "increasing geopolitical unrest" — not routine portfolio rebalancing, not updated liquidity modelling, but geopolitical unrest. And that unrest has a specific geography: the Trump administration's conduct toward its traditional allies, the weaponisation of the dollar and of US-controlled financial infrastructure, and the six-month Iran war that has rattled global financial markets and pushed bond yields across the developed world to multi-decade highs.

The signal embedded in the DNB's move is subtle but clear to anyone watching the architecture of global finance. Central banks hold gold precisely because it is the one reserve asset that does not depend on the creditworthiness or political reliability of any issuing government. The decision to move those holdings closer to home — or to a venue that is more easily accessible without American intermediation — is a hedge against the scenario in which American financial infrastructure becomes less reliably neutral than it has been since 1945.

This is not an abstract concern. The US government has in recent years demonstrated a willingness to use its control over the dollar clearing system as a geopolitical weapon: the freezing of Russian central bank assets in 2022, the secondary sanctions architecture targeting Iran, the use of SWIFT access as leverage in diplomatic disputes. These are all legitimate policy tools from Washington's perspective. But they have had a predictable effect on the calculations of every other central bank in the world: if the US can freeze Russia's $300bn in reserves, it can, in principle, freeze anyone's. The prudent response, for a central bank with fiduciary obligations to its government and people, is to ensure that at least some reserves are held outside of American custody.

At the beginning of 2026, there were [reported concerns in Germany about the security of Bundesbank reserves in New York](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/03/netherlands-gold-dutch-central-bank-uk-us-and-canada-geopolitical-unrest). The Bundesbank chose not to act on those concerns — but the fact that they were publicly aired is itself significant. The question is not whether the Netherlands is staging a withdrawal from the dollar-centred financial order; it is not. The question is whether the DNB's move is the first visible data point in a longer trend that, if it accelerates, would represent a genuine erosion of the institutional architecture that has underwritten American power since Bretton Woods.

The answer to that question depends largely on American behaviour. The Iran war, with its Strait of Hormuz disruption and its contribution to the bond yield wildfire that [BBC Economics Editor Faisal Islam described as "keeping world leaders up at night"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0rez9zegj5o), is an American-origin shock to the global financial system. The administration's response to the resulting market instability — including what Lord Jim O'Neill described as "efforts by the US Government to try to manage down surging yields" — has introduced its own uncertainty. Central banks that are watching the 30-year US Treasury yield rise above levels not seen in a generation are doing exactly what they should be doing: preparing for scenarios they hope will never arrive.

## What to watch

- Whether other European central banks — particularly the Bundesbank, which holds roughly 1,200 tonnes of gold at the New York Fed — publicly reassess their storage arrangements in the coming months.
- The Bank of England's disclosure of any significant new inflows of foreign central bank gold to its vaults, which would indicate the DNB move is part of a broader pattern.
- US Treasury yield behaviour as the November midterms approach: if yields continue rising, the feedback loop between the Iran war, inflation, and sovereign borrowing costs will intensify.
- Any formal joint European statement about the security of frozen Russian assets held in Belgium — if Belgium's position softens and those assets are deployed for Ukraine, it will signal a further willingness to use reserve assets as a geopolitical instrument, which cuts both ways.
