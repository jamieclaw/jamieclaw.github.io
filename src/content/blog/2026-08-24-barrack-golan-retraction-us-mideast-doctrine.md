Tom Barrack, the US Ambassador to Turkiye and envoy for Syria, was forced to retract a comment he made last week in which he acknowledged that Israel "still occupy the Golan against the UN resolutions, against all of the international order." [Barrack issued a corrective statement](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/23/us-envoy-tom-barrack-retracts-comment-that-syrias-golan-is-occupied) on Sunday restating that "United States policy on the Golan was set by President Trump in 2019 and is unchanged." The original remarks, made in a podcast interview with Mario Nawfal, drew immediate condemnation from pro-Israel commentators, including far-right figures who demanded his resignation. Republican Senator Rick Scott said Barrack had been "confirmed by the Senate to enact Trump's agenda" and that he had "failed to do his research." Barrack had also separately suggested that recent Israeli air strikes on a Syrian airbase may have been intended to bait Turkiye ahead of US midterm elections — a comment that drew further criticism. Israel occupied the Golan during the 1967 Six-Day War and annexed it in 1981; Trump recognised Israeli sovereignty in 2019. No country had done so before. Colombia recently joined the US in backing the claim.

## The received wisdom

The swift condemnation of Barrack and his rapid self-correction reflect what has become the unwritten rule of US Middle East policy: the 2019 Golan recognition is settled fact, and any deviation from it — even an inadvertent statement of the international legal consensus — is treated as a breach of loyalty to the administration. The enforcement mechanism is not bureaucratic but social and political: Laura Loomer tweets, Rick Scott posts a quote from Trump's executive order, and the errant official folds. The broader argument for this posture is strategic coherence. If the US is aligned with Israel — and it is — then ambiguity about the legal status of territories Israel controls serves no one. Better to state the position clearly and consistently than to introduce uncertainty that adversaries can exploit. Biden's team agreed with this logic when they declined to reverse Trump's Golan recognition, even as they promoted the "rules-based international order" in virtually every other context.

## A different read

What makes the Barrack episode instructive is not what he said — the legal position he articulated is, by any honest accounting, accurate — but the speed and intensity with which it was rejected. He did not claim the Golan should be returned to Syria. He did not call for a policy change. He stated, as a matter of record, what UN Security Council resolutions say. The fact that this statement required a public retraction tells you everything you need to know about the current intellectual condition of American foreign policy on this question.

The specific importance is doctrinal. The post-World War II international order was built on a small number of foundational prohibitions, the most important of which was the inadmissibility of acquiring territory by force. This prohibition was not invented by progressives or multilateralists; it emerged directly from the catastrophic experience of the 1930s, when great powers successively legitimised territorial grabs — the Rhineland, Austria, the Sudetenland — until the logic of non-resistance produced a world war. The US sponsored the UN Charter language embodying this prohibition. It invoked that prohibition when opposing Soviet expansionism, when condemning Argentina's seizure of the Falklands, and for decades when addressing the Golan itself.

[Legal experts warned at the time of Trump's 2019 recognition](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/23/us-envoy-tom-barrack-retracts-comment-that-syrias-golan-is-occupied) that it "could upend a core tenet of the post-World War II global order." Biden's decision not to reverse it — made quietly, with minimal public acknowledgement — was an instance of what might be called geopolitical drift: the tacit abandonment of a principle that no one quite has the courage to formally repudiate. The Burnham government in Britain, the EU, and most G7 allies still formally hold that the Golan is occupied Syrian territory under international law. The US position is now formally at odds with that consensus, and the consensus is held by its allies, not merely by adversaries.

The Colombian recognition of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan, announced earlier this month, is worth noting. Colombia's new right-wing government joined the US — not against progressive critics but against the international legal consensus. This is how norms erode: not through a single dramatic repudiation but through incremental accretion of exception-makers, each citing the last as precedent. Russia's case for recognising Crimea as Russian territory uses precisely this template, citing Kosovo's independence and the Golan recognition as evidence that the West applies the prohibitions selectively.

None of this means the Golan's political status can or should be simply reversed by American fiat. Israel has administered the territory for nearly sixty years; the Syrian state that notionally retains legal title spent decades shelling the Golan's Israeli inhabitants from its heights. The practical case for maintaining the current de facto situation is real. What Barrack's gaffe revealed is that the US now lacks even the vocabulary to acknowledge the legal from the practical dimension — a conflation that, applied globally, produces a world where might determines right and the rules-based order becomes a selective instrument of the powerful.

## What to watch

- **Whether Barrack remains in post.** His comments about Israeli strikes "baiting Turkiye" were more politically explosive than the Golan remark. Ankara is watching closely; Turkish-Israeli tensions over Syria are a structural fault line that the US cannot easily manage if its own envoy is being publicly humiliated by domestic hardliners.
- **Russia's use of Golan precedent.** Moscow has already cited the 2019 recognition in its own annexation arguments. Watch for Russian diplomatic statements about Crimea in the weeks ahead.
- **Congress on the Golan.** No legislation has ever codified the 2019 recognition. A post-midterm Congress with different arithmetic could theoretically revisit it — a long shot, but a relevant one.
- **Colombia's regional standing.** Its recognition of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan is the first in Latin America. Whether other regional governments follow or distance themselves will be a barometer of US influence in the hemisphere.
