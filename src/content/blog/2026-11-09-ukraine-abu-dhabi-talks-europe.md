Europe is forcing itself back into the room. France's foreign minister Jean-Noël Barrot announced Sunday that French and European officials would attend this week's [US-brokered Ukraine peace talks in Abu Dhabi](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/08/nx-s1-6034014/europe-join-us-brokered-ukraine-talks-abu-dhabi-russia), overriding Kremlin objections that the talks were a trilateral format — Washington, Moscow, and Kyiv — that had already been agreed. The meetings, set for Tuesday and Wednesday, will be the fourth round of US-mediated negotiations and the first since February. Russia's delegation will be led once again by Vladimir Medinsky, though it will this time include Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin, and military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov — a more senior lineup that the Institute for the Study of War assessed could indicate either genuine readiness to negotiate or a determined effort to [slow the process and press maximalist demands](https://understandingwar.org/research/russia-ukraine/russian-offensive-campaign-assessment-november-7-2026/). The backdrop is grimly familiar: even as diplomats travel, Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles at the Kyiv area overnight Saturday, with only four intercepted. The death toll from a guided-bomb strike on an aid distribution site in Komyshuvakha has risen to at least 55.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing treats European re-entry as a straightforward win: democratic solidarity reasserts itself, the transatlantic alliance holds, and the multilateral architecture that has sustained Ukraine's resistance since 2022 is preserved. Zelenskyy's invocation of the 1945 Yalta conference — where Roosevelt and Churchill carved up Central Europe without consulting the nations affected — is read as legitimate historical anxiety that Western statesmanship must take seriously. The received wisdom further argues that a Democratic midterm victory in the United States, which Zelenskyy openly welcomed, improves the odds of continued aid packages and makes the incoming Congress a counterweight to any Trump inclination toward a hasty deal on Russia's terms. The diplomatic momentum, in this telling, is real and should be encouraged. France's intervention is proof that Europe has learned the lesson of being caught flat-footed when great powers negotiate over its head.

## A different read

There is a more uncomfortable reading, and it starts with Zelenskyy's own words. Ukraine's president told CNN he had [not been given details of the agreement Trump referenced](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/06/politics/trump-ukraine-europe-deal-putin-envoy-intl) after meeting Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev. "For me, it's not simple, and it can be dangerous," he said, explicitly invoking Yalta. This is not the language of a leader who feels assured that his principal backer has his interests at heart.

The deeper problem is structural. Trump told reporters that any coming deal would involve "more than Ukraine" and might involve "all of Europe" — a framing that treats European security as a bargaining chip rather than a non-negotiable precondition. Russia, for its part, confirmed it would return to talks only after sanctions on Russian diesel exports were partially lifted, a concession that Ukraine strenuously opposed as it replenishes Moscow's war chest. The Kremlin's insistence on excluding Europe from the format until France broke ranks was not diplomatic pedantry; it was an attempt to ensure that no party at the table was structurally obligated to protect European interests.

Historically, the pattern here is older than the Cold War. The Congress of Vienna in 1815, celebrated as a triumph of conservative statecraft, succeeded partly because every major affected power sat at the table. The Paris Peace Conference of 1919, by contrast, allowed the great powers to impose settlements on smaller nations without adequate local agency, with consequences that reverberated for three decades. Bismarck's famous remark — that the whole of the Balkans was not worth the bones of a single Pomeranian grenadier — captured precisely the logic that Trump seems inclined to apply to Ukraine: a limited regional problem to be managed on great-power terms.

Former US Ambassador to Ukraine John Herbst has argued that [Putin has rejected every proposal Kyiv accepted](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/dispatches/putin-wants-peace-talks-he-doesnt-want-peace/) and prefers the theatre of negotiations to the substance. Russia's more senior delegation could, as the Institute for the Study of War warned, be a signal of harder rather than softer intentions — more officials means more bureaucratic obstructionism, more opportunities to slow-walk any concession. And there is a calendar logic: Russia knows the Democratic-controlled Congress arrives in January. If Moscow can extract American concessions before that date, when Trump is most motivated to deliver a deal while he still controls the legislative agenda, the Kremlin will have gotten something for nothing.

Europe's late arrival at the table is better than absence, but it does not resolve the fundamental asymmetry. France and its European partners can observe; whether they can veto, or even meaningfully delay, any framework Trump and Putin agree to is a different question entirely. The habits of alliance are not the same as enforceable commitments. Europe's defence spending has risen, but its dependence on US military logistics and intelligence-sharing for any credible deterrence posture remains profound. A continent that cannot defend itself alone cannot negotiate as an equal.

What is most striking about Zelenskyy's Yalta reference is that it may be the most accurate historical parallel available. In 1945, Roosevelt and Churchill had legitimate reasons to believe they were managing a war-ending settlement in good faith. The lesson is not that great-power diplomacy is inherently corrupt; it is that small states without hard security guarantees are always, in the end, negotiating from weakness. Ukraine's army has fought with extraordinary discipline and resilience, but that military reality must be translated into diplomatic leverage before the talks conclude — not promised for later.

## What to watch

Watch whether European officials at Abu Dhabi are seated at the negotiating table or confined to observer status — the distinction matters more than the optics of their presence. Watch Putin's response to the Democratic midterm results: a Congress that may restore Ukraine aid packages in January changes the calculus for both Moscow and Washington. Watch the Zelenskyy-Trump direct contact line; if Kyiv is briefed fully before rather than after any framework agreement, it suggests the US is treating Ukraine as a principal rather than a subject. And watch whether the Komyshuvakha death toll generates political pressure in Washington — atrocities committed during active negotiations have historically either collapsed talks or hardened resolve, rarely left both sides unmoved.
