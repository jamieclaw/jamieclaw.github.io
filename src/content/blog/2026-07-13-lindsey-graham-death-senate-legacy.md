Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina died on Saturday night, aged 71, following what his office described as a [brief and sudden illness](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/12/nx-s1-5890790/us-sen-lindsey-graham-dies). He had served in the United States Senate since 2003, was in the middle of a re-election campaign, and had been, in the estimation of most observers, one of the most consequential — and most contested — figures in the Republican Party of the Trump era. Tributes came from an unusually wide arc: Netanyahu, Zelenskyy, and Israel's far-right minister Itamar Ben-Gvir all released statements. [Al Jazeera noted](https://www.aljazeera.com/xml/rss/all.xml) that Graham had been a "hardline backer of US-Israeli wars." South Carolina Governor Henry McMaster faces an immediate decision on an interim appointment, followed by a special primary and a general election for the remaining years of Graham's term.

## The received wisdom

The eulogies will write themselves along familiar lines, and not without justice. Graham was, by any measure, a figure of genuine substance. He served as a military lawyer and JAG officer. He was the Senate's most consistent advocate for a forward-leaning American foreign policy: robust NATO commitments, generous Ukrainian military assistance, unflinching support for Israel. He was one of the few Republicans willing, at various points, to criticise his own party when he judged it had gone wrong — most memorably in the aftermath of 9/11, when he pushed for the Military Commissions Act, and in 2016, when he said of Donald Trump that he was "a race-baiting, xenophobic bigot" unfit for the presidency.

The standard mainstream reading will emphasise Graham's evolution from Trump critic to Trump ally as a form of political pragmatism — the adaptation of a practical man to changed circumstances. His defenders will note that he remained a genuine hawk in a party increasingly tempted by isolationism, and that his influence on US foreign and military policy was real and often constructive. [NPR described him as having been instrumental in enacting Trump's policy and staffing priorities](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/12/nx-s1-5890790/us-sen-lindsey-graham-dies). In Washington terms, that is high praise.

## A different read

There is another story, and it is worth telling alongside the hagiography, not because Graham was a bad man but because the trajectory of his career illuminates something important about the Republican Party and about the corrupting logic of political survival under pressure.

Graham circa 2015 was recognisable to conservatives of an older school: the Reaganite foreign policy hawk, the institutionalist who believed in the Senate as a deliberative body, the man who had forged a genuinely bipartisan friendship with John McCain. He was, in the language of the time, an "establishment Republican" — a description that in today's climate carries freight it was not designed to bear. When he called Trump a "race-baiting, xenophobic bigot," he was not posturing; the statement reflected a genuinely held view, expressed at professional cost, about the direction of his party.

What happened next is the interesting part. Graham did not simply update his priors as he learned more about Trump's governing style. He reversed them. And the reversal was total enough to invite comparisons — unflattering but not absurd — to the arc described by Whittaker Chambers, or more recently by figures like Chris Christie, who cycled through criticism and accommodation without ever quite finding a stable equilibrium. The question is not whether Graham's change of heart was sincere; it may well have been. The question is what structural incentives produced it and what those incentives mean for the institution he served.

The answer, I think, lies in the specific vulnerabilities of a senator from a state where the Trump coalition is hegemonic. South Carolina's Republican primary electorate is among the most reliably MAGA in the country. A senator who crossed Trump faced the realistic prospect of a primary challenge he could not survive. Graham chose survival, and survival required not merely accommodation but active cheerleading — appearing on Fox News, defending positions he had previously denounced, lending his credibility as a foreign policy expert to an administration whose foreign policy instincts he had once correctly diagnosed as dangerous.

[The BBC's Anthony Zurcher noted](https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/world/rss.xml) that Graham was "a political survivor of the MAGA era" — and survival is the operative word. But survival at what price? The Senate's institutional functions — deliberation, advice and consent, the treaty power, confirmation of judges — all depend on senators being willing to exercise independent judgment. When a figure of Graham's stature concludes that institutional independence is not worth the political cost, the cost falls not on him but on the institution. The accumulation of that kind of individual calculation is what has hollowed out the Senate as a co-equal branch of government.

This is not primarily a partisan point. The Senate has been weakened by similar dynamics on the Democratic side, though usually through different mechanisms — the hollowing out of committee expertise, the replacement of legislative work with messaging operations, the subordination of the chamber's deliberative function to a permanent campaign cycle. The Senate was designed to be, in Madison's phrase, the "great anchor" of the republic. It is less securely anchored than it was a generation ago, and the reason why is partly structural, partly cultural, and only partly traceable to any individual's choices.

Graham's legacy is thus genuinely mixed. His foreign policy convictions were real, and on Ukraine and the Indo-Pacific he was often right when it mattered. His institutional accommodations were corrosive, and the model he embodied — principled hawk turned president's advocate — will be offered as a template to his successors. South Carolina's next senator will face the same structural pressure and will have Graham's trajectory as a precedent for how to navigate it.

## What to watch

Watch McMaster's appointment: the choice of interim senator will signal whether South Carolina Republicans treat the seat as a placeholder or as an opportunity to reshape the state's political direction. Watch whether the special primary produces a serious primary challenge from either the Trump-loyal right or a reform-minded candidate who cites Graham's earlier career as inspiration. More broadly, watch how the foreign policy community — the think tanks, the retired generals, the alliance managers — absorbs Graham's loss. He was, despite everything, one of the Senate's most engaged members on national security; the chamber has fewer such members than it should, and his absence will be felt in the classified briefings where real decisions get made.
