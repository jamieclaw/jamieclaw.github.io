Ethiopian government forces have launched drone attacks against Eritrean troops who crossed into the Tigray region from Eritrea's southern border, raising fears of a full regional war between the two neighbouring countries. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/08/ethiopia-drone-attack-against-eritrean-troops-in-tigray) that military vehicles carrying Eritrean soldiers drove across the border and reached the town of Adigrat, 25 miles inside Tigray, according to civilian witnesses, a militia commander, and the conflict monitoring group ACLED. [The BBC separately confirmed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckvgd63rj92lo) that Eritrean troops had "moved deep into Ethiopia's Tigray region" based on resident accounts. The incursion comes as renewed fighting between Ethiopian federal forces and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) has been ongoing since September 23rd, shattering the Pretoria peace agreement signed in November 2022.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream humanitarian framing is largely correct on the facts: this is a devastating region with a recent history of mass atrocity. The Tigray conflict between 2020 and 2022 killed an estimated 300,000 to 500,000 people, involved serious allegations of ethnic cleansing on multiple sides, and produced one of the worst humanitarian crises in recent African history. The Pretoria agreement was fragile but real, and its collapse is a tragedy. Eritrea's President Isaias Afwerki is an authoritarian who has made his country one of the most repressive on earth — he is not a neutral actor. The Guardian noted that the new fighting has already prompted reports of TPLF-aligned groups forcibly recruiting boys as young as fifteen. The urgency of international attention is real.

Progressive advocates would add that the lack of Western media and diplomatic attention to the Horn of Africa — compared with, say, the coverage of the same period's events in Europe — reflects a troubling hierarchy of concern that deserves challenge. On this they are also largely right.

## A different read

What the humanitarian framing tends to obscure is the structural political failure that produced this moment — and specifically, the failure of international conflict resolution mechanisms to address the conditions that made renewed war predictable.

The Pretoria agreement of November 2022 was welcomed with considerable diplomatic fanfare. The African Union mediated it; Western governments praised it; the humanitarian community hoped for stabilisation. What it did not do was resolve the underlying political questions: the status of the disputed Tigray-Eritrea border, the disarmament of Tigrayan forces, the relationship between the TPLF and the federal government in Addis Ababa, and — critically — the role of Eritrea. Isaias Afwerki was not a signatory to the agreement and was not bound by it. He intervened massively in the 2020-22 war on the side of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, and Eritrean forces committed documented atrocities in Tigray. The Pretoria agreement required Eritrean withdrawal without providing a mechanism to enforce it.

This is a pattern that students of African conflict resolution will find depressingly familiar. Agreements that paper over rather than resolve key disputes — particularly those involving external actors who are not parties to the agreement — produce interim peace at best. The question of who has authority over the Tigray-Eritrea border, and under what political arrangement Eritrea would ever voluntarily withdraw its forces and economic interests in the region, was never answered at Pretoria. It was deferred.

There is a broader structural argument here about how the international community approaches African conflicts. The African Union's mediation capacity is real but limited by its founding principle of non-interference in member states' internal affairs — a principle that makes sense as a check on neo-colonial meddling but that systematically disadvantages smaller, weaker states when a larger neighbour with revisionist ambitions is involved. Eritrea under Isaias has exploited this structural gap repeatedly.

The drone attack by Ethiopian forces on Eritrean troops is a significant escalation. [The Guardian notes](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/08/ethiopia-drone-attack-against-eritrean-troops-in-tigray) it "raises the risk that renewed internal conflict in Ethiopia could trigger an all-out regional war." The historical precedents for inter-state wars in this region are not encouraging: the 1998-2000 Ethiopia-Eritrea war killed an estimated 70,000-120,000 people and ended in a stalemate that planted the seeds for the current crisis. A second iteration, in which both sides have expanded military technology including drones, could be significantly worse.

The diplomatic problem is that there is no obvious external actor with both the will and the credibility to intervene constructively. The United States is distracted. The European Union has leverage through aid but limited political will. The African Union is structurally constrained. China has economic interests in both countries but has not historically played a constructive conflict-resolution role. The result is a dangerous vacuum.

## What to watch

- Whether Eritrean forces advance further into Tigray or whether the Ethiopian drone strikes cause a halt — the next 72 hours are likely decisive for whether this becomes a contained border incident or a wider war.
- Signals from the African Union and whether emergency mediation is attempted; the AU's response time in 2020 was lethally slow.
- Any Western diplomatic communications to Asmara — the EU and UK have some leverage through aid relationships with Eritrea, and whether they use it will matter.
- The humanitarian corridor situation; if Eritrean forces cut access routes into Tigray, the population faces a severe food security crisis within weeks, given the pre-existing fragility.
