Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has announced that Canada will pursue [a "unique security and economic alliance" with the European Union](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/15/canada-eu-economic-alliance) as the country actively distances itself from the United States amid a bitter trade war. Speaking at the Canada Investment Summit, Carney described strengthening ties to Europe as a strategic imperative, presenting [more than 160 projects worth $1 trillion to global investors](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmq8jdgel128o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as evidence of Canada's pivot away from its traditional economic anchor. The EU is being courted as both a market replacement and a political counterweight. The move comes directly in response to Trump administration tariffs that have [devastated cross-border industries](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5964930/canada-tariffs-hurting-montana-farmers) — including American farmers in border states who are themselves suffering from the retaliatory measures Canada has imposed. UK officials have also disclosed that Britain is [in talks to join a global defence bank led by Canada](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6e30e98v7j1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), suggesting Carney's realignment project has ambitions beyond a simple bilateral arrangement.

## The received wisdom

The progressive internationalist case for the Canada-EU alignment is straightforward and, on its own terms, quite compelling. When your largest trading partner weaponises trade relationships for domestic political purposes, diversification is not disloyalty — it is prudent risk management. Canada has spent decades over-indexed to the American market; Trump's tariff regime has simply made explicit a vulnerability that always existed. Moreover, the EU represents a rules-based trading order that Canada's own values more closely align with: multilateralism, labour standards, environmental commitments, and the WTO framework that the Trump administration has systematically undermined. Carney — a former Bank of Canada and Bank of England governor — brings technocratic credibility to the pivot that his predecessors lacked.

There is also a genuine security logic. Canada offering air defence support to Ukraine while Zelenskyy visited Calgary signals that Ottawa wants to be seen as a serious player in European security — not merely a junior partner in NORAD but an active participant in the post-Ukraine European security architecture.

## A different read

And yet the Canada-EU pivot, while understandable, deserves a clear-eyed assessment of its limits — and of what it reveals about middle-power strategic thinking more broadly.

The United States and Canada share the longest undefended border in the world, integrated energy infrastructure, deeply intertwined supply chains from automotive to agriculture, and a defence relationship that is constitutionally embedded in NORAD. No investment summit and no number of EU trade agreements changes that geographic and structural reality. The EU's average tariff on goods is not zero, its regulatory barriers are formidable, and the distances involved in transatlantic supply chains impose permanent cost disadvantages compared to north-south continental trade. The $1 trillion of projects Carney is pitching to European investors are largely projects in Canada's energy, mining, and technology sectors — sectors that benefit enormously from proximity to the US market regardless of what the diplomatic temperature is.

There is also a historical precedent worth examining. Britain has run a version of this argument since Brexit: that EU membership could be replaced by a combination of Commonwealth trade, US bilateral deals, and nimble independent regulatory policy. The [Bank of England's own assessment](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmq8jdgel128o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) of Brexit's economic costs has been uniformly negative. The gravity model of trade — which predicts that trade volume correlates with economic size and inversely with distance — is one of the most robustly confirmed findings in international economics. Canada cannot trade its way out of geography with declarations of European solidarity, however politically satisfying those declarations might be.

The more honest reading of the Carney pivot is that it serves two purposes simultaneously. First, it is genuine leverage — demonstrating to Washington that Canada has alternatives, however imperfect, creates negotiating space that Ottawa previously lacked. This is the correct move, and Carney, as a central banker, understands leverage and credibility. Second, it is domestic politics: Canadian public opinion has turned sharply against the United States under Trump, and positioning Canada as a sovereign actor capable of independent strategic choices plays well at home.

The danger is if the leverage play becomes the strategy itself. Alignments built as tactical pressure tools have a way of developing their own institutional momentum, especially when they involve committed investments in new infrastructure and trade relationships. [Montana farmers are already experiencing the collateral damage of the US-Canada trade war](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5964930/canada-tariffs-hurting-montana-farmers) — constituencies that should, in theory, generate domestic political pressure for a US de-escalation. The question is whether that pressure materialises before Canada commits so deeply to the European track that a transatlantic recovery becomes structurally difficult. Middle powers do not generally benefit from permanently estranged relationships with their largest neighbour; they benefit from keeping their options open while extracting maximum concessions from all parties. Carney appears to understand this. Whether his successors will is less certain.

## What to watch

- The specific terms of any Canada-EU comprehensive trade enhancement: the devil will be in the regulatory alignment provisions, which are where the real economic friction lives.
- Congressional reaction to the Canada pivot: border-state senators and representatives who are hearing from constituents harmed by retaliatory tariffs may push for US-Canada de-escalation faster than the White House wants.
- The UK-Canada defence bank talks: if Britain joins, the arrangement begins to look like an Anglosphere-EU hybrid security architecture with real institutional weight.
- Investment flows: whether European capital actually moves into Canada at scale, or whether the $1 trillion figure turns out to be aspirational rather than committed.
