The Guardian reported this week that [the US-China trade war seems to have subsided](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/01/us-china-trade-war-trump-xi), but cautioned that "the sense of calm is misleading." Both Washington and Beijing have tactical incentives to project stability ahead of the US midterm elections on November 4: the White House does not want financial market turbulence in the campaign's final days, and Beijing does not want to be seen as directly intervening in American domestic politics. The result is a studied mutual quietude — a pause in the public rhetoric of economic confrontation, a delay in further tariff escalation, and a series of working-level engagements on agricultural purchases and fentanyl precursor controls that both sides can describe as progress. Behind this calm, however, the structural drivers of the trade conflict — overcapacity in Chinese manufacturing, American concerns about technology transfer and supply chain security, and the fundamental question of whether the two largest economies can sustain integration while competing for geopolitical primacy — remain entirely unresolved.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream economic and foreign-policy establishment tends to view the US-China trade conflict as a manageable disagreement that skilled diplomacy and mutual interest can contain. From this perspective, both countries are too economically intertwined for a full decoupling to be rational: American consumers benefit from Chinese manufacturing efficiency, American technology companies depend on Chinese markets and supply chains, and Chinese growth depends on export access that a trade war jeopardises. The received wisdom holds that extreme positions — blanket tariffs, export controls on entire technology categories, formal decoupling of supply chains — are economically self-defeating, and that a negotiated framework of managed competition, with guardrails on the most dangerous flashpoints (Taiwan, South China Sea, semiconductor exports), is achievable and desirable. The presence of working-level contacts, the resumption of military-to-military communications, and the absence of new tariff escalation in recent weeks are cited as evidence that the system is muddling through adequately.

## A different read

The trouble with the "managed competition" framework is that it assumes both parties share a common interest in the framework's maintenance that is stronger than their interest in winning the underlying competition. There is substantial evidence that this assumption does not hold for Beijing.

China's economic model has not, despite years of Western expectation, fundamentally reformed toward domestic consumption. Its industrial policy — Made in China 2025, the more recent programmes for advanced semiconductors, electric vehicles, and green energy — is explicitly designed to achieve dominance in precisely the sectors where American and European companies are most competitive. The electric-vehicle trade dispute with the EU is a preview of the dynamic: China builds capacity massively subsidised by state finance, floods export markets, and then treats any protective tariff response as an act of aggression. The US-China "calm" has not addressed this; it has merely postponed the next round of the argument.

There is also a domestic political dynamic that the "managed competition" framework consistently underweights. American public opinion on China has shifted dramatically since 2018. The bipartisan consensus for engagement — which held from Nixon's opening through the Obama years — has collapsed. Both parties now compete to appear tougher on Beijing, which means that whatever working-level progress diplomats achieve tends to be undermined by legislative action: export controls, investment screening, restrictions on Chinese nationals in sensitive research roles. The midterm results will be read in Beijing as a signal of American political direction; a strong Republican performance, or a Democratic performance that depends on economic-nationalist messaging, will harden rather than soften the structural pressures.

The Guardian's framing — that the calm is "misleading" — is correct, but perhaps not for the reasons its editorial line usually emphasises. The danger is not primarily that the Trump administration is itching to restart tariff escalation for its own sake; it is that the structural incompatibilities between the two economic systems will produce conflict regardless of what individual diplomats prefer. When Chinese electric vehicles, solar panels, and steel appear in American and European markets at prices that reflect state subsidy rather than genuine comparative advantage, the political demand for protection does not require any particular ideological posture to be rational. Free trade with a state-capitalist economy that does not play by market rules is not free trade; it is a subsidy transfer from Western workers to Chinese industrial policy.

The lesson of the 2018-2022 tariff cycle is not that tariffs solved the problem — they did not — but that the previous consensus that integration would gradually reform China's political economy was wrong. Thirty years of engagement produced the world's second-largest military, a surveillance state of unprecedented technological sophistication, and a Communist Party more confident in its model than at any point since 1989. The "sense of calm" the Guardian describes is the calm of two sides pausing to assess their positions, not the calm of a problem solved.

## What to watch

- Post-midterm US trade policy signals — whether the new Congress moves toward further technology export restrictions or pauses for review.
- Chinese industrial production and export data for Q4 2026 — the scale of overcapacity in EVs and clean energy will determine how quickly the next trade flashpoint materialises.
- Any movement on the bilateral investment framework discussions — their absence from recent working-level communiqués is itself a signal.
- Taiwan Strait incident risk: the trade calm tends to correlate with reduced military signalling, but military posture and trade policy have decoupled before, and the structural pressures have not diminished.
