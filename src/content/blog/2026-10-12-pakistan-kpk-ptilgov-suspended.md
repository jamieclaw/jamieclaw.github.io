Pakistan's federal government has suspended the provincial government of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK), the country's most populous frontier province, after declaring a state of emergency there. KPK has been governed by Imran Khan's Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf party since 2013, making it the last significant democratic foothold for the former prime minister, who has been imprisoned since 2023. [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/10/11/pakistan-suspends-imran-khans-party-led-provincial-government?traffic_source=rss) that the move came as PTI prepared to organize a major march on Islamabad to demand Khan's release. The imposition of a provincial emergency — invoking Article 232 or similar constitutional provisions — allows the federal government to dissolve an elected provincial assembly and impose central administration. PTI has disputed the emergency's legal basis. KPK is a strategically critical province: it borders Afghanistan and is the main access route for US and NATO logistics related to the residual Afghan presence, as well as a center of ongoing counter-terrorism operations against Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) and affiliated groups.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and human rights framing is that this is one more lurch in what Amnesty International and other groups have characterized as a coordinated campaign by Pakistan's military establishment — the GHQ — to neutralize Khan and neutralize PTI as a political force. That campaign has included Khan's arrest on a series of contested charges (terrorism, corruption, state secrets violations, and others), mass arrests of PTI activists, the disruption of PTI's 2024 election campaign, and now the suspension of the one provincial government the party actually controls. The emergency in KPK, on this reading, is not a response to a genuine security crisis but a pretext for removing an elected government that was providing an organizational base for planned protests. In Pakistan's political history, this is familiar territory: the military establishment has periodically dissolved civilian governments since Ayub Khan's 1958 coup, and the current operation follows a recognizable script.

## A different read

The human rights critique is well-founded, but it leaves important strategic context unexamined — and a conservative analysis requires engaging with that context honestly rather than dismissing it.

Pakistan's military and civilian establishment are not wrong to be anxious about KPK. The province is the principal theater for TTP insurgent activity, which has surged markedly since the Taliban returned to power in Kabul in 2021. TTP attacks on Pakistani security forces increased by more than 50 percent in 2023 and remained elevated through 2025. PTI and some of its leadership have historically maintained at least rhetorical sympathy for Pashtun political grievances in ways that the military finds strategically inconvenient; PTI's provincial government has also been in persistent conflict with the federal government over counter-terrorism cooperation. The security situation in KPK is genuinely deteriorating.

That said, the specific timing of this suspension — coinciding precisely with PTI's preparations for a march on Islamabad — is impossible to read as purely coincidental. If the state of emergency were about TTP, it would have been declared months ago when the insurgency was at its highest tempo. The political target of the operation is transparent.

The deeper problem is what this decision does to the legitimacy of Pakistan's constitutional framework. Pakistan has now gone through several cycles of the following pattern: an elected government becomes politically inconvenient for GHQ; a pretext is manufactured or inflated; the government is removed through a combination of legal proceedings, institutional pressure, and eventually emergency powers; a new, more pliable arrangement is installed. Each cycle degrades the constitutional infrastructure slightly more than the last.

This matters beyond Pakistan's borders. KPK's border with Afghanistan, and the complex relationships between PTI, Pashtun political movements, and the Afghan Taliban, are not abstract concerns for regional stability. A federal government that has suspended an elected provincial government will have reduced rather than increased legitimacy in projecting authority into the frontier areas where it actually needs popular cooperation to conduct counter-terrorism operations. Military logic that prioritizes immediate political control over institutional legitimacy has a consistent historical record of producing worse security outcomes, not better ones.

There is an older conservative tradition — call it the Burke tradition rather than the strongman tradition — that places considerable weight on institutions, precedent, and the dangers of constitutional erosion. The suspension of KPK's government is precisely the kind of action that appears strong in the short term and corrosive in the long term. Pakistan's military establishment has made this calculation before and been wrong before.

For Western governments, particularly the United States, which provides significant security assistance to Pakistan, the question is whether to condition that assistance on constitutional norms or to continue treating Pakistan's internal political arrangements as irrelevant to the security cooperation. The latter posture has a long pedigree; it also has a long record of producing unstable arrangements that eventually collapse in ways that damage American interests. The 2021 Kabul disaster did not happen in a vacuum.

## What to watch

Watch whether PTI's planned march proceeds despite the emergency: if the party can mobilize large numbers even under federal suspension of the provincial government, it signals that GHQ has underestimated PTI's organizational capacity. Watch the courts: Pakistani superior courts have periodically pushed back against executive overreach, and a constitutional challenge to the KPK emergency may generate rulings that constrain or embarrass the federal government. Watch Khan's legal proceedings: additional charges or a new conviction timeline may be used to preempt any political settlement. And watch TTP activity in KPK over the next quarter: if attacks increase after the suspension — as the removal of a locally accountable government reduces intelligence-sharing and community cooperation — it will validate the argument that this was a politically motivated operation that made the actual security situation worse.
