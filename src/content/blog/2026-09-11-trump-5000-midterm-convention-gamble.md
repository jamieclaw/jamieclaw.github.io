President Donald Trump headlined the Republican Party's first-ever midterm convention in Dallas on Wednesday, delivering a lengthy keynote address that included a surprise pledge: every adult American citizen would receive a $5,000 payment if Republicans retained both chambers of Congress in November. The offer, which Trump said would need to be spent inside the United States, came with no details about its funding mechanism, implementation, or legal basis. Democrats swiftly labelled it an "empty promise," and legal experts questioned whether such a conditional pre-election payment pledge was lawful. With approximately 270 million American adults, the policy as described would cost roughly $1.3 trillion. Trump also acknowledged at the convention that oil prices, elevated by the ongoing war against Iran, were unlikely to fall before November, and that Americans might have to wait "a little longer" for relief. His approval rating has fallen to a new low of 33 percent among registered voters, according to a recent Financial Times/Focaldata poll, as the [BBC's four-key-takeaways analysis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cg7kjlzlev1o) from Dallas noted.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream political analysis of the Dallas convention leans negative for Republicans. The absence of more moderate senators — Susan Collins of Maine and other competitive-race Republicans chose to stay away — underlines how completely Trump has transformed the party into an extension of his personal brand rather than a broad electoral coalition. The $5,000 pledge reads, to most commentators, as a sign of desperation: a party that cannot make a credible economic case reaching instead for a flashy uncosted giveaway. The argument goes that Trump's continued grip on the party is also its greatest vulnerability — he can fire up the base, but the base alone is insufficient to win in November.

Oil prices trading above $105 a barrel, [as BBC Business reported on Thursday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5y48rz2nddo), combined with surging mortgage rates tied to historically high UK and US bond yields, have made the cost of living a persistent albatross. Polls show it remains the top issue for American voters. A party convention that delivers no credible economic message other than a questionable $5,000 promise has, on this reading, wasted its moment.

## A different read

The conventional critique is largely correct on the policy merits, but it may underestimate the political logic at work — and overestimate the Democrats' ability to capitalise on it.

Start with the $5,000 promise. It is uncosted and legally dubious; no serious economist would endorse it. But it is not random. It is a direct response to the single most damaging domestic vulnerability the Trump administration faces: the perception that the Iran war has made ordinary Americans poorer. The pledge reframes the narrative. Instead of "Trump's war has raised your gas prices," the administration wants voters to hear: "If you stick with us, we will cut you a cheque." The political logic is crude but not stupid. In a low-information environment, a $5,000 promise made at a nationally televised convention is worth more than a detailed policy paper explaining why bond yields are high.

Trump's explicit instruction to convention-goers — "pretend I'm on the ballot" — is a more important signal than the $5,000 headline. It is a candid acknowledgment that Republican turnout is structurally Trump-dependent, and that without a commanding Trump figure at the top of the ticket, the party faces real problems in competitive districts. The absence of Collins and other moderate incumbents from the Dallas arena [was noted by the BBC's Washington correspondent](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cg7kjlzlev1o) as evidence of how far the party has drifted from the swing voters it needs.

Yet here lies the deeper dilemma. The 2026 midterms will not be decided by MAGA diehards in red arenas. They will be decided by voters in suburban districts who supported Trump in 2024 on the expectation of lower prices, fewer foreign entanglements, and American renewal. Those voters are now paying more for petrol, watching a war in Iran that Trump admitted will not end before November, and listening to a president whose approval rating has collapsed to 33 percent. The convention's failure to offer a serious economic off-ramp — rather than a one-time cash transfer that requires both chambers to be won first — may prove more damaging than any single policy miss.

There is a historical rhyme worth noting. Lyndon Johnson won a landslide in 1964 and lost the Congressional majority in 1966. George W. Bush won convincingly in 2004 and was swept from Congressional control in 2006. In both cases, a war had turned toxic faster than the incumbent expected. Trump is essentially asking voters to trust his timeline on the Iran war the same way Johnson asked them to trust him on Vietnam. The convention speech did not offer a convincing answer to why this time will be different.

The deeper structural question — who replaces Trump in 2028? — is one that convention delegates were discussing among themselves even while he held court in Dallas. JD Vance and Marco Rubio were most frequently cited by BBC reporters speaking to attendees. Neither has yet demonstrated they can drive the kind of turnout Trump generates. The party's dependence on one man remains its most significant long-term vulnerability, regardless of what happens in November.

## What to watch

- Polling in six to eight swing House districts over the next three weeks, particularly among suburban voters in Pennsylvania, Arizona, and Georgia, which will give the clearest indication of whether the Dallas convention moved any needles.
- Whether Trump's approval rating stabilises or continues to fall below 33 percent; a sustained sub-30 figure would be historically alarming for a sitting president heading into midterms.
- Any legal challenge to the $5,000 payment pledge — the Federal Election Commission may find grounds to challenge a conditional cash payment tied to an election outcome.
- Energy prices: a material drop in oil below $90 a barrel before October could significantly change the political terrain.
