Europe's automotive industry is in [full-scale crisis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6vgyq598k9po). Across Germany, France, and the UK, manufacturers are facing a convergence of pressures that individually would be serious and collectively are potentially fatal: the green transition's demand for upfront capital investment in electric vehicle platforms, Chinese competition in the EV segment that has compressed margins to near-zero, high energy costs driven by the post-Ukraine energy rupture, and a demand slump in core European markets. Sir Jim Ratcliffe, the industrialist who owns a stake in Manchester United and controls INEOS, this week [suspended production at key UK plants specifically citing high gas prices](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw305ynd69n6o) — a direct consequence of the structural changes to European energy markets since 2022. The BBC's headline framing the story asked the question that European policymakers have been reluctant to pose directly: "Will the threat of war rescue them?"

## The received wisdom

The progressive diagnosis of Europe's automotive crisis focuses on transition management and the failures of industrial policy. The auto sector was given insufficient support to navigate the shift to electric vehicles; Chinese subsidies created an unfair competitive environment that European competition policy failed to counteract quickly enough; energy policy was inadequately protected during the post-Ukraine adjustment period. The solution, in this reading, is more structured state intervention — green industrial policy on the US Inflation Reduction Act model, punitive tariffs on Chinese EVs, and energy price supports that cushion the industrial sector through the transition. Some add that the crisis reflects deeper failures of European economic governance: the inability to coordinate fiscal policy across member states, and the persistence of an austerity consensus that denied the investment the transition required.

## A different read

The industrial policy prescription is partially right about the symptoms and substantially wrong about the cure — and the BBC's headline question about war deserves a direct answer rather than the squeamishness it typically receives.

Begin with the energy problem, because it is foundational. European industry's structural competitiveness depends on energy costs that are now, post-2022, structurally elevated relative to the United States and China. This is not a policy failure in the narrow sense; it is the consequence of a deliberate strategic choice to decouple from Russian gas at pace. The choice was correct — the alternative was strategic dependence on a belligerent revanchist power — but its industrial costs cannot be wished away by green industrial policy. The Ratcliffe plant suspensions are not an anomaly; they are a preview of what will happen across European heavy industry unless energy costs structurally decline, which requires either new gas infrastructure, accelerated nuclear buildout, or renewable capacity at a scale and speed that no serious analyst believes is achievable in the near term.

The Chinese EV competition problem is real but also somewhat overstated as the primary factor. European auto manufacturers — Volkswagen above all — misallocated capital during the 2010s, making strategic bets on diesel technology at precisely the moment they should have been building EV platforms. That is a corporate governance failure, not a Chinese assault. The Chinese advantage in battery supply chains and manufacturing cost is substantial, but it was created partly by European manufacturers' own choices. Punitive tariffs address the symptom; they do not reconstruct the industrial capacity that was allowed to atrophy.

Now the rearmament question. The defence industry is one of the few sectors where Europe is actively increasing spending, driven by the obvious security environment. Defence manufacturing uses many of the same industrial competences as automotive manufacturing — precision engineering, supply chain management, high-value assembly. Germany's Rheinmetall has already begun converting some of its automotive component suppliers toward defence applications. If European governments follow through on defence spending commitments toward 3% of GDP — and the pressure to do so is intensifying — the demand signal for European industrial capacity will shift in ways that could partially offset the automotive decline. This is not a comfortable argument for progressives who have spent a generation treating rearmament as the antithesis of social progress. But industrial capacity requires demand, and demand is currently coming from defence in a way it has not since the Cold War.

The broader point is about the coherence — or lack of it — in European industrial strategy. A continent that has simultaneously committed to zero-carbon manufacturing, refused to invest in new gas infrastructure, punished its cheapest energy supplier through sanctions, and spent two decades underinvesting in defence, has created its current industrial crisis through an accumulation of choices that made sense politically one at a time but are catastrophic in aggregate. The auto industry's crisis is the most visible expression of that contradiction, not its cause.

## What to watch

- Volkswagen's restructuring plan: whether it involves plant closures in Germany, which would represent a major political test for the Scholz-era coalition's industrial commitments.
- EU EV tariff implementation: the recently announced tariffs on Chinese EVs will be tested by WTO challenges and by European consumer resistance to higher prices.
- UK energy price trajectory: if wholesale gas prices ease through winter 2026-27, the Ratcliffe production suspensions may reverse; if they remain elevated, expect further closures.
- Defence industrial conversion: watch for whether any major European auto supplier publicly pivots toward defence contracts as automotive margins collapse — that would be the structural shift this story is pointing toward.
