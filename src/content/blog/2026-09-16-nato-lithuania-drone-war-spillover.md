A NATO fighter jet [shot down a drone over Lithuania](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/15/nato-jet-shoots-down-suspected-russian-drone-over-lithuania) on Tuesday after the unmanned aircraft entered the country's airspace from neighbouring Belarus, according to Lithuanian and alliance officials. The incident follows a similar pattern of airspace violations that have rattled Eastern Europe throughout the Ukraine conflict. The drone's origin has not been definitively confirmed, but Lithuanian authorities say it almost certainly entered from Belarusian territory, making Minsk — and by extension Moscow — the presumptive source. Separately, [Denmark accused a Russian warship of firing flares at a military helicopter](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwn8mjl31p5yo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in the same week, with Copenhagen's prime minister calling the behaviour "reckless." The EU's chief meanwhile warned publicly of a wider conflict risk as border incursions multiply.

## The received wisdom

The consensus response from Brussels and most Western capitals is predictably measured: the shootdown demonstrates NATO's capability and willingness to defend its territory, the alliance is united, and the incident — while serious — should not be escalated. European Commission officials have called for restraint while affirming solidarity with the Baltic states. The mainstream read is that Russia is probing and posturing but has no real intention of triggering Article 5; these are calculated acts of harassment designed to unnerve without crossing the threshold of open war. The argument runs that the West should respond firmly but proportionately — and above all, avoid the kind of overreaction that hands Moscow a propaganda gift or a pretext.

There is genuine wisdom in this view. NATO has maintained cohesion under real pressure. The institutional machinery of Allied Command Operations has functioned. Lithuania was not left to fend alone. These are not small achievements given the centrifugal strains the alliance has faced.

## A different read

The problem is that "measured" and "adequate" are not synonyms, and what looks like stability from a Brussels press conference looks rather different from Vilnius or Riga.

Consider what has actually accumulated. NATO has now [shot down a drone inside a member state](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/15/nato-jet-shoots-down-suspected-russian-drone-over-lithuania). Danish naval forces are being buzzed by Russian warships firing flares. Baltic airspace has been violated repeatedly. The war that Western governments assured their publics would be "contained to Ukraine" is not contained — it is spreading in exactly the way that limited wars historically do when one side senses the other's deterrence is uncertain.

The Cold War comparison is instructive but cuts against the optimists. During the tense years of the early 1980s, NATO maintained something close to hair-trigger discipline — not because everyone trusted Moscow, but because everyone understood the rules of the game. Today's environment is different in one crucial respect: Russia has been fighting a sustained land war in Europe for four years, its military is simultaneously depleted and battle-hardened, and its leadership has invested enormous domestic political capital in not losing. That combination produces erratic behaviour, not rational cost-benefit calculation.

The deeper issue is what strategic theorists call "salami slicing" — the gradual annexation of normality. Each individual incursion, taken alone, does not warrant a dramatic response. A flare fired at a helicopter. A drone drifting into Lithuanian skies. A disruption to Dutch rail infrastructure from "[suspected sabotage](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8ly49w9g1edo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss)" attributed to unknown actors. Considered individually, each is a provocation that falls below the threshold of war. Considered collectively, they describe a systematic campaign to demonstrate that NATO's eastern flank is penetrable and that Western governments will absorb harassment indefinitely rather than risk escalation.

History's lesson on such campaigns is uncomfortable. Britain and France absorbed precisely this kind of "sub-threshold" pressure from Nazi Germany throughout the 1930s — the demilitarisation of the Rhineland, the annexation of Austria, the Sudetenland. Each episode was framed at the time as the last demand. Chamberlain's error was not merely naivety; it was a structural failure to understand that accommodation of incremental aggression encourages more of it. The Kremlin's current leadership reads history too, and it reads Munich as a success story.

None of this means NATO should scramble fighters toward Moscow. But it does mean the alliance needs to shift from a reactive posture — intercepting drones after they enter NATO airspace — to something more credibly pre-emptive: clearer warnings, more visible force deployments on the eastern flank, and above all a frank conversation with Western publics about what deterrence actually costs. The [Iran war has already created US munitions shortfalls](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9gk58xgng0vo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the Pentagon's own inspector general has confirmed. An alliance that is running short on munitions in one theatre while absorbing provocations in another is not projecting strength; it is projecting vulnerability.

The Baltic states understand this. Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia have spent decades warning exactly what Western Europe dismissed as Baltic paranoia. They were right about Russian intentions before. The grown-ups in Warsaw, Tallinn, and Helsinki are not hysterical; they are reading the same history books that Moscow's generals read, and they are drawing the correct conclusions.

## What to watch

- Whether NATO formally attributes the drone to Russia or Belarus — ambiguity here is itself a signal of political reluctance to confront Moscow directly.
- The pace of reinforcement of NATO's eastern flank brigades; troop numbers and readiness levels will show whether the alliance's rhetoric matches its posture.
- Any movement on European defence spending commitments in the wake of the Dutch rail sabotage and the Danish maritime incident — three near-simultaneous provocations suggest coordination, not coincidence.
- The US midterm political context: a Congress preoccupied with domestic battles may be less willing to fund NATO commitments at the moment the alliance most needs credible backing.
