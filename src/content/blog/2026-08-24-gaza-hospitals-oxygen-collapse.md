Gaza's hospitals are running out of oxygen, according to [Al Jazeera reporting](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/8/23/gazas-hospitals-are-running-out-of-oxygen-as-healthcare-nears-collapse), as the enclave's healthcare infrastructure approaches total collapse. Medical staff at hospitals including Al-Shifa and the European Gaza Hospital have described operating without sufficient oxygen concentrators and cylinders to sustain intensive care patients, ventilated patients, and those in post-surgical recovery. The breakdown is not attributable to a single dramatic strike but to the cumulative attrition of fuel shortages, supply restrictions, generator failures, and the destruction of the industrial oxygen plant that previously supplied the territory. Doctors interviewed describe having to choose which patients receive the remaining supply — a form of triage that, in any other context, would be immediately recognisable as a humanitarian emergency warranting urgent international response.

The breakdown in Gaza's oxygen supply occurs against the backdrop of an ongoing Israeli military campaign focused on preventing Hamas from reasserting control, and of a regional ceasefire diplomacy that has repeatedly stalled.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream humanitarian framing is unambiguous: the loss of medical oxygen in a densely populated territory of more than two million people is a public health catastrophe. Human rights organisations, UN agencies, and medical charities have consistently called for the restoration of fuel, medical supplies, and humanitarian access. The argument is that even in conditions of lawful armed conflict, deliberate or foreseeable deprivation of life-sustaining medical supplies to a civilian population violates international humanitarian law, including the Fourth Geneva Convention's protections for civilian hospitals and medical personnel. The WHO and Médecins Sans Frontières have both documented the deterioration of Gaza's health system in forensic detail.

This framing commands broad support outside Israel and its closest Western allies, and even within those quarters there is growing discomfort. The collapse of medical infrastructure is not a contested fact; it is documented.

## A different read

The right-of-centre instinct here is not to minimise the suffering — it is to ask what it means for the moral and strategic coherence of Israel's campaign, and what obligations Western governments that supply and diplomatically support Israel have as a consequence.

The Israeli government's position is that Hamas has systematically used civilian infrastructure, including hospitals, as military assets — a claim that has been substantiated in some instances, including by the IDF's discovery of tunnel infrastructure beneath Al-Shifa. The argument is that the breakdown of civilian services is partly a consequence of Hamas embedding its operations in populated areas, and that responsibility therefore lies substantially with the group that chose to govern in this way. This is not an implausible legal argument; it is a genuine complexity in the law of armed conflict.

But the law of armed conflict does not work as a simple blame-assignment exercise. The principle of proportionality requires that anticipated civilian harm not be excessive relative to the concrete military advantage anticipated. And the principle of distinction requires not just that attackers avoid targeting civilians and civilian objects, but that they take all feasible precautions. The question is not whether Hamas bears responsibility — it does — but whether Israel has met its own obligations.

The oxygen crisis illustrates a particular difficulty with siege-style warfare. When a blockade is comprehensive enough to prevent the entry of industrial fuel and medical supplies, the military authority exercising the blockade becomes, under international law, a party with responsibility for the civilian consequences. This was litigated — albeit inconclusively — in relation to Allied bombing campaigns in World War II and in the treatment of besieged Leningrad. The [Al Jazeera reporting](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/8/23/gazas-hospitals-are-running-out-of-oxygen-as-healthcare-nears-collapse) describes a situation in which the supply chain for medical oxygen has effectively collapsed, which raises the question of whether Israel has taken all feasible precautions to ensure that life-sustaining supplies reach hospitals.

Western governments — above all the United States and the United Kingdom — have a particular responsibility here because their diplomatic and military support gives them real leverage. The record suggests that leverage has been applied inconsistently, and that domestic political pressures in both Washington and London have tended to suppress frank official acknowledgement of what is happening. This is not a sustainable position. The oxygen crisis will generate documentation — from hospitals, from UN agencies, from journalists — that will outlast the current administration in Washington and the current government in London. At some point, the question of what Western governments knew and what they chose to say or do will be answered.

A right-leaning analysis that takes international law seriously and is genuinely sympathetic to Israel's security situation can still conclude that the systematic collapse of civilian medical infrastructure demands a clearer Western response than it has so far received. Solidarity with Israel's right to defend itself is not the same as silence about the means.

## What to watch

Whether the Rafah crossing reopens sufficiently to allow commercial-scale fuel and medical supply deliveries to resume is the most immediate operational question.

Track the WHO's formal statements on Gaza's hospital capacity — the language has been escalating, and further escalation would signal that the UN health agency is preparing to issue a formal finding.

Watch whether the United States conditions any near-term military supply discussions on verifiable improvements in civilian access. The language from the State Department on this point has been notably softer than from the UN.

Any ICJ or UN Security Council action on the medical supply question will be blocked by US veto, but the record of votes will matter for the longer historical and legal accounting.
