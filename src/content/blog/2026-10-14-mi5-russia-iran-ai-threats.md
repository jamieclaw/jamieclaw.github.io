MI5 Director-General Ken McCallum delivered a rare public warning on Tuesday, identifying [three simultaneous and escalating threat streams facing the United Kingdom](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c60rl5vwpk02o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss): Russian state-directed sabotage operations against British and European infrastructure; Iranian assassination and intimidation plots targeting dissidents, journalists, and British officials on UK soil; and the emerging use of artificial intelligence tools to enhance the operational capabilities of hostile intelligence services. The speech, addressed partly to a domestic audience and partly as a message to allied services, was notable for its candour and the breadth of the threat picture it described. McCallum also addressed [Russian interference in the Brexit referendum](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cky83q49e4w0o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), cautioning against overstating Moscow's influence while acknowledging that Russia had indeed attempted to exploit British political divisions. The combined picture is of a domestic security service that is stretched, concerned, and increasingly willing to make its concerns public.

## The received wisdom

The liberal security establishment's preferred framing of the MI5 speech will emphasise proportionality and civil liberties. Yes, the threats are real — but they must be addressed without creating a surveillance state, without discriminating against Muslim communities in counterterrorism operations, and without providing political cover for the restriction of legitimate protest or dissent. The Guardian-reading commentariat will note that the Russian interference framing risks being weaponised by those who wish to delegitimise the Brexit result and that McCallum's nuanced position on that question — real interference, but modest in impact — should be the ceiling of the discussion rather than the floor. Progressives will add that the Iranian threat to dissidents on British soil, while real, is partly a consequence of the UK's historic arms-trading relationship with the Islamic Republic and its predecessors, and that the solution is diplomatic engagement rather than escalating counterintelligence postures. AI, in this framing, is an amplifier of existing threats rather than a qualitative change.

## A different read

The MI5 director's speech is more important than the usual threat-assessment address for a reason that the civil liberties framing systematically underweights: the simultaneous nature of the three threat streams represents a qualitative shift, not merely an accumulation of familiar problems.

The Russian threat McCallum described is not primarily the headline-grabbing cyber-attack on critical infrastructure but something subtler and more corrosive: a systematic effort by Russian intelligence services to recruit criminal networks within Western countries to carry out physical sabotage, harassment, and intimidation operations at arm's length from the Russian state. This hybrid model — outsourcing to criminals who can be disavowed — is specifically designed to operate below the threshold of formal attribution and diplomatic response. [BBC Politics coverage](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cky83q49e4w0o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) confirms that McCallum's comments on Brexit interference were carefully calibrated precisely to avoid the attribution-threshold problem becoming a domestic political football.

The Iranian threat is arguably more immediately alarming for the individuals directly affected. Tehran has conducted what amount to [state-sponsored murder-for-hire operations on British soil](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c60rl5vwpk02o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — targeting Iranian dissidents, journalists covering the Islamic Republic, and individuals associated with the National Resistance Council of Iran. The British government has repeatedly responded with diplomatic protests, sanctions, and the occasional expulsion of Iranian diplomats. What it has not done — and what the received wisdom's emphasis on diplomatic engagement actively discourages — is impose the kind of hard costs on Tehran that might actually alter the calculus of the Revolutionary Guard's intelligence directorate. The pattern here is the same one that has characterised Western responses to Iranian provocations since the 1979 revolution: enough response to register displeasure, not enough to deter.

The AI dimension is where the speech breaks genuinely new ground. McCallum's framing — that hostile intelligence services are using AI to accelerate and enhance traditional tradecraft — aligns with the picture painted by the [Saudi-OpenAI espionage case reported by BBC Business](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwg5jq4vlddzo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). The implication is that the labour-intensive, error-prone process of running human intelligence networks — identifying vulnerabilities in targets, crafting personalised deception operations, processing intercepted data — is becoming faster and cheaper as AI tools improve. This is an asymmetric threat multiplier: it is disproportionately useful to states with smaller intelligence budgets seeking to punch above their weight against larger adversaries.

The conservative reading of all three threat streams converges on a single diagnostic point: Western open societies are being systematically exploited through the very freedoms they are reluctant to curtail. Russian intelligence recruits in Western criminal networks because those networks operate in jurisdictions with strong due-process protections. Iran targets dissidents on British soil because the UK's asylum and free-movement frameworks make it relatively easy to identify, track, and reach them. AI espionage exploits the openness of academic and commercial AI research communities.

None of this means the answer is to become a police state. It means the answer requires a significantly more sophisticated and honest public discussion about where open-society freedoms create exploitable vulnerabilities — a discussion that is politically difficult because it requires acknowledging that some freedoms have real security costs that cannot be wished away by process or diplomacy alone.

## What to watch

- **Legislation on hostile-state activity**: The UK's National Security Act 2023 introduced new offences around foreign-state interference. Watch for whether the MI5 speech accelerates enforcement actions or new legislative proposals, particularly around state-criminal hybrid operations.
- **Iranian diplomatic relations**: The UK downgraded but maintained diplomatic relations with Iran following previous assassination plots. Whether this speech presages a further downgrade or formal severance will signal how seriously the government rates the deterrence failure.
- **AI counterintelligence investment**: GCHQ and MI5 will require significant new investment in AI-enabled counterintelligence capabilities to match the offensive AI tools McCallum described. Budget signals in the forthcoming Strategic Defence Review will indicate whether that investment is materialising.
- **Brexit interference debate**: McCallum's careful calibration will not satisfy either side of the Brexit culture war. The key question is whether the parliamentary inquiry into Russian interference — long called for by critics of the 2020 Russia Report — is finally granted the access it needs.
