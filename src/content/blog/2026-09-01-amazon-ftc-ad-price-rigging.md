The US Federal Trade Commission and a bipartisan coalition of 22 state attorneys general filed a lawsuit on Monday alleging that Amazon secretly manipulated the online advertising auctions it uses to set ad prices, [overcharging more than a million advertising customers by approximately $20 billion since 2019](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvgy91nvy27o). The complaint, filed in Washington state, alleges that Amazon overrode auction results and replaced them with higher prices to increase its own profits — charging Sponsored Products advertisers their actual winning bid close to 80 percent of the time, rather than one cent above the next-highest bid as the auction rules implied. The FTC also alleges that the extra costs were ultimately passed to consumers. Amazon strongly denied the allegations, calling the suit "misguided" and arguing that "average winning bids fell 50% from 2019 to 2025." The company's stock fell 2.5 percent following the announcement. [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/31/nx-s1-5950482/ftc-amazon-advertising-lawsuit) this is the latest in a series of FTC confrontations with the company; last year Amazon settled a Prime subscription manipulation case for $2.5 billion.

## The received wisdom

The left-progressive reading of this lawsuit is essentially vindication. Amazon, like the other digital giants, has spent a decade leveraging its platform dominance to extract rents from advertisers, sellers, and consumers alike. The FTC's action — and the bipartisan coalition of state AGs — represents the kind of structural regulation that should have been applied years ago. The argument draws on the neo-Brandeisian tradition revived by legal scholars like Lina Khan, who built her career on precisely this critique of Amazon before becoming FTC Chair: that platform monopolies must be understood not just through price effects but through market structure, competitive harm, and the ability of dominant players to set the rules of the game they also play. The $20 billion figure, if sustained by the courts, would represent one of the largest antitrust penalties in American history.

## A different read

The conservative tradition has long been ambivalent about antitrust — and that ambivalence has costs. The Chicago School consensus, which dominated antitrust thinking from the 1970s through the 2010s, held that consumer welfare — typically measured by price effects — was the sole legitimate standard for competition enforcement. By that standard, Amazon was perennially difficult to prosecute: its retail prices were low, its Prime subscription was widely loved, and the "free" nature of its services made harm hard to quantify. The result was a two-decade holiday from serious platform regulation that allowed the company to accumulate the kind of structural dominance that the Sherman Act was designed to prevent.

Monday's lawsuit operates in different territory. The allegation is not merely that Amazon is large, but that it is engaged in active deception of its own advertising customers — misrepresenting the mechanics of its auction system to extract higher fees. That is not a novel or philosophically contested antitrust theory. It is fraud in a marketplace that Amazon controls. The bipartisan composition of the 22-state coalition — which almost certainly includes Republican attorneys general — signals that this is not a progressive political project. It is a law enforcement action.

The right's most cogent critique of the FTC's recent antitrust activism has been methodological: that agencies have overreached, brought cases they cannot win, and created regulatory uncertainty that chills investment. That critique has merit in several recent cases. But it applies less well here. The complaint's theory of harm — secret price manipulation above stated auction rules — is concrete, documentable, and does not require the court to accept contested economic theories about market definition or competitive effects. Either Amazon charged advertisers more than the auction mechanics implied, or it did not. Discovery will establish which.

There is a broader point that conservatives invested in market integrity ought to make more loudly. The Chicago School's consumer welfare standard was always, in part, a proxy argument: if prices are low, the market is working. But Amazon's advertising business is not the retail business. Advertisers — the direct customers of the ad auction — are businesses, not households. The harm is to small merchants who pay more for Sponsored Products placements than they should, and whose cost overruns are then passed to end consumers. The consumer welfare standard, applied to a three-sided market (Amazon, advertisers, shoppers), does not illuminate the harm nearly as well as it would in a simple retail context. That is not a left-wing insight. It is a straightforward application of market economics to a complex marketplace.

Amazon settled its Prime case for $2.5 billion. If the ad-pricing allegations are proven, the $20 billion figure would represent a qualitative escalation. Courts and investors will be watching whether the FTC under the current administration has the institutional capacity to sustain a case of this complexity.

## What to watch

- **Discovery and document production**: Whether internal communications show Amazon executives explicitly discussing the gap between stated and actual auction mechanics will be decisive for the government's case.
- **State AG coalition durability**: Twenty-two states filed together. Whether Republican-led states remain in the coalition through trial, or settle separately, will signal the lawsuit's long-term political durability.
- **Amazon's counter-narrative on bid dynamics**: The company claims average winning bids fell 50 percent over six years. If true and verifiable, that is a powerful defence. If the FTC can show the falls reflected market conditions rather than Amazon restraint, the company's argument collapses.
- **Congressional response**: Senate Commerce Committee has been eyeing platform advertising market power for two years. This lawsuit may provide the evidentiary foundation for legislation the FTC alone cannot deliver.
