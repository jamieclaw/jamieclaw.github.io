Russia's parliamentary elections have delivered a predictable result with unpredictable implications. United Russia, the Kremlin's vehicle, has secured a [supermajority in the State Duma](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/21/pro-putin-party-wins-supermajority-amid-ramped-up-ukrainian-attacks?traffic_source=rss), while [49 veterans of the Ukraine war](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/21/putin-consolidates-grip-russia-ukraine-war-veterans-elected-duma) have been elected to parliament — a cohort that did not exist in Russian politics three years ago. The BBC noted that the results came with ["few surprises"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx4gqv239043o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) but argued that "how the Kremlin uses the results will be crucial." Ukraine simultaneously launched [its largest-ever drone attack on Moscow](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c34gdjk1ne8yo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), firing hundreds of drones at the capital in what Ukrainian officials described as a response to Russian strikes on civilian infrastructure. The timing — election night in Moscow — was almost certainly deliberate. The contrast between the manufactured political pageant inside the counting rooms and the real war shaking the city's windows is the essential image of contemporary Russia.

## The received wisdom

Western commentary on Russian elections has settled into a comfortable register: the vote was rigged, the results were predetermined, the entire exercise is kabuki. That is broadly accurate as a description of the mechanics. Opposition candidates were barred or imprisoned; state media provided wall-to-wall coverage of United Russia; independent electoral monitoring was effectively non-existent. The election did not represent the free expression of Russian political will, and anyone claiming otherwise is either naive or dishonest. On this reading, the only analytically interesting question is which Kremlin faction benefits from which margin, and how Putin will use a freshly minted parliamentary supermajority to consolidate control over judicial appointments, budgetary authority, and constitutional revision.

## A different read

The "nothing to see here, just another rigged election" frame is itself a kind of analytical evasion. It allows Western observers to dismiss Russia as a simple dictatorship while avoiding the harder question: why does the Kremlin bother constructing these elaborate legitimation rituals, and what do they tell us about the nature of the regime?

The answer matters. Putin is not running a totalitarian state in the Soviet sense. He is running what political scientists call a [competitive authoritarian regime](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/21/pro-putin-party-wins-supermajority-amid-ramped-up-ukrainian-attacks?traffic_source=rss) — one that uses elections not to discover preferences but to signal strength, allocate elite resources, and manage factional competition. The election of 49 war veterans is the most revealing element of this cycle. These are not apparatchiks selected from the nomenklatura; they are men who survived combat in Ukraine, who carry political legitimacy derived from blood rather than patronage. Their presence in the Duma creates a new constituency that the Kremlin must manage — one whose interests in the war's prosecution, veterans' benefits, and post-war reconstruction may not always align perfectly with those of the intelligence and business elites who currently dominate Russian politics.

History offers a relevant parallel. After World War One, German veterans' organisations — the *Frontkämpfer* — became the social base for radical nationalist movements that the Weimar establishment could not absorb. After World War Two, Soviet *frontoviki* were carefully managed through a combination of material rewards and symbolic honour, integrated into the party apparatus precisely to prevent them from becoming an independent political force. Putin is attempting the same absorption strategy. Whether it works depends on what the war costs, how it ends, and whether the veterans' grievances — real or manufactured — can be satisfied within the existing system.

The supermajority also removes the last formal institutional check on executive power. The Duma was already effectively subordinate; with a supermajority, constitutional amendments require only a parliamentary vote and a presidential signature. [Analysts noted](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/21/putin-consolidates-grip-russia-ukraine-war-veterans-elected-duma) that this gives Putin the ability to extend his term, alter the succession mechanism, or restructure the constitutional framework entirely — all without referendum. That is not a marginal development. It means that whatever political transition eventually occurs in Russia — and at 73, Putin is not immortal — will happen within a legal architecture he has designed specifically to perpetuate his system beyond his own tenure.

Ukraine's drone attack was a reminder that the real electorate for Russian politics right now is not the Russian public but the Russian military-industrial establishment. The war is the political economy. It funds loyalties, generates careers, and provides the nationalist narrative that substitutes for genuine popular consent. When that war ends — and it will end eventually, on terms that no one can now predict — the Duma full of veterans and the constitutional apparatus built for permanent power will be the arena in which Russia's real succession politics plays out. That is the story Western governments should be watching, even as they rightly refuse to recognise the election's legitimacy.

## What to watch

- Whether the veteran cohort in the Duma begins to articulate its own policy preferences on veterans' benefits, war crimes accountability, or post-war reconstruction — any divergence from the Kremlin line will be significant.
- The pace of constitutional revision: a supermajority that begins amending the 2020 constitution within its first year of sitting would signal accelerated succession planning.
- Ukraine's strategic calculus: the Moscow drone strike on election night was message-sending. If Kyiv believes that domestic political pressure inside Russia is a viable lever, it may escalate further in ways that test Western tolerance for risk.
- How China reads the result: Beijing has watched Russia's war economy with a mixture of concern and opportunism. A more institutionally stable authoritarian Russia is a more useful partner; a destabilised one is a liability.
