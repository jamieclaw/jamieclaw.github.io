Russia has struck a second major bridge in Kyiv, [Ukrainian and British officials confirmed on Saturday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c83vqxzdg1yko), following a ballistic missile attack that brought down a span over the Dnipro River in the capital. The strike — coming just days after a first bridge was destroyed — marks a qualitative shift in Russian targeting doctrine: Moscow is no longer merely disrupting logistics at the front; it is now systematically dismantling the infrastructure of a capital city in a European country. Ukrainian emergency services reported multiple civilian casualties, and the damage severed a key arterial route used for both civilian movement and military supply.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading is that this is precisely the kind of war crime that demands a firm Western response, possibly including accelerated delivery of longer-range weapons to allow Ukraine to strike comparable Russian infrastructure. Liberal commentators point to the strikes as proof that any negotiated settlement that leaves Russian forces inside Ukraine simply rewards aggression and invites further escalation. The International Criminal Court, which already holds an arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin, is expected to add fresh counts. European chancelleries are calling emergency consultations, and NATO's secretary-general has issued the sharpest condemnation since the war began. The consensus view is that the West must hold firm and increase pressure.

## A different read

That consensus is comforting but incomplete. The harder question is not whether Russia is committing war crimes — it obviously is — but whether the West's available responses actually deter further escalation or merely accelerate it up the ladder.

Consider the record. Since February 2022, each Western "red line" — tanks, fighter jets, long-range missiles, permission to strike inside Russia — has been crossed after varying delays. Each crossing was followed not by Russian de-escalation but by Russian adaptation and continued offensive operations. [Putin's simultaneous warning that Russia is "ready to use every weapon" to protect Kaliningrad](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqg7k7v499jjo) is not new rhetoric — it has been the standing refrain since 2022 — but the bridge strikes give it operational credibility it lacked when confined to press conferences.

There is a respectable historical parallel in the Allied strategic bombing campaign of the Second World War. The early RAF doctrine assumed that attacking civilian-adjacent infrastructure in Germany would break morale and force political capitulation. It did neither in Hamburg, in Dresden, or in the industrial Ruhr. What it did do was harden German civilian resolve, at least until the physical destruction became total. The analogy is imperfect — Ukraine is the victim, not the perpetrator — but the structural logic applies: destroying the other side's infrastructure does not by itself end wars. Wars end when the political leadership of one side decides the cost of continuing exceeds the cost of stopping.

Putin has shown no such calculation. His domestic political position has, if anything, solidified since 2022. Russian state media is depicting the bridge strikes as legitimate military operations against "terrorist" supply infrastructure — a framing that, however dishonest, resonates internally. Garry Kasparov, the Russian dissident chess grandmaster, [disclosed this week that the US government warned him of an imminent threat to his life](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6y8z8kr5mk8o), which suggests the Kremlin's external operations arm remains active even as its army grinds forward.

The practical dilemma for Western policymakers is this: supplying Ukraine with systems capable of striking Moscow — which some commentators now demand — is a categorically different decision from supplying HIMARS or even Storm Shadow. It risks triggering the very nuclear escalation the West has been carefully managing around for four years. The arms-supply consensus, always more fragile than it appeared, is beginning to show cracks in Budapest, Rome, and parts of Washington. Viktor Orbán's government has already used bridge imagery as part of its campaign to reopen ceasefire talks.

None of this means the West should abandon Ukraine. It plainly should not. But there is a difference between arming a country to defend itself and assuming that escalation dominance is available without limit. The West does not have escalation dominance over a nuclear-armed state that has demonstrated a consistent willingness to absorb economic punishment. What it has is the capacity to make Russian adventurism expensive — and that capacity has limits that honest strategists should name.

The right response to the bridge strikes is continued military and economic support, tighter enforcement of the oil-price cap, and diplomatic investment in the secondary states — India, Turkey, Brazil — whose neutrality allows Russia to keep its war economy running. None of that is glamorous. But glamour and strategy are different things.

## What to watch

- Whether NATO members agree in emergency consultations to expedite specific long-range systems, and whether that agreement holds against domestic political pressure in Germany and Italy.
- The trajectory of the oil-price cap enforcement, especially following [G7 coordination on reserves this week](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck87zg8jnwngo) — tighter enforcement would hit Russian war financing harder than any additional weapons shipment.
- Ukraine's own ability to sustain bridge reconstruction under continued Russian air pressure, which will determine whether the military and civilian logistics of the country hold together through winter.
- Any signal from Beijing on whether it is willing to use its diplomatic leverage with Moscow — a signal that has not yet materialised in any meaningful form.
