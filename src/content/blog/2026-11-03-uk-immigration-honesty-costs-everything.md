The British government's proposed changes to immigration rules have this week produced a textbook demonstration of what happens when a political party attempts to govern against its own instincts. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood put forward a package of reforms that included, in its original formulation, a proposal to make some refugees wait up to 30 years before being eligible for settlement — a suggestion so extreme that it drew [condemnation from charities and prompted a cabinet backlash](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/nov/02/shabana-mahmood-softens-immigration-plans-cabinet-backlash-leave-to-remain) within 48 hours of being reported. Mahmood has since softened the plans. Meanwhile, [Labour MPs are pulling their punches on immigration changes](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck0e0jpww9wro) more broadly, and the BBC's political team notes that the government is [making up its China policy as it goes along](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr86zn9lj8p1o) — a phrase that could, with minimal adjustment, describe its immigration policy too. The Guardian reports Prime Minister Burnham asking countries [affected by Britain's historical role in slavery to help shape a formal apology](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/02/andy-burnham-ask-countries-affected-uk-role-slavery-shape-formal-apology), a symbolic gesture that will please some and infuriate others, and that arrives in the same week as the immigration row. The juxtaposition is not accidental; it reflects the governing tension at the heart of this Labour administration.

## The received wisdom

The progressive reading of the immigration row is that Mahmood's original proposals were a capitulation to the politics of fear — an attempt to outflank Reform UK on its own terrain at the cost of Labour's values. The party, the argument runs, won its historic majority on a platform that did not include punitive treatment of refugees; the 30-year wait proposal was a betrayal of that mandate and was rightly abandoned after internal pushback. [Migration, on this reading, is fundamentally a moral issue](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/nov/02/migration-andy-burnham-shabana-mahmood-labour-immigration) — one that demands principled policy rather than anxious triangulation.

There is genuine substance to this critique. The 30-year settlement proposal was not just politically combustible; it was practically unworkable and legally dubious, likely to be challenged successfully under existing human rights frameworks. The cabinet was right to push back, and the softened version of the policy is almost certainly more legally durable.

## A different read

And yet the progressive critique, for all its validity on the specific proposal, avoids the harder question that any serious immigration policy must eventually confront: what number of arrivals does the country have the institutional capacity to absorb, and is the government willing to say so out loud?

The British public, according to every serious polling operation of the past decade, has consistent and relatively stable views on this question. It does not think immigration is inherently bad; it does think the pace and scale of recent arrivals — which peaked at net figures above 900,000 annually under the previous Conservative government — is too high for public services, housing markets, and community cohesion to manage without significant stress. These views are not confined to Reform UK voters. They are held by a significant proportion of Labour's own electoral base, including the working-class communities in the North and Midlands whose return to the party produced Burnham's majority.

The Mahmood episode illustrates a recurring pathology in centre-left politics on this issue: the gap between private acknowledgement and public communication. Ministers know the numbers are unsustainable; they know their voters want action; they reach for dramatic-sounding proposals (30-year waits, caps, points systems) without having done the institutional groundwork to make them workable; the proposals collapse under legal and political scrutiny; and the whole cycle repeats. What it never produces is a clear, honest statement of what the government actually believes about the appropriate level of immigration and why.

The right-of-centre tradition offers a more coherent framework here, even if its practitioners have been no more honest in government than their Labour counterparts. The argument is simply this: nation-states have both the right and the responsibility to manage their borders in a way that reflects the absorptive capacity of their institutions, the preferences of their existing citizens, and their specific economic needs. This is not a racist argument; it is a description of what sovereignty actually means in practice. Margaret Thatcher said it bluntly — "people are rather afraid that this country might be swamped" — and was vilified for her phrasing while being accurate about the political anxiety. Niall Ferguson has observed that mass migration has historically been politically destabilising when it outpaces the capacity of receiving societies to integrate new arrivals into shared civic norms. The evidence from the past decade suggests that observation holds.

The [BBC's tracking of UK migration numbers](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70989jrdweo) confirms that net migration, while lower than its peak, remains historically very high. The government's own fiscal forecasters have noted that the public finances depend partly on continued immigration to sustain the working-age population. This creates a genuine dilemma, not a simple political choice, and the honest response to a genuine dilemma is to explain the trade-offs rather than oscillate between punitive proposals and tactical retreats.

The slavery apology initiative, announced in the same week, is being handled with slightly more political finesse — [asking affected countries to help shape the process](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/02/andy-burnham-ask-countries-affected-uk-role-slavery-shape-formal-apology) rather than making a unilateral declaration. Whether that produces a durable outcome or simply defers the political cost is an open question.

## What to watch

- **The softened proposals**: Whether Mahmood's revised immigration package can survive legal challenge, and whether it actually reduces net migration numbers or simply changes the composition of who arrives.
- **Labour backbench cohesion**: The BBC notes that Labour MPs are [pulling their punches](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ck0e0jpww9wro) — how long that discipline holds as living standards face a predicted fall will determine whether immigration becomes an internal party crisis.
- **Reform UK's response**: Whether Nigel Farage's party can capitalise on the government's visible discomfort with its own policies, or whether the softening of proposals defuses their political attack.
- **The slavery apology process**: Whether the consultation with affected nations produces a meaningful statement or becomes a years-long bureaucratic process that satisfies nobody.
