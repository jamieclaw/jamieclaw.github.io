Australia is [preparing to formally warn China](https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/jul/22/australia-to-warn-china-it-wont-be-bulled-by-provocative-actions-as-it-builds-military-and-nuclear-arsenal) that it will not be bullied by what it characterises as "provocative" actions from Beijing, even as Canberra simultaneously accelerates its own military and nuclear-capable arsenal. The warning, reported by the Guardian, represents a significant hardening of Australia's public posture toward China — a shift from the careful diplomatic ambiguity of the Morrison and Albanese governments toward a more explicit deterrence-based framework. The nuclear dimension is particularly notable: Australia has historically been one of the most vocal advocates of nuclear non-proliferation, and its own programme — developed in part through the AUKUS framework — represents a departure from the country's post-Cold War strategic culture. Canberra is developing nuclear-powered submarines rather than nuclear weapons, but the symbolic and strategic significance of the posture shift is considerable.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and centre-left reading of Australia's military buildup is primarily anxious. Critics within Australia argue that Canberra is locking itself into a confrontational framework with its largest trading partner, potentially sacrificing economic interests for a security alignment whose costs — financial, diplomatic, environmental — have not been honestly presented to the Australian public. The AUKUS submarine deal has already faced sustained criticism over its timeline, its cost overruns, and the degree to which it represents genuine strategic autonomy versus deeper dependence on American and British industrial capacity. The concern is that Australia is acquiring the posture of a significant military power while remaining fundamentally dependent on the United States for the intelligence, logistics, and political backing that posture requires.

There is also a non-proliferation argument. If Australia — a liberal democracy, an NPT signatory, a historically responsible international actor — normalises nuclear-powered submarines and builds capabilities that brush against nuclear thresholds, it becomes harder to hold the line against other states making similar arguments. The precedent matters.

## A different read

These concerns deserve a fair hearing, but the alternative — a continued policy of deliberate ambiguity and quiet accommodation toward a China that has shown no reciprocal interest in moderating its military activities in the South China Sea, Taiwan Strait, and near Australian maritime territory — has its own costs, which the critics do not always acknowledge.

The trajectory of Chinese military expansion in the Indo-Pacific over the past decade has been consistent and substantial. Beijing has built and militarised artificial islands, harassed Philippine and Indonesian fishing vessels, repeatedly conducted live-fire exercises near Taiwan, and significantly expanded its submarine fleet. Australia's warning this week is not a response to a hypothetical threat but to a documented pattern of behaviour that has continued regardless of whether Canberra has been conciliatory or cautious.

The historical precedent here is instructive. Great Britain faced a comparable dilemma in the 1930s when it had to decide whether appeasement of a revisionist power — ostensibly in the interest of preserving trade and avoiding confrontation — was sustainable. The analogy is not perfect, and China in 2026 is not Nazi Germany; it is a great power with legitimate interests and an economy deeply integrated with the liberal trading system. But the fundamental logic of the deterrence argument — that revisionist powers are more likely to moderate when facing credible costs than when facing strategic ambiguity — has significant historical backing. Australia's decision to [build its military and nuclear arsenal](https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/jul/22/australia-to-warn-china-it-wont-be-bulled-by-provocative-actions-as-it-builds-military-and-nuclear-arsenal) is a bet that deterrence is more stabilising than accommodation.

The economic argument against confrontation also weakens on inspection. China is Australia's largest trading partner, and the bilateral relationship has recovered somewhat after Beijing's punitive tariffs of 2020–2022. But Australia's experience of that coercive episode — where Chinese economic pressure was applied in direct response to Canberra's call for a COVID-19 inquiry — illustrates precisely why pure economic interdependence cannot serve as the foundation for security policy. An Australia that modulates its defence posture to avoid upsetting a major trading partner has, in effect, allowed that trading partner to exercise a veto over its strategic choices. That is not sovereignty.

The more difficult question is whether Australia's buildup, while strategically defensible, is adequately resourced and coherently planned. The AUKUS submarine timeline — which has repeatedly slipped — and the broader question of whether Australia has the defence-industrial base to sustain the capability it is publicly committing to are legitimate concerns. Announcing a confrontational posture without the underlying military capacity to back it risks the worst of both worlds: antagonising Beijing without deterring it.

## What to watch

- The Chinese response: Beijing's formal reaction to Australia's warning will indicate whether it views Canberra's posture shift as a manageable irritant or as a more serious threshold crossing requiring direct counter-measures.
- AUKUS submarine delivery timeline: any further slippage in the schedule for Australian acquisition of nuclear-powered vessels will test the credibility of the deterrence posture Canberra is publicly advertising.
- The Philippines and Indonesia: whether other middle powers in the region respond to Australia's hardened posture by aligning more closely with Canberra's framework or maintaining their own strategic ambiguity.
- Trade data: watch whether China resumes any of its previous coercive trade measures — tariffs on Australian barley, wine, coal — in response to the warning, which would test the resilience of Canberra's public commitment.
