[North Korea fired ten ballistic missiles](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/20/north-korea-fires-10-missiles-day-after-trump-curtails-south-korea-joint-drills) into the sea on August 20, one day after the Trump administration announced it was curtailing the scale of annual US–South Korea joint military exercises. The launches were accompanied by a pointed statement from Kim Jong-un's sister, Kim Yo-jong, [casting doubt on Trump's claims](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/19/kim-jong-un-sister-doubt-trump-claims-secret-us-north-korea-talks) of ongoing secret diplomatic talks between Washington and Pyongyang. Meanwhile, the BBC reported that [South Korean president Lee Jae Myung's government](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c4gj1vje8dxo) is now facing questions about whether Washington's gestures toward Kim amount to a strategic betrayal of Seoul — a US ally that has kept 28,500 American troops on its soil for over seventy years.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream progressive and liberal internationalist view holds that Trump's attempts to re-engage North Korea are inherently reckless — a return to the failed "bromance" diplomacy of his first term that produced no verifiable denuclearisation and legitimised a hereditary dictator on the world stage. The joint drill reductions, in this framing, are an unforced concession that weakens deterrence without extracting anything in return. Kim simply pockets the gesture and fires missiles anyway, demonstrating that flattery and reduced military signalling cannot neutralise a regime whose nuclear arsenal is its existential guarantee. The BBC's analysis piece notes that South Korea itself now fears it is being traded away in a bilateral Washington–Pyongyang conversation it has no seat at. On this reading, reducing exercises and hinting at diplomatic talks is naive at best, dangerous at worst.

## A different read

The case against this dismissal begins with a simple empirical observation: the current policy — decades of maximum pressure, isolation, and large-scale deterrence exercises — has not prevented North Korea from building an estimated [forty to sixty nuclear warheads](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/20/north-korea-fires-10-missiles-day-after-trump-curtails-south-korea-joint-drills) and a growing ballistic missile inventory. The received consensus has presided over one of the most spectacular failures of non-proliferation in the nuclear age. Critiquing Trump for departing from it requires showing that the established approach was working, which the evidence does not support.

That said, Trump's specific move — reducing joint exercises as an apparent goodwill gesture without securing any reciprocal concession — is a different and legitimate concern. The ten-missile salvo within twenty-four hours of the announcement is a blunt signal. Kim Yo-jong's public ridicule of Trump's claims of secret talks is even more pointed: it is rare for Pyongyang to publicly contradict a US president in real-time on a factual matter, suggesting either that Trump's claims were exaggerated or that North Korea is deliberately embarrassing Washington to demonstrate its leverage.

The historical parallel worth reaching for here is not the first Trump term's Singapore summit, but rather the pre-Korean War period of 1949–1950, when American policymakers sent ambiguous signals about the US defence perimeter in Asia — Dean Acheson's famous speech of January 1950 appeared to exclude Korea — and miscalculation followed catastrophically. The problem is not that Trump is talking to Pyongyang; engagement has theoretical merit. The problem is that the combination of reduced exercises, personalised diplomacy claims that Pyongyang flatly denies, and conspicuously reduced consultation with Seoul creates exactly the kind of ambiguity that invites miscalculation.

South Korea's reaction is the more structurally important development. President Lee, coming from the left, has called for autonomous South Korean defence capability — including the reinstatement of [independent military planning](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/20/north-korea-fires-10-missiles-day-after-trump-curtails-south-korea-joint-drills) stripped from Seoul under the mutual command framework. Seoul is quietly watching Tokyo, which under successive LDP governments has been quietly inching toward a more independent defence posture. If both Pacific allies simultaneously begin to question whether the US security umbrella is reliable, the architecture of post-1945 East Asian security undergoes a structural shift that no future US president can easily reverse.

Niall Ferguson has written about what he calls the "Chimerica" unravelling — the slow fraying of economic and security interdependence between the US and its East Asian partners. The Korean peninsula adds another thread to that unravelling. And unlike trade deficits or tariff disputes, nuclear miscalculation is not a negotiable variable.

The Trump administration's defenders argue that reducing exercises is a relatively cheap gesture that keeps the diplomatic channel open and avoids the kind of provocations that historically precede North Korean tests. This is not an unreasonable position in the abstract. But it requires that the goodwill generated actually produce some verifiable movement — which Kim Yo-jong's statement suggests it emphatically has not. If the concession is real and the reciprocal benefit is fictitious, that is not realpolitik. That is simply being outmanoeuvred.

## What to watch

- Whether South Korea formally requests a larger role in US–North Korea dialogue, or begins unilateral back-channel communication with Pyongyang — either would mark a significant change in the peninsula's triangular diplomacy.
- North Korea's next provocation timeline: ten missiles in one day is substantial, but Pyongyang typically escalates with ICBM tests ahead of US election cycles and major diplomatic moments. Watch for a seventh nuclear test, which analysts have long said is technically ready.
- The reaction in Tokyo: Japan sits within range of everything North Korea fired yesterday, and a more independent-minded South Korea emboldens voices in the LDP who argue Japan needs its own deterrence capability.
- Whether Trump's claims of "secret talks" receive any substantiation — or whether Kim Yo-jong's public denial stands uncontested, which would mark an unusual loss of credibility for a sitting US president.
