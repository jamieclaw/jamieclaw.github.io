American forces struck two rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, the [first known US attack on Iran since late July](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z72x5z1po), killing two people and injuring two more according to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. US Central Command described it as a "limited, precise action" after detecting Iranian preparations to launch rockets and deploy sea mines into the strait. Iran responded by firing ballistic missiles at US bases in Jordan — King Hussein and al-Azraq — and claimed to have targeted the UAE's Al-Minhad Air Base with drones, a claim Abu Dhabi denied. The UAE confirmed it intercepted one drone. Trump later posted apparent AI-generated videos on Truth Social captioned "Kharg Island being blown to smithereens!!!" — a reference to Iran's main oil export terminal. [Attacks had effectively paused for over a month](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/31/g-s1-141175/attacks-flare-us-iran) before Monday's exchanges.

## The received wisdom

The progressive case against this war's resumption is serious and deserves a full hearing. Critics point out that the United States has now been in open conflict with Iran for over six months with no coherent articulation of what "victory" would look like. The original February strikes — wide-ranging attacks launched by the US and Israel — were followed by Iranian retaliation, the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz to normal traffic, and a cascade of economic consequences that have rattled global energy markets. The pause since late July, the argument runs, was evidence that de-escalation was achievable. By striking again, Washington has squandered a ceasefire-in-practice and handed Iran's government a propaganda gift at a moment when Iranian President Pezeshkian was publicly suggesting Tehran preferred "a path of agreement." Returning to hostilities, this view holds, is not strength — it is the inability to accept an impasse.

## A different read

That critique has merit as far as it goes, but it rests on a misreading of what the pause actually represented. The Strait of Hormuz did not return to normal shipping during the ceasefire-in-practice. Iran continued to demand that vessels pay [a reported $2 million to cross](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z72x5z1po) and required tankers to pass by Larak Island for "checks" — an extortion regime dressed in maritime procedure. Roughly 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas used to transit the strait before the conflict began. The pause was not a ceasefire; it was a frozen coercion. Washington's stated rationale for Monday's strike — that Iran was preparing to deploy sea mines — is precisely the kind of threat that could not be allowed to metastasise unchallenged.

The broader strategic question is whether there is any off-ramp that both governments can plausibly sell to their own constituencies. History is not reassuring. The US-Iran relationship has been locked in a pattern of deterrence, provocation, and periodic kinetic exchange since 1979. What distinguishes this moment is the apparent absence of the diplomatic back-channels that historically moderated the relationship. The 2015 JCPOA, whatever its flaws, provided a framework. The post-2018 maximum pressure campaign destroyed it without replacing it, and the February 2026 escalation consumed whatever goodwill remained.

There is a relevant historical parallel: the so-called "tanker war" of the 1980s, in which both Iran and Iraq attacked oil shipping in the Persian Gulf, drawing in US naval escorts and eventually leading to the accidental downing of Iran Air Flight 655 by USS Vincennes. That conflict had no formal beginning and no formal end — it simply exhausted itself amid the attrition of the broader Iran-Iraq War. The danger now is analogous: a grinding, undeclared quasi-war that neither side can escalate to a decisive conclusion nor find the political room to end.

Trump's posting of AI-generated destruction footage is a new and genuinely alarming variable. [J.D. Vance confirmed the video was meant to "send a message"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/31/putin-and-xi-affirm-strategic-alliance-amid-unpredictable-world) — which is to say, the President of the United States is now using algorithmically generated disinformation as a diplomatic instrument. The ambiguity may be deliberate, but ambiguity in a kinetic conflict is itself a form of escalation. The IRGC does not have the luxury of assuming the video is purely performative. Neither do the Gulf monarchies, who are caught between Iranian pressure and American protection.

The right-leaning instinct here is not to call for escalation. It is to insist on strategic clarity: what is the objective, what are the conditions for ceasing operations, and who in the administration owns the policy? The Larak strike may have been tactically justified. The absence of a coherent endgame — after six months of conflict — is not.

## What to watch

- **Iran's response to Jordan strikes**: Whether Iran limits follow-on retaliation to proxy or symbolic attacks, or escalates with precision strikes on American installations, will determine whether Monday marks a contained exchange or the beginning of a new escalatory phase.
- **Kharg Island**: Trump's AI video threatens Iran's main oil terminal. A real strike there would collapse global oil markets. Watch for fleet repositioning in the Persian Gulf.
- **SCO communiqué on Iran**: Putin met with Iranian President Pezeshkian in Bishkek on Monday. Any joint statement from Bishkek signalling Russian-Chinese backing for Tehran will reframe the strategic calculus.
- **Congressional War Powers invocation**: Democrats on the Armed Services Committee have been threatening to force a War Powers Act debate. Monday's exchanges may supply the political moment.
