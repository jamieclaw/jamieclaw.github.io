Germany confirmed on Tuesday what many had suspected for weeks: Russia was behind the drone attack on Leipzig/Halle airport that narrowly missed triggering a catastrophic explosion on a major NATO logistics hub last month. The European Commission and NATO responded within hours, with Commission President Ursula von der Leyen calling Leipzig [a "new escalation on European Union soil directly attributed to Russia"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce9e810pg7ko) and vowing a coordinated response. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte stood in "full solidarity with Germany." Berlin moved to close the Russian consulate in Bonn and shutter the Russian House cultural centre. And within a 24-hour window of the attribution, two electricity substations in Germany were attacked, a drone factory in Poland was torched, and a second factory in Lublin caught fire. Whether those incidents are connected or coincidence, the tempo is unmistakable.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream response to Leipzig has been to treat it as proof that Europe must now invest more heavily in security, intelligence-sharing, and critical infrastructure protection. This view holds that Russia is testing the West's resolve through "hybrid" operations — drones, arson, cyberattacks — precisely because they fall below the threshold of conventional military engagement. The solution, on this reading, is more coordination: joint EU sanctions, visa bans on Russian citizens, expanded enforcement against the sanctions-busting "shadow fleet." Von der Leyen's language was calibrated to frame this as a law-and-order problem with a bureaucratic solution. The EU is "working on how to tighten enforcement of existing sanctions," Kallas said. More committees, more taskforces, more communiqués. The underlying logic is that patient institutional pressure will eventually raise the cost to Moscow high enough to deter further provocations.

## A different read

There is a problem with that logic, and it is not new. Russia has been running hybrid operations against European infrastructure for years — cable sabotage in the Baltic, assassination attempts in Western capitals, interference with GPS signals over the Baltic states, fires at defence-adjacent factories — and the pattern has been consistent: Europe attributes, condemns, expels a diplomat or two, and the operations continue. Leipzig is the most brazen incident to date: a military-grade drone on the tarmac of a NATO hub used by Ukrainian transport aircraft, [with components and detonators matching known Russian hybrid operation signatures](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce9e810pg7ko), two identified suspects including a Belarusian with a Russian passport. The attack failed, but its audacity was the message.

The measured response Europe is calibrating — coordinated sanctions enforcement, visa restrictions on Russian tourists, summoning ambassadors — is essentially the same toolkit deployed after Salisbury in 2018, after the Nord Stream sabotage in 2022, after the series of Baltic cable cuts in 2024. At what point does repetition of the same response begin to communicate not resolve but accommodation?

There is a historical analogy worth dwelling on. In the 1930s, democratic governments learned to their cost that graduated responses to incremental provocations do not deter authoritarian actors who have correctly calculated that the responding power prefers de-escalation to confrontation. The analogy is inexact — Russia is not Nazi Germany, and nuclear deterrence genuinely constrains escalatory options — but the core dynamic recurs: an actor who believes the other side will not pay the price of genuine deterrence will continue testing the threshold.

Russia's Deputy Foreign Minister Alexander Grushko said Moscow was giving diplomacy "a chance," while pointedly answering "maybe" when asked whether Russia might sever relations with Berlin. That is not the language of a government that fears consequences. Lithuania's Foreign Minister Kestutis Budrys said the quiet part aloud: [Russia sees no political cost in its hybrid operations against European allies](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ce9e810pg7ko). He is right.

The harder question Europe has been reluctant to answer is whether hybrid war requires a hybrid deterrent — visible, credible, proportionate responses that Russia's security establishment actually registers as costly. That might mean covert disruption of Russian logistics infrastructure, aggressive use of the €193bn in frozen Russian assets, or meaningful interdiction of the shadow fleet rather than announcements about interdicting it. None of these are easy, and some carry risks of their own. But the alternative — another round of expulsions and communiqués — is a policy of managed decline masquerading as resolve.

Poland's Prime Minister Donald Tusk called the factory fires a "deliberate act of arson and sabotage." Poland has been the clearest voice in Europe about the severity of Russian hybrid operations, and Warsaw has earned the right to be heard. The question is whether Berlin — historically the most reluctant major NATO member to confront Moscow — will translate its Leipzig anger into sustained pressure rather than a short-term diplomatic package that Russia waits out.

## What to watch

- Whether the EU's coordinated response goes beyond visa restrictions to active interdiction of the shadow fleet and seizure of frozen Russian assets — the gap between announced measures and implemented ones has been conspicuous.
- Germany's Bundestag vote on defence spending: whether the Leipzig attribution accelerates the push past the 3% NATO target, or whether coalition politics dilute it.
- Russian Foreign Ministry "mirror-image" retaliatory measures against German diplomatic presence, and whether Moscow escalates from expulsions to more targeted action against German personnel or infrastructure.
- Further attacks on infrastructure in Poland, Baltic states, and Germany in the coming weeks — the tempo of the past 48 hours suggests a coordinated campaign, not isolated incidents.
