Reports circulating this week indicate that Elon Musk is [actively plotting a mega-merger of SpaceX and Tesla](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr93z1e87e0do), a combination that would create one of the largest and most strategically consequential private companies in history. SpaceX holds long-term NASA contracts, operates the Starlink satellite internet network — which provides communications infrastructure to Ukraine's military and has been at the centre of international diplomatic incidents — and is the primary launch vehicle for a significant proportion of US government payloads. Tesla remains the world's largest pure electric vehicle manufacturer. A merged entity would span satellite communications, energy storage, autonomous vehicles, rocket propulsion, and artificial intelligence — and would be controlled, under current structures, essentially by a single individual. This week also brought news that [Trump confirmed Musk had restored Starlink access to Ukraine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq74pjpmx0e8o) after a reported interruption — a decision with direct military consequences that was made without any publicly visible government authorisation process.

## The received wisdom

The liberal critique of Musk focuses on his politics — his support for Donald Trump, his ownership of X (formerly Twitter) and the alleged platforming of far-right content, his fraught role in the DOGE advisory body, and his public interventions in European political debates. In this framing, the SpaceX-Tesla merger is primarily concerning because it would further empower a politically destabilising billionaire whose ideological commitments are antithetical to progressive values. The structural governance concerns are real but are often instrumentalised: the objection is Musk specifically, not the broader category of unconstrained technocratic power.

There is also a market-regulatory critique: the merger would raise obvious competition concerns, combining a dominant position in commercial launch with the largest EV fleet, and could be expected to trigger antitrust scrutiny from the FTC and its European equivalents. This is the technocratic remedy — a regulatory problem with a regulatory solution.

## A different read

The governance concern about a SpaceX-Tesla merger is more fundamental than either the partisan progressive or the technocratic regulatory framing captures, and it does not depend on having a view about Musk's politics.

The Starlink Ukraine episode crystallises the issue. A decision with direct consequences for the conduct of a European land war — whether a military ally's front-line communications remained active — was made by a private individual on no publicly disclosed criteria, reversed on no publicly disclosed criteria, and then confirmed by a US president on social media. There was no congressional notification, no allied consultation, no published memorandum of understanding governing the conditions under which Starlink access could be modified. This is not normal. It is also not primarily Musk's fault: successive US administrations have allowed critical national security capabilities to migrate into private hands without establishing adequate governance frameworks for those capabilities.

The right-of-centre tradition has historically been concerned with concentrations of power — not only state power, but any power that operates without accountability to law, process, or democratic consent. Edmund Burke's scepticism of abstract schemes of power applied to revolutionary governments, but its logic extends to any institution that accumulates transformative authority outside constitutional channels. A merged SpaceX-Tesla, controlling satellite internet infrastructure used by military allies, autonomous vehicle fleets generating vast surveillance-capable data, and rocket launch capability that no other private entity can match, is exactly the kind of concentration that should give conservatives pause — independent of who owns it.

[The financial press has noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr93z1e87e0do) that the merger logic is partly about using Tesla's public-company equity to capitalise SpaceX without a conventional IPO, giving Musk liquidity while maintaining control. This is clever financial engineering; it is also a mechanism for avoiding the shareholder accountability that public markets, for all their imperfections, impose. Tesla's minority shareholders would be acquiring exposure to SpaceX's government-contract dependencies — including the political risks that those contracts carry — without any corresponding input into SpaceX's governance.

The antitrust remedy is necessary but insufficient. What is actually required is a governance framework for critical infrastructure — whether satellite communications, launch capability, or AI training compute — that does not depend on the good intentions of the individuals who happen to own it. The UK's approach to critical national infrastructure designation, and the US's Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) model, offer partial templates. Extending similar oversight to domestically-owned critical tech infrastructure is overdue, and the SpaceX-Tesla merger is a natural political moment to advance it.

## What to watch

- Whether the FTC or Department of Justice opens a formal antitrust review of the proposed merger, and on what timeline
- Congressional hearings on the Starlink-Ukraine decision-making process, which several members of the Senate Armed Services Committee have reportedly requested
- Whether any NATO member formally requests a bilateral agreement governing Starlink terms of service during active conflict operations
- Tesla's next quarterly earnings call and whether institutional investors raise governance concerns about the merger structure
