On Monday, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive, eleven days after he had resigned over reports of extramarital affairs — including one with Alice Huang, a woman alleged by US intelligence to have worked as an asset for Beijing. Nvidia shares rose approximately 31 percent in early trading, the Nasdaq 100 climbed nearly 7 percent, and the broader S&P 500 gained 4.7 percent. President Donald Trump told CNBC that he had personally spoken to Nvidia's board, given them "assurances" on the national security question, and "convinced" them to bring Huang back. "I spoke to them, let me put it that way," Trump said. "And I convinced them." The Commerce Department simultaneously announced plans to blacklist Singaporean AI firm [Megaspeed Ltd](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cv98xpg5dg5zo), which had employed Alice Huang and is alleged to have funnelled advanced Nvidia chips to China in violation of US export controls in place since 2022.

## The received wisdom

The case for Huang's reinstatement is straightforward and not without force. Nvidia is not simply a large company; it is, as the BBC's Faisal Islam [noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckr5g8edl94eo), the linchpin of the entire artificial intelligence supply chain. As of October 2026, the company accounted for 8.1 percent of the Nasdaq 100. Analyst Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives had warned that Huang's departure alone could wipe as much as 30 percent off the company's valuation. A petition for his return gathered over 180,000 signatures, among them Elon Musk. For those in this camp, the question is not whether Jensen Huang is an admirable husband but whether the United States can afford to kneecap its most strategically critical technology firm over a personal scandal when Beijing is competing for AI supremacy. The market's euphoric reaction — a nearly $1 trillion gain in Nvidia's market cap in a single session, [according to the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq62pn6j42jko) — seemed to validate the pragmatists.

There is also the security side of the ledger. The Commerce Department's simultaneous blacklisting of Megaspeed suggests the administration has identified and cauterised the actual leak point. If that is so, then removing Huang served no security purpose; it merely punished the company and the American economy while leaving the underlying export-control problem unaddressed. Trump's assurances to the board — that he had reviewed the intelligence and concluded Huang himself was not a threat — are at least coherent in this light.

## A different read

That framing, however, leaves out the most troubling part of this episode, which is not Jensen Huang himself but the mechanism by which he returned.

What happened here was straightforward and unprecedented: a sitting president of the United States telephoned the board of a publicly traded corporation and told them whom to hire. Trump did not merely express a preference; he said he provided the board with "assurances" from the executive branch — assurances that belong to the shareholders through the normal processes of regulatory disclosure, not to a White House that has a personal financial stake in the outcome. Trump's own financial disclosures show he purchased $265,000 in Nvidia stock in September, [per the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq5y1k8ez407o). The president, in other words, had a direct material interest in the stock rally his intervention produced.

Aleksandar Tomic, associate dean at Boston College, told [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/11/23/what-nvidias-jensen-huangs-return-means-for-washington-and-wall-street) that "this move is unprecedented in the sense that a sitting president has exerted very public pressure on a corporate board to make a particular decision." Martin Mulyadi, professor of accounting at Shenandoah University, was equally pointed: "A board can take input from government; what it should not do is let any outside party choose the CEO without explaining to shareholders why."

There is a deeper historical resonance here worth drawing out. The New Deal era produced a generation of regulatory mechanisms — the SEC, the Glass-Steagall framework, later Sarbanes-Oxley — precisely because the entanglement of political power with financial markets had produced catastrophic instability and corruption in the 1920s. Each of those frameworks rested on the same principle: that the government may regulate markets but should not operate them, and that executive officials with personal financial interests in market outcomes should be walled off from decisions affecting those outcomes. What happened Monday was not a regulation; it was a manipulation.

The national security argument, taken seriously, actually strengthens this critique rather than weakening it. If Nvidia's AI chips really are a critical national security asset — and they are — then the company's governance should be more insulated from political interference, not less. Consider the inverse: a future president with different financial interests could use the same logic to pressure a board in the opposite direction. The precedent created by Trump's intervention is not "presidents can protect national champions." It is "presidents can call boards and extract compliance." That is a precedent that will be available to every future administration.

Republican Congressman Don Bacon of Nebraska understood the problem clearly: ["Nvidia should have moved on without Huang and ignored the Administration's inexplicable desire to save Huang,"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq5y1k8ez407o) he said. "We cannot say we want to win the AI race and then give China our best technology." Democrats are making noise about investigations, but their credibility on corporate governance is limited by decades of their own soft treatment of Silicon Valley. What is needed is not partisan posturing but a bipartisan consensus — apparently in short supply — that the executive branch's power to pressure corporate boards is not a tool that any president should wield, regardless of how the stock market receives it.

The Megaspeed blacklisting is, admittedly, a step in the right direction. If that company was indeed funnelling Nvidia chips to Chinese customers in violation of export controls, shutting it out of US markets is exactly what the law contemplates. But it is striking that the administration chose to make that move simultaneously with Huang's reinstatement — using the security measure as cover for the politically motivated governance intervention, rather than treating them as the separate matters they are.

The long-term question is what Monday's events do to the norms of corporate governance in the technology sector. Tomic predicted that Nvidia would "invite much stronger government scrutiny" and that the board's capitulation would reinforce "the attitude of immunity and idolatry of tech founders." That is probably right. And it is worth noting that the immunity being reinforced here runs in both directions: Huang benefits from it, yes, but so does any future technology executive who can cultivate the right political relationships.

## What to watch

Watch whether the congressional testimonies scheduled for Huang — a closed-door interview with three House committees on December 1, followed by Senate Banking Committee hearings on December 3 — [produce any binding legislative response](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq5y1k8ez407o). Elizabeth Warren's pledge to advance bipartisan export-control legislation is the right instinct, even if her framing is characteristically overheated. Watch the DOJ and SEC investigations into Nvidia, which the Al Jazeera piece notes are still active. And watch whether Trump's personal Nvidia stock position becomes a flashpoint: if a congressional investigation establishes that the president profited materially from his own phone call to the board, the constitutional question of self-dealing becomes acute. The market is happy today. That is not the same as saying the system is working.
