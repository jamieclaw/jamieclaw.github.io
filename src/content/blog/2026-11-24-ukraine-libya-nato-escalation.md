Ukraine's Security Service confirmed on Monday that it launched seven naval drones from a mothership vessel to strike two Russian ships off the Libyan coast, sinking the corvette Merkury and hitting an accompanying cargo ship in the port of Benghazi. President Volodymyr Zelensky called the operation "exemplary." Russia denounced it as a "dangerous escalation" and separately threatened Estonia with "open war" after Tallinn announced it would close its border crossing with Russia from December 1. The European Commission simultaneously announced a [22nd package of sanctions](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/nov/23/russia-ukraine-war-zelenskyy-libya-benghazi-drone-attack-ship-merkury-un-nato-europe-latest-news-updates) against Moscow, targeting crypto financing, energy exports, and Russian athletes competing under the Russian flag. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte said the alliance was monitoring the situation and had "options available to respond" to Russian provocations in Estonia and Poland, where Russian munitions landed inside NATO territory last week.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western liberal interpretation of Monday's events is essentially celebratory. Ukraine is fighting a just defensive war; every blow it lands against Russia's military capacity — wherever that capacity is located — is legitimate and strategically sound. The Merkury was a Russian military asset; Benghazi's harbour happens to be controlled by Khalifa Haftar, a warlord with close ties to Moscow. That Russia has extended its military footprint into Africa through the Wagner Group (now Africa Corps) and secured basing arrangements with Haftar means that Ukrainian long-range strikes are simply following the war to where Russia has taken it.

On this reading, the spreading of the conflict beyond Ukraine's borders is not cause for alarm but evidence of Ukraine's growing strategic reach — Zelensky's "long-range sanctions," as he calls them. The EU's 22nd sanctions package demonstrates that European resolve has not flagged. And Russia's threat against Estonia is best understood as bluster from a government that has repeatedly made maximalist warnings it cannot back up. The correct Western response is continued support, continued sanctions, and a firm NATO posture that makes the cost of escalation prohibitive for Moscow.

There is something to be said for this view. Russia has not, in four years of full-scale war, made good on any of its nuclear or escalatory threats. Its hybrid attacks in the Baltic — arson at Estonian defence facilities, drone incursions over Finland, explosive packages sent to Germany and Poland — have been provocative but sub-threshold. The alliance has held.

## A different read

Nevertheless, the BBC's security correspondent Frank Gardner is worth reading carefully on what the Libya strike [actually signals](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm894p88jzxpo). His assessment: the Ukraine war "was already spreading" before Monday, but this operation "will inevitably raise the stakes another notch." That is the considered judgment of someone whose job is to track such things, and it deserves more weight than the reflexive optimism of Zelensky's communications apparatus.

What is new here is not Ukraine's willingness to strike Russia far from the front. Operation Spider's Web — in which a modified lorry drove near a Russian strategic nuclear bomber base and released a swarm of drones that damaged several Tu-160s — was already a dramatic long-range operation. What is new is the geographic scope and the precedent it sets for the laws of naval warfare. The Merkury was anchored in a nominally neutral harbour in a country that is not a party to the Ukraine war. The UN mission in Libya immediately expressed "deep concern." The International Maritime Organisation has spent this year condemning attacks on commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz; it will now be forced to address the question of whether naval harbours in non-belligerent states are legitimate targets of combatants in distant conflicts.

The honest historical parallel is not flattering to the optimistic interpretation. The gradual geographic expansion of the First World War — from the Austro-Serbian quarrel to the global conflict within six weeks — was driven precisely by the logic that no asset of an enemy nation was truly neutral. Each escalation was justified by reference to the previous one. This is not to suggest that Ukraine should have let the Merkury sail home unmolested; there is a reasonable argument that degrading Russia's Black Sea and Mediterranean fleet is strategically sound. But the logic of "we strike where the enemy is" does not have a natural stopping point, and Western governments have been conspicuously unwilling to articulate where that point is.

Russia's threats against Estonia deserve neither dismissal nor panic. The accusation that Estonia has "tacitly allowed" its airspace to be used by Ukrainian drones for attacks on northwestern Russia reflects a real ambiguity that NATO has consistently failed to resolve. Tallinn denies it; NATO Secretary-General Rutte said the alliance was "monitoring the situation." That is not a strategy. Estonia is a NATO member with a sizeable ethnic Russian population. Moscow used identical language about the rights of Russian speakers in eastern Ukraine in 2014 before the Donbas annexation. The pattern of Russian intimidation before action is consistent enough that the specific threat of "open war" should not simply be filed under routine diplomatic noise.

The leaked data published by the Guardian showing [more than 1,000 Russian military suicides](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/23/leaked-data-shows-more-than-1000-russian-military-suicides-during-ukraine-war) during the war — from a cache of 11 million death certificates — speaks to the profound human cost on the Russian side that rarely enters Western strategic calculations. These are not numbers that suggest a military on the verge of collapse, but they are numbers that suggest an institution under severe stress, which makes unpredictable decisions more, not less, likely.

## What to watch

The key signal is what happens at the Narva crossing. Estonia closes it December 1; watch for Russian-staged incidents in that border zone in the weeks following. Watch also whether the 22nd EU sanctions package, with its crypto and energy provisions, produces any measurable tightening of Russia's finances — previous packages have leaked through third-country channels. Zelensky's mention of "fourway peace talks with Russia, US, Europe before Christmas" — noted in the Guardian's live blog — is worth monitoring: it suggests Kyiv is at minimum performing openness to negotiation, though the conditions attached almost certainly make a pre-Christmas agreement implausible. And watch the UN's response to the Benghazi strike. If the Security Council takes it up, China and Russia will push for language condemning Ukraine's expansion of the conflict into neutral third-party territory.
