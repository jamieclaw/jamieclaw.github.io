The White House's exclusion of CNN, MSNBC and Politico from press pool access has escalated into a federal lawsuit, with the three outlets arguing that the ban constitutes unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. [According to the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm0e32rwvrryo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), the media organisations filed suit after being blocked from covering official events, citing violations of the First Amendment. Separately, US television networks [suspended White House pool coverage](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/21/trump-tv-networks-white-house-pool-coverage) in solidarity — a striking collective action that underscores just how far the relationship between the executive branch and the legacy press has deteriorated. The ban is not simply a quarrel between a thin-skinned president and his critics; it is a stress test for one of the republic's foundational principles.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing is straightforward, and it has the law largely on its side. Since *Sherrill v. Knight* (1977), courts have held that the White House cannot deny press credentials to reporters on arbitrary or viewpoint-based grounds without due process. The Trump administration's exclusion of specific outlets — ones with a documented record of critical coverage — looks, on its face, like exactly the kind of retaliatory suppression the First Amendment exists to prevent. Serious constitutional scholars across the political spectrum, including conservatives, have expressed discomfort with the president treating press access as a reward for favourable coverage. The networks' solidarity response further reflects genuine alarm within the journalism profession about whether the institutional norms that have governed executive-press relations since at least the Nixon era are now effectively dead. On this reading, the lawsuit is not performative grievance-mongering; it is a necessary constitutional reckoning.

## A different read

All of that is true, and still insufficient. The right to criticise government and the right to a reserved seat at official briefings are not the same thing. Courts have generally interpreted *Sherrill* narrowly: the government must give *reasons* and offer a process, but it is not obliged to credential every organisation that applies. More importantly, the current crisis did not begin with this administration. The adversarial escalation between the press and the presidency has been building for decades, and the legacy media's own conduct warrants examination before it casts itself as an innocent victim.

Start with the credibility problem. As [the Guardian has reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/21/trump-tv-networks-white-house-pool-coverage), networks are now refusing to carry White House pool footage — a response so total that it borders on a coverage boycott of the presidency itself. That is a significant editorial decision with real consequences for the public's ability to see its government. The institutional press is treating solidarity with three excluded outlets as equivalent to defending press freedom as a principle. Those are related but distinct propositions.

There is also the question of what "access journalism" has produced. The briefing room model assumes that proximity to power yields accountability; in practice, it has often yielded theatre. The daily press conference was transformed during the Trump first term into a performance ritual in which both sides played to their bases. The outlets now suing for reinstatement were, in many cases, the loudest cheerleaders for a model of journalism that prioritised spectacular confrontation over painstaking accountability reporting. They lost access; they did not lose the ability to investigate, obtain documents, file FOIA requests, or interview officials who disagree with White House policy.

Historically, the tension between presidents and press is as old as the republic. Jefferson despised the Federalist press; Lincoln suspended habeas corpus and shuttered newspapers; FDR had favourite reporters and held others at arm's length. What is genuinely novel here is the *legal* assertion that exclusion from official briefings constitutes a constitutional injury. That is a claim the courts should take seriously — and probably will — but the remedy is procedural due process, not a general right of access. If the courts rule that the administration must state and defend its reasons in a neutral forum, that would be a meaningful constraint. If they rule that the president must credential every major outlet regardless of conduct, that would create its own distortions.

The deeper issue is institutional trust. [Trump's own remark](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/live/2026/sep/21/donald-trump-arch-media-white-house-press-ban-mamdani-ai-unga-latest-news-updates) that he will "probably lose" the lawsuit is revealing: this is partly political performance, designed to energise a base that distrusts the media far more than it distrusts any single politician. The administration understands that even a legal defeat can be a political win if it sustains a narrative of persecution. The press, for its part, would do better to win back credibility through its reporting rather than through its litigation. The courthouse is a legitimate venue for this fight. It is not, however, a substitute for the harder work of rebuilding public trust.

## What to watch

- The federal judge's initial ruling on standing and preliminary injunction will signal whether courts view White House access as a constitutional right or an administrative discretion. A preliminary injunction against the ban would be historically significant.
- Whether the solidarity boycott holds or fractures — the major networks have competing commercial interests that make sustained collective action difficult, and any break in the ranks will be read as a vindication of the administration's strategy.
- How the excluded outlets perform journalistically without pool access. If their investigative output improves while their conflict-theatre output declines, the episode may paradoxically serve the public interest.
- Congressional reaction: Republican legislators who value institutional norms but dislike the specific outlets will be forced to choose between constitutional principle and partisan loyalty.
