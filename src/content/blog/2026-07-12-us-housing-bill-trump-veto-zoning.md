A landmark piece of US housing legislation — the 21st Century Road to Housing Act — became law this week without President Trump's signature. [According to BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn8qwj611qxo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), Trump allowed the bill to pass automatically by neither signing nor vetoing it, after previously cancelling the signing ceremony to protest the Senate's failure to advance separate voter identification legislation. Both chambers of Congress had passed the housing bill in a rare bipartisan vote; experts described it as the most comprehensive congressional action on housing affordability in the twenty-first century. The bill includes more than 40 provisions aimed at increasing housing supply, making construction easier, and limiting institutional investor purchases of single-family homes. The median price of existing US homes hit an all-time high of $440,660 in June, according to the National Association of Realtors — up 1.8 percent from the prior year. A US family now needs an income of roughly $117,000 per year to afford an average home, according to Redfin; median US household income falls nearly $30,000 short of that figure.

## The received wisdom

The dominant progressive read of this episode is a straightforward accountability story: Trump held a bipartisan housing bill hostage to his voter-ID demands, and only the constitutional mechanics of automatic enactment prevented the harm. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries captured the left's response concisely: Republicans "would rather make it harder to vote than easier to afford a home." The broader framing situates Trump's demands within a long pattern of election-integrity claims that courts and election officials have consistently rejected as unsupported, while the housing crisis — affecting renters and aspiring homeowners across the political spectrum — was one of the clearest areas of genuine cross-party voter demand. On this view, the episode illustrates the dysfunction of governing through grievance: even when the legislative process produces something genuinely useful, it gets subordinated to the culture-war agenda.

## A different read

There is something in the progressive critique worth honouring before complicating it: a president who cancels a signing ceremony for a bill that benefits his own voters because a separate bill failed is, in fact, prioritising political signalling over substantive governance. The housing crisis is a real emergency in the lives of tens of millions of Americans — the Bipartisan Policy Center found that [89 percent of voters across the political spectrum](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn8qwj611qxo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) wanted congressional action to lower housing costs. The bill exists because of that demand. Using it as leverage for voter ID — a priority of a narrower political coalition — was a genuine misuse of executive influence over the legislative agenda.

But the housing bill's substance deserves to be examined on its own terms, because it is the more durable story here. Housing affordability in the United States is the product of a multi-decade, bipartisan regulatory failure at the local level. Exclusionary zoning, NIMBY litigation, environmental review abuse, and restrictions on multi-family development have collectively suppressed housing supply in virtually every major American metropolitan area for fifty years. The result — a median home price of $440,660 against a median household income that cannot support that purchase — is not primarily a failure of the federal government. It is a failure of local governance, repeatedly ratified by existing homeowners who benefit from asset inflation at the expense of younger and lower-income people who are priced out of ownership and rental markets alike.

Federal housing legislation can help at the margins, and the Road to Housing Act's provision limiting institutional investor purchases of single-family homes is politically popular. But the deeper supply constraint lives in the zoning codes and permitting processes of municipalities — jurisdictions where the federal government has limited direct authority. Previous bipartisan housing frameworks, including efforts under both Obama and Trump's first term, foundered on exactly this constraint: federal incentives to reform local zoning are weak, and localities have strong political incentives to resist them regardless of which party controls Washington.

The more intellectually honest framing of this legislation is not "Congress finally fixed housing" but rather "Congress addressed several genuine federal-level distortions while leaving the primary structural problem — local supply restriction — largely intact." That is still worth doing. But presenting it as a comprehensive solution, which expert Dennis Shea's comment ("I don't use that word lightly") implicitly invited, risks setting expectations that the bill's eventual impact will fail to meet. The American housing crisis will not be solved by federal legislation alone; it will require the kind of sustained state and local political will to override exclusionary zoning that almost no major jurisdiction has demonstrated since Houston.

There is also a fiscal note worth registering. Several provisions of the bill that make it "easier to build" in practice involve changes to federal permitting and environmental review timelines. Conservatives are right to be cautious about whether those streamlining measures survive the regulatory bureaucracy intact, or whether agencies find ways to preserve their procedural footprint regardless of the legislative text.

## What to watch

- Implementation: which of the bill's 40-plus provisions are subject to agency rulemaking, and whether federal agencies — particularly those with a structural preference for process-heavy review — implement them in ways that actually accelerate construction or merely create new bureaucratic layers.
- Whether the provision limiting institutional investor purchases of single-family homes faces legal challenges on constitutional or property-rights grounds — it is one of the more novel federal interventions into real estate markets and will attract litigation.
- Local zoning reform activity: if federal action creates political space for states and municipalities to liberalise land use, the bill's downstream impact could significantly exceed its direct provisions; if local resistance stiffens in response to federal action, the supply problem persists.
- The political trajectory of the voter ID fight that triggered Trump's signing boycott: the SAVE Act still lacks the Senate votes to pass, and Trump's next leverage opportunity over a bipartisan bill is now unclear.
