Indonesia's Vice President stepped down from his post this week amid what [the BBC described](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmvgxgz87qqro?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as "signs the ruling alliance is fraying." The resignation — the first by an Indonesian vice president since independence — follows weeks of reported tensions within the coalition that brought President Prabowo Subianto to power in last year's election. The immediate trigger appears to be a policy dispute over the distribution of cabinet positions and the pace of Prabowo's nationalisation agenda, which has alarmed some of the business-aligned parties that backed his coalition while pleasing the nationalist wings of Gerindra, his own party. Indonesia is the world's fourth most populous country, a major emerging economy, and a democracy that has maintained broadly civilian governance since Suharto's fall in 1998. What happens to its political coalition over the next twelve months matters well beyond the archipelago.

## The received wisdom

The standard liberal-internationalist reading of the vice presidential resignation treats it as a natural and even healthy feature of Indonesia's young democracy: coalition politics are inherently messy, internal tensions are better aired than suppressed, and the fact that a vice president can resign without triggering a constitutional crisis is itself evidence of institutional maturity. On this account, Prabowo's broad-tent coalition — which spans nationalist populists, Islamist parties, business liberals, and the political networks inherited from the Jokowi era — was always going to face friction. What matters is whether the underlying democratic institutions can manage the friction without producing authoritarian backsliding. Indonesia has, on this view, proven its democratic resilience before, and there is no particular reason to expect it to fail now.

There is genuine historical evidence for this optimism. Indonesia survived the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, the Suharto transition, the Bali bombings, and multiple regional separatist movements. It has conducted six direct presidential elections since 2004, each broadly competitive and each producing a peaceful transfer of power. The democratic infrastructure — a constitutional court, an electoral commission, an independent press, a military that has (mostly) accepted civilian oversight — is real, however imperfect.

## A different read

But the comfortable narrative about Indonesian democratic resilience understates several things that make the current moment genuinely worrying.

Prabowo Subianto is not a normal politician. He is a former special forces general with a documented human rights record from the 1990s — he was dismissed from the military in 1998 following allegations of involvement in the enforced disappearance of student activists — who spent two decades in the political wilderness before the Indonesian electorate, tired of technocratic incrementalism, decided to give him a chance. He ran on a platform of economic nationalism, food sovereignty, and military modernisation. His coalition was assembled not around shared values but around the prospect of shared access to state resources — which is the most fragile kind of coalition there is.

The pattern that makes the vice presidential resignation alarming is not the resignation itself but what it signals about the coalition's internal bargaining. When a broad-tent government holds together, it is usually because the rents are large enough to satisfy all the constituent factions. When a faction feels it has been shortchanged, the incentive to defect — or to sell its continued participation at a higher price — becomes rational. Indonesia's economy, while growing, faces genuine headwinds: a commodity price cycle that has turned against it, a demographic dividend that is beginning to expire, and persistent challenges in manufacturing diversification. If the economic rents available for distribution shrink, the coalition's internal price of cohesion rises.

The deeper structural concern is about what Prabowo's nationalisation agenda actually entails. [Several ASEAN observers have noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmvgxgz87qqro?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the policy direction — state intervention in key industries, renegotiated resource contracts, expanded military involvement in civilian economic activities — is consistent with what scholars of post-democratisation call "competitive authoritarianism": a system that retains the forms of electoral democracy while systematically tilting the playing field toward incumbents. Indonesia has been here before, or close to it: the New Order under Suharto maintained elections and a parliament, but both were instruments of control rather than genuine accountability mechanisms.

The resignation of a vice president who represented the more business-liberal wing of the coalition, if that is indeed the dynamic at play, would be a concerning signal that the balancing act is tipping. A government that loses its internal liberal check and doubles down on economic nationalism, military preference, and resource control is harder to correct at the ballot box.

Indonesia is too large and too strategically significant — [sitting astride the sea lanes linking the Indian and Pacific Oceans, adjacent to the South China Sea dispute, and hosting the world's largest Muslim-majority population](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwvg25nypmrvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — for its democratic health to be anyone's peripheral concern. The West, in particular, has tended to treat Indonesia as a low-maintenance success story. The vice presidential resignation is a prompt to look more carefully.

## What to watch

- **Who replaces the vice president**: The replacement pick will reveal whether Prabowo is trying to hold the coalition together by accommodating the disaffected faction or is consolidating power around the nationalist-military core.
- **The constitutional process**: Indonesia's constitution requires parliamentary approval for a new vice president; watch whether the process is transparent or choreographed.
- **Jokowi's network**: Former President Jokowi retains significant political influence through his infrastructure networks and regional allies; whether his faction's interests were among those shortchanged will determine its future stance.
- **Foreign investment flows**: If business-aligned coalition partners publicly express concern about the nationalisation agenda, watch for a response in FDI data and in the rupiah.
