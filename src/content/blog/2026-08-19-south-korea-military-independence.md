South Korea's president has called for the country to develop independent military capabilities, responding to reports that Donald Trump had directed the Pentagon to scale back or cancel joint US–South Korean military exercises. The remarks came after [Trump moved to cut joint drills](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/17/trump-south-korea-alliance), a decision that US lawmakers from both parties described as "inane" and "haphazard." Seoul subsequently issued a statement stressing the importance of the bilateral alliance while [the president called for military self-reliance](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/18/south-korea-president-trump-military), a phrase that carries enormous strategic weight in a country that has relied on the American security umbrella for seventy-three years. [South Korea also stressed its US alliance](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/18/g-s1-139096/s-korea-stresses-us-alliance-amid-confusion-over-trump-drill-cut) even while signalling it can no longer take that alliance for granted.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading is one of concern about Trump's alliance management: an erratic commander-in-chief, for reasons that remain opaque, has once again undermined a cornerstone security relationship at the worst possible moment — with North Korea's nuclear programme more capable than ever and China watching every signal emanating from Washington. In this framing, Seoul's call for military autonomy is a rational and reluctant response to an unreliable partner, not a strategic pivot. The Democrats calling the drill cuts "inane" are broadly correct: joint exercises are the operational glue of the alliance. They keep interoperability alive, signal resolve to Pyongyang, and reassure a population that has lived under the shadow of the North for generations. If Trump is genuinely willing to degrade that relationship for leverage in some unspecified negotiation with Kim Jong-un — or simply because he resents the cost — the consequences for stability in Northeast Asia could be severe. From this view, Seoul's president is doing the only prudent thing: hedging.

## A different read

The mainstream concern is legitimate. But the situation is more strategically interesting than the "Trump damages an alliance" narrative alone captures. For decades, successive American administrations — Republican and Democrat alike — have urged South Korea to do more for its own defence. Seoul's defence budget has grown substantially, and its military is formidable, but the country has remained structurally dependent on the American extended deterrent in a way that has sometimes discouraged the development of truly sovereign capabilities. Trump's chaos, however counter-productive his methods, may be inadvertently accelerating a conversation that Seoul needed to have anyway.

The analogy here is Europe. After Trump's first term rattled NATO allies, Germany began — haltingly, insufficiently, but genuinely — rethinking its defence posture. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 completed the psychological shock that Trump had begun. The result is a European defence architecture that is, for the first time in decades, treating the American umbrella as a supplement rather than a substitute. South Korea faces a structurally similar problem: a country of fifty-two million people, a top-fifteen global economy, and a world-class industrial base that is nonetheless asking Washington to carry the nuclear deterrent on its behalf.

There is a harder question lurking here too. The drill cuts reportedly came alongside Trump's suggestion that South Korea had not contributed enough to the cost of stationing US forces on the peninsula. This is not a new complaint — Trump raised it persistently in his first term — and it is not an entirely unreasonable one, even if the method of raising it (abrupt operational disruption rather than negotiated burden-sharing talks) is diplomatically reckless. The United States does bear a disproportionate share of the alliance's cost, and there is a legitimate conservative argument that allies capable of doing more should be pushed to do so.

The problem, as Niall Ferguson has noted in the broader context of American alliance management, is that there is a fundamental difference between disciplined retrenchment — using leverage to renegotiate burden-sharing within a framework both parties value — and erratic signalling that undermines the credibility of the commitment itself. The former strengthens alliances; the latter hollows them out. What Seoul appears to be reading in Washington's behaviour is the latter. A South Korea that genuinely pivots toward strategic autonomy, acquiring independent strike capabilities and potentially revisiting its commitment not to develop nuclear weapons, would be a profound shift in the Indo-Pacific order — one that China would view with alarm, Japan with anxiety, and the United States with a mixture of satisfaction and regret.

The deeper risk is the domino logic. If South Korea concludes that the American guarantee is contingent on an unpredictable president's mood, Japan will draw the same lesson. Japan is already quietly expanding its defence posture under Prime Minister Ishiba's government. Taiwan is watching. The architecture of the post-1945 Pacific, imperfect as it is, rests on the credibility of American commitments. Every drill cut, every "inane" announcement that goes unexplained, erodes that credibility a little further.

## What to watch

- Whether Seoul moves toward acquiring its own long-range strike capability, possibly including a reconsideration of its self-imposed nuclear restraint — the most seismic potential signal.
- Whether the Trump administration provides any strategic rationale for the drill cuts, or whether Congress acts to prohibit the drawdown unilaterally, as it has done with other alliance-weakening moves.
- Japan's reaction: Tokyo has for years quietly expanded its security perimeter; a South Korean pivot would accelerate that process significantly.
- Whether Kim Jong-un interprets the US–South Korea friction as an opportunity to press for a diplomatic opening or to test new provocations.
