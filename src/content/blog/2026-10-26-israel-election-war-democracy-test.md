Israel went to the polls on Monday in what analysts are calling [one of the most pivotal elections in the country's history](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckpdgy6pl7xlo), with voters choosing between a Netanyahu-led right-wing bloc and a broad opposition coalition that includes centrists, Arab-Israeli parties, and remnants of the political left. The election takes place against the backdrop of an ongoing conflict in Gaza, with [West Bank crossings closed on election day](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6y93ylpl5p2o) as Israeli forces maintained their expanded West Bank operations. [NPR noted](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/25/g-s1-149563/israel-election-war-netanyahu) that Israeli society has undergone a profound psychological transformation since October 7, 2023, with polling suggesting that security considerations are displacing the judicial reform debate that dominated 2023's mass protests. The question is not simply who wins, but whether any result produces a government capable of making the hard choices the current moment demands.

## The received wisdom

The dominant international framing — particularly prominent in European and American left-liberal media — holds that this election is a referendum on Netanyahu's fitness to govern, that his continuation in office is bad for Israeli democracy, dangerous for regional stability, and an obstacle to any eventual Palestinian political settlement. This view points, with justice, to the judicial overhaul crisis of 2023, the October 7 intelligence failures, and the [controversial West Bank operations including Hebron](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/24/israel-soldiers-raze-west-bank-neighbourhood-hebron-families-from-homes) that have expanded significantly during the election period. It argues that Netanyahu's political survival has become inextricably entangled with the prosecution of a war that serves his legal predicament as much as Israel's strategic interest. That case has genuine empirical grounding and is made by serious Israeli commentators, not just foreign observers.

## A different read

The trouble with this framing is that it understates the genuine strategic dilemmas any Israeli government faces, and overstates the degree to which the opposition offers a coherent alternative.

Begin with the security reality. Since October 7, 2023, Israel has dismantled much of Hamas's military infrastructure in Gaza, but at enormous cost in civilian suffering and international legitimacy. The question of "the day after" in Gaza — what governance structure replaces Hamas — remains unanswered by any Israeli political faction, left or right. The opposition's position is that a hostage deal and a political horizon for Palestinian statehood are both more achievable without Netanyahu; but the same opposition cannot say what it would actually offer to the Palestinian Authority, which has its own profound legitimacy crisis, or to regional actors like Qatar and Egypt whose interests do not straightforwardly align with Israeli security requirements.

The domestic dimension is equally fraught. [BBC's analysis of the election noted the bitter underlying row over military service exemptions for ultra-Orthodox men](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmvg7w0x742do) — a fault line that runs deeper than Netanyahu and predates him. The ultra-Orthodox community has grown dramatically as a share of the Israeli population, increasingly strains the military's manpower planning, and is disproportionately dependent on state subsidies while contributing relatively little to the tax base. Any government that addresses this — and it eventually must be addressed — will face a coalition crisis. Netanyahu's right-wing coalition includes the ultra-Orthodox parties precisely because their support is necessary for a parliamentary majority; an opposition government would face the same structural constraint in a different form if it tried to govern with their cooperation, or a different crisis if it tried to govern without them.

The international community tends to analyse Israeli elections through the lens of the Palestinian question, but for many Israeli voters — particularly those who were at the forefront of the 2023 judicial reform protests — the primary question is about the character of Israeli democracy itself: whether the state remains governed by law rather than by the emergency prerogatives of a man facing criminal trial. [NPR's coverage highlighted this dimension](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/25/g-s1-149563/israel-election-war-netanyahu), quoting Israeli commentators who argue that the war has paradoxically both strengthened Netanyahu's hand (by making security paramount) and weakened it (by exposing the catastrophic intelligence failure on his watch). That paradox makes outcome prediction unusually difficult.

Israel is a small, capable democracy operating under genuine existential pressure. Its voters deserve credit for taking these questions seriously, even if the choices they face are harder than the international commentary typically allows. The outcome matters not just for Israel and the Palestinians but for the broader question of whether democracies under security stress can make institutional choices that preserve both their security and their constitutional character.

## What to watch

- **Coalition arithmetic**: Netanyahu needs his full right-wing bloc to hold; even one defection collapses the majority. Watch Gantz and the centrist factions for any last-minute movement.
- **Arab-Israeli voter turnout**: Higher turnout in Arab-Israeli communities historically benefits the opposition. If mobilisation remains low — a concern given community disillusionment over Gaza — it advantage the right.
- **The hostage file**: Whether a new government, of either composition, moves quickly on a hostage deal will be the first concrete test of whether electoral change alters strategic outcomes.
- **Judicial reform resumption**: If Netanyahu returns to power with a full coalition, watch whether the judicial overhaul agenda — paused during the war — is revived as a coalition management tool.
