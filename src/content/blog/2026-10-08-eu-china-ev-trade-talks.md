European Union negotiators have opened talks in Beijing with the express aim of curtailing the flood of subsidised Chinese hybrid and electric vehicles into the European market, [according to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/oct/07/eu-negotiators-head-to-china-hoping-to-curb-cheap-imports-of-hybrid-electric-cars). The talks follow Brussels' imposition of provisional tariffs on Chinese EV imports earlier this year — levies that Beijing has challenged as protectionist and retaliatory — and run in parallel with EU efforts to negotiate a price undertaking agreement under which Chinese manufacturers would commit to minimum selling prices rather than face escalating duties. The stakes are substantial: [Al Jazeera reports](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/7/eu-china-trade-talks-begin-in-beijing-amid-escalating-pressure) that the two sides are under "escalating pressure" to reach a framework deal before the tariff review process locks in permanent measures that would be far harder to walk back.

## The received wisdom

The progressive-technocratic framing of this dispute is that Brussels is doing exactly what a responsible trading bloc should: using legally defensible trade tools to respond to market-distorting state subsidies, while keeping diplomatic channels open. China's EV manufacturers — BYD, SAIC, Nio, and others — benefit from an industrial policy ecosystem involving state loans, below-market energy prices, and integrated supply-chain subsidies that no private Western competitor can replicate. Under WTO rules, subsidies that harm foreign competitors are actionable. The EU has done its homework: the anti-subsidy investigation was lengthy, transparent, and methodologically serious. Maintaining European manufacturing capacity — including the vast supply chains of VW, Stellantis, and Renault that employ millions across France, Germany, Spain, and Italy — is a legitimate policy interest, not mere protectionism. The right will always label industrial policy as market interference; the reality is that the alternative is deindustrialisation, and the social costs of that are borne disproportionately by the working class the left claims to represent.

This is a genuinely strong argument. The asymmetry of Chinese industrial policy is real and documented.

## A different read

But the EU's position contains a contradiction that the negotiators in Beijing will not be able to paper over.

The contradiction is this: Europe's EV transition depends on cheap electric cars. The entire strategic rationale for the EV mandate — banning new internal combustion engines by 2035 — rested on the assumption that EV prices would fall fast enough for ordinary European households to afford the transition without extraordinary subsidy. That price-fall is happening, and it is happening primarily because of Chinese manufacturing scale and state-backed cost compression. [BYD and other Chinese manufacturers have achieved price points that European incumbents have not matched](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/7/eu-china-trade-talks-begin-in-beijing-amid-escalating-pressure), and the principal beneficiaries of cheap Chinese EVs are not wealthy early adopters but the middle-income families that European climate policy urgently needs to capture.

So the EU is simultaneously committed to an EV transition that requires cheap EVs and is using trade law to make Chinese EVs expensive. It is trying to protect European manufacturers — whose EV cost curves are higher — while claiming the protection is in the service of consumers and the planet. You cannot square this circle with a price undertaking agreement. Minimum pricing arrangements are, by design, price-floors that protect incumbents. They are the opposite of the competitive pressure that drives technological cost reduction.

The historical parallel that comes to mind is the Multi-Fibre Arrangement, the complex quota system that protected Western textile manufacturing from Asian competition from the 1970s until 2005. It succeeded in its narrow aim — slowing the pace of textile industry decline in France, Britain, and the United States — while significantly raising the cost of clothing for ordinary consumers and delaying the development of competitive domestic manufacturing alternatives. When the MFA finally expired, the adjustment was brutal precisely because the protected industries had not used the breathing space to restructure.

There is a respectable case for managed transition: giving European manufacturers time to retool their production systems, build battery supply chains, and achieve competitive cost structures is not obviously irrational. That case requires honesty about what it is — a temporary production subsidy for incumbents, paid for by consumers and taxpayers — rather than dressing it up as a principled response to unfair competition. Germany's car industry, which lobbied hardest against the tariffs before the political wind shifted, at least had the refreshing candour of naked self-interest.

The deeper geopolitical dimension is that these trade talks occur as China and the EU are simultaneously competing and interdependent across energy, finance, and supply chains in ways that make the MFA precedent look simple. Beijing will extract concessions on something — market access elsewhere, regulatory treatment of Chinese investment, European positioning on Taiwan — as the price of any deal on EVs. The question is whether Brussels has adequately priced that into its negotiating mandate.

## What to watch

- Whether a price undertaking agreement is reached before the tariff review deadline, and at what minimum price level — the number matters for the affordability question.
- Germany's posture: Berlin has been the internal EU voice for accommodation with Beijing, and German domestic politics — with the Scholz coalition fragile — may pull in different directions.
- China's counter-moves: retaliatory tariffs on European cognac, dairy, and luxury goods are already under consideration, and their imposition would sharpen the European industry-vs-consumer divide.
- Whether any deal includes Chinese investment in European EV manufacturing — a potential route to technology transfer that would undercut the protectionist rationale.
