[Sales of Chinese-made hybrid cars have exploded in the European Union](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/24/eu-sales-chinese-hybrid-cars-rocket), with fully hybrid vehicles jumping from 659 units sold in 2022 to 160,662 in the first seven months of 2026 alone. Plug-in hybrids from Chinese manufacturers have risen even faster, from 56,706 across all of 2022 to 217,764 in just January–July this year. The surge followed Brussels' imposition of anti-subsidy tariffs on fully electric vehicles from China in 2024 — tariffs that hybrids were not subject to. Three Chinese manufacturers — BYD, Chery, and Leapmotor — recorded triple-digit sales growth in the bloc, while Geely (owner of Volvo and Polestar) sold 205,000 vehicles in the first eight months of the year. BYD's EU sales [rocketed 163% year on year to 177,000 units](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/24/eu-sales-chinese-hybrid-cars-rocket), overtaking Tesla's 142,000. European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen has described the EU-China trade deficit — now running at €1.18 billion a day — as having reached an unsustainable "tipping point." Brussels has asked China to voluntarily reduce hybrid exports or face quotas, with trade talks scheduled for 8–9 October. Germany's VDA automotive lobby, which previously [opposed EV tariffs](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2024/oct/04/eu-chinese-ev-tariffs-split-vote-germany-car-firms) to protect its own China sales, has now indicated it would consider hybrid-specific tariffs.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive-technocratic framing celebrates the rise of Chinese electric and hybrid vehicles as proof that the green transition is working globally — if European consumers can buy affordable Chinese-made hybrid cars, they are accelerating decarbonisation at lower cost, which is precisely what the Paris Agreement demands. Tariff protection for European legacy automakers, on this reading, is industrial-policy rent-seeking dressed up as strategic competition — it raises consumer prices, punishes exactly the behaviour the EU's own Green Deal should encourage, and produces the same kind of "Fortress Europe" industrial dysfunction that left the continent's car industry dependent on Russian gas for too long. The European Commission should instead push for investment partnerships, technology-sharing agreements, and consumer subsidies — not quotas that protect Volkswagen's shareholders at the expense of French retirees who want an affordable plug-in hybrid.

## A different read

The problem with this framing is that it treats a trade policy question as a climate question, which allows the climate argument to pre-empt the industrial strategy question. Whether Chinese hybrid cars are good for the atmosphere (they are, at the margin) is a separate question from whether their subsidised penetration of the EU market at scale is good for European strategic autonomy, employment, or long-term technological capacity.

Begin with the subsidy question, which is not in serious dispute. [China's automotive industry has received enormous state support](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/24/eu-sales-chinese-hybrid-cars-rocket) — direct grants, subsidised land, cheap industrial credit, and procurement advantages — that EU automakers simply cannot access. When Brussels imposed EV tariffs in 2024, it was applying a WTO-compatible anti-subsidy remedy, not protectionism in the pure sense. The problem is that the tariff architects defined the tariff around a technology category — "fully electric" — rather than around the economic behaviour being targeted, which is subsidised manufacturing. Chinese carmakers pivoted to hybrids with admirable industrial agility, and the EU found itself having won the battle over BEVs while losing the market.

The VDA's belated conversion to tariff-mindedness is itself revealing. German carmakers spent years arguing against EV tariffs because they had significant China production and China sales that retaliation could damage. Volkswagen's Wolfsburg plant closures, combined with [50,000 further job cuts](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/03/volkswagen-confirms-100000-job-cuts-by-2030), have concentrated minds. The political economy of tolerance for Chinese competition changes sharply when it is German industrial workers rather than German consumers who bear the cost. This does not make the VDA's new position hypocritical — it makes it democratic, in the specific sense that democratic industrial policy is supposed to reflect the interests of workers and communities, not just consumers and shareholders.

The deeper issue is about what kind of industrial capacity Europe wants to retain. Hybrid vehicles are a transitional technology, likely to remain commercially significant for at least a decade given the pace of charging infrastructure deployment and consumer range anxiety. If European manufacturers cede the hybrid market entirely — as they appear to be doing — they lose the production volumes, the supply chain relationships, and the engineering talent that make the electric transition feasible. You cannot simply skip from an ICE industry to an EV industry if you have been put out of business in the intermediate stages. Japan's strategy with Toyota, Nissan, and Honda in the 1980s — transitioning from cost competition to quality competition while maintaining domestic production — succeeded because Japan protected intermediate-stage manufacturing. Europe is doing the opposite: protecting the starting point (combustion engines) while losing the transition.

The EU-China trade talks on 8–9 October will be decisive. [Trump and Xi's discussion of rare earth export suspensions](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/24/eu-sales-chinese-hybrid-cars-rocket) adds another variable: if Washington and Beijing reach a rare earths deal, it may accelerate Chinese EV manufacturing in ways that further disadvantage European producers who are not part of the US-China bilateral arrangement. Europe is increasingly a third party in a bilateral US-China negotiation over the terms of the industrial future — which is exactly the position a €1.18 billion daily trade deficit suggests it has already conceded.

## What to watch

Watch the outcome of EU-China trade commissioner talks on 8–9 October: whether Brussels can secure a credible voluntary export restraint agreement, or whether China refuses and forces a formal quota — which triggers WTO dispute proceedings and Chinese retaliation. Track BYD's European manufacturing plans: the company has a plant under construction in Hungary, which if operational would allow it to sell as a "European-made" vehicle and defeat any further import tariffs. Monitor Volkswagen's Wolfsburg restructuring for signs of whether German industrial policy is shifting toward supporting tariffs, which would decisively change the EU political calculus. Finally, watch how Geely's Volvo and Polestar navigate the hybrid category — they are technically "Chinese" for trade purposes while being Swedish in brand and partly European in production, a classification problem that will test EU trade law.
