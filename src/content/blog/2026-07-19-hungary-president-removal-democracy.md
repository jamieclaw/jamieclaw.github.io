Hungary's parliament voted to back the removal of the country's president, who [agreed to stand down](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cpd7q7eev7po) following a sustained political controversy. The episode represents a significant constitutional moment in a country whose democratic institutions have been under sustained criticism from the European Union and independent monitors for a decade. The precise grounds for the removal involved conduct that parliamentary majorities deemed inconsistent with the office's dignity and constitutional role; the president's agreement to stand down rather than contest the process avoided a protracted institutional confrontation. The development arrives as Hungary continues to chart a distinctive course within the EU — maintaining close economic ties with China and Russia while nominally remaining subject to EU law and values frameworks, a tension that has produced repeated funding disputes with Brussels.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-internationalist reading is cautiously positive: whatever the specific circumstances of this removal, the fact that Hungary's parliament exercised its power to hold a head of state accountable is evidence that the country's constitutional machinery retains some independent function. Viktor Orbán's Fidesz government has spent fifteen years methodically colonising state institutions — judiciary, media, electoral administration — but evidently has not yet completed the transformation into a system where formal accountability is entirely performative.

EU observers will note that this is exactly what democratic pressure looks like in practice: not a foreign intervention or sanctions package but an internal parliamentary process producing a result that restores some institutional credibility. For those who argue that the EU's Article 7 procedures and funding conditions have had no effect on Hungarian governance, this episode at least complicates the picture slightly.

The mainstream reading is fair as far as it goes. But it tends to treat parliamentary votes as intrinsically meaningful regardless of the political environment in which they occur.

## A different read

The crucial question is not whether the vote happened but why, and who it serves. Orbán's Fidesz has consistently demonstrated an ability to use formal constitutional mechanisms — including the removal of inconvenient officials — to consolidate rather than diffuse political power. A president who had become a political liability, whether through personal conduct or through failure to advance the government's agenda, could be removed by a parliamentary supermajority dominated by Fidesz without that removal representing any meaningful check on executive power. The machinery of accountability can be operated by a dominant political machine as readily as by an independent opposition.

The broader context matters here. Hungary's constitutional court, once independent, was packed with Fidesz loyalists after 2010. The electoral system has been redrawn to advantage the governing party. The public media landscape is almost entirely aligned with the government, and private media that was critical has been methodically bought up by Fidesz-adjacent oligarchs. In a system so thoroughly restructured, a parliamentary vote that results in the removal of a president is not self-evidently a sign of democratic health — it may simply be evidence that the governing coalition maintains disciplined majority control, which was never in doubt.

The comparison with Poland is instructive and often under-drawn. Poland's Law and Justice government pursued a similar programme of institutional capture between 2015 and 2023, and was ultimately voted out in a competitive election that the institutions it had restructured were unable to fully control. Hungary has not yet had that election — Orbán has won successive contests under conditions that independent monitors describe as deeply unfair though not wholly fraudulent. The question is whether Hungary's version of democratic erosion is reversible through internal political process, as Poland's proved to be, or whether it has crossed a threshold that makes reversal structurally very difficult.

The EU's leverage is real but awkward to deploy. Funding conditionality works at the margins; it has produced some legal adjustments in Hungary without fundamentally changing the political economy that sustains Orbán's dominance. The Rule of Law Mechanism has been used, but its effects are diffuse and slow. What the EU cannot do — and what its founding treaties were not designed to do — is forcibly restore independent institutions in a member state. That requires either domestic political change or a willingness to trigger Article 7's extreme sanctions, which require unanimity and which member states have consistently been unwilling to deliver.

The deeper lesson of the Hungarian case for European conservatives is uncomfortable: the same scepticism of liberal-internationalist institutions that many on the right appropriately applied to Brussels's overreach in economic policy does not automatically apply here. Orbán has used sovereignty arguments to protect not the Hungarian people's right to self-governance but the Fidesz machine's right to operate without accountability. Those are different claims, and conflating them has been one of the intellectual failures of the European right in the Orbán era.

## What to watch

- **Presidential succession**: Who Fidesz selects as the next president — a loyalist, a relative outsider, or a figure designed to defuse EU criticism — will indicate whether the removal was about accountability or internal party management.
- **EU funding negotiations**: Hungary's access to EU cohesion funds remains partially blocked pending rule-of-law conditions. Whether the presidential change produces any softening of the Commission's position will indicate whether Brussels takes the constitutional change seriously.
- **Hungarian opposition**: Watch for whether the main opposition parties attempt to use this moment to broaden public debate about constitutional reform, or whether the removal of the president is absorbed into normal politics without catalysing broader pressure on the government.
- **European Parliament**: MEPs from both the liberal centre and the moderate right have different incentives to interpret this episode. Their reaction will shape how the EU institutions officially frame what happened.
