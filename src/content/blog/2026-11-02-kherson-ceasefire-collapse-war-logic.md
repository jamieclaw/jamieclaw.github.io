A short-lived ceasefire around the Kherson region has collapsed, with Russian forces [resuming strikes that cut power to thousands](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c64g1y90p71eo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) of Ukrainian civilians. Al Jazeera reported that [Kherson was bracing for a return to full-scale fighting](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/1/ukraine-evacuates-civilians-in-kherson-) as the localised truce expired, with Ukrainian authorities conducting civilian evacuations in anticipation. Meanwhile, Ukraine's air-raid warning application was apparently compromised, with users receiving [false radiation threat notifications](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c51kdy1ew7zvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — a sign that the information war is being fought as aggressively as the kinetic one. The resumption of strikes follows a pattern now familiar to observers of this conflict: any pause in hostilities becomes an occasion for repositioning, resupply, and renewed pressure rather than a genuine pathway toward negotiated resolution.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream interpretation of Kherson's ceasefire collapse runs roughly as follows: Russia never negotiated in good faith, the truce was a tactical manoeuvre to consolidate positions, and the international community's continued pressure — through sanctions, weapons transfers, and diplomatic isolation — remains the only viable path to Ukrainian sovereignty. This framing holds that Moscow calculates rationally under coercion, that enough pain will eventually produce a negotiated settlement on terms Ukraine can accept, and that the West's job is to maintain solidarity and resist ceasefire pressure from those who would reward Russian aggression with territorial gains. There is real merit to this view. Putin's track record — Chechnya, Georgia 2008, Donbas 2014, the full-scale invasion of 2022 — is one of exploiting hesitation rather than responding to it. Ceasefires that freeze conflicts have, historically, tended to freeze them in Russia's favour.

## A different read

The trouble with the received wisdom is not that it is wrong about Putin's intentions; it is that it has developed a troubling immunity to the question of ends. The logic of sustained attrition requires a plausible theory of victory — some account of what "enough pressure" actually produces, and on what timeline. After nearly five years of full-scale war, that theory has not been clearly articulated by any Western government.

Localised ceasefires like the Kherson arrangement deserve more analytical attention than they tend to receive. The historical record of sub-regional truces in protracted wars is, admittedly, poor. The 1994 Bishkek Protocol in Nagorno-Karabakh produced a frozen conflict that lasted until Azerbaijan shattered it in 2020. The various humanitarian corridors negotiated in Syria produced tactical pauses that Assad and his backers exploited to consolidate. Partial truces in existential wars tend to fail because the parties have not changed their fundamental objectives — they have merely changed their operational posture.

But there is a difference worth noting. The Kherson ceasefire was not a grand diplomatic initiative; it was a localised, limited arrangement around a specific piece of contested territory. Its failure tells us something specific: that even the smallest de-escalatory gestures remain hostage to strategic calculations that neither side has any incentive to alter while the military balance remains genuinely contested. Russia has shown, by [striking power infrastructure](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c64g1y90p71eo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) within hours of the ceasefire's expiry, that it treats civilian energy grids as legitimate military targets — a posture that is both a war crime under international law and a rational instrument of coercion designed to break Ukrainian civilian morale before winter.

The cyber dimension compounds this. The compromise of Ukraine's air-raid app — [sending false radiation alerts to civilians](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c51kdy1ew7zvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — is not a trivial nuisance. It is an attempt to corrode the trust between the Ukrainian state and its own population, to make the apparatus of civil defence feel unreliable at exactly the moment when that apparatus is most needed. This is a form of warfare that Western democracies have been slow to take seriously as a category requiring specific deterrence and response.

The right-of-centre case for continued Ukrainian support is not sentimental about Ukraine's virtues or naive about the costs. It rests on a structural argument: a Europe in which Russia's 2022 gamble is rewarded with territorial control and Western exhaustion is a Europe that has fundamentally revised the post-1945 settlement. Smaller states — the Baltic states, Moldova, Georgia, eventually Poland — would draw the correct lesson. That lesson is not one Western policymakers should want drawn. The question is not whether to support Ukraine but whether Western governments have been honest with their own publics about the duration and scale of commitment that structural argument actually requires. They have not.

What is also missing from most Western commentary is an honest accounting of what this winter will mean. Russia's targeting of power infrastructure is not random; it is a deliberate strategy to make Ukrainian urban life unbearable before spring, testing Western public patience for continuing to fund a war whose end is not in sight. The management of public opinion in Germany, France, and the United States will matter as much over the next six months as the movement of front lines.

## What to watch

- Whether Russia's infrastructure strikes this winter produce measurable shifts in European public support polling — watch German and French opinion trackers specifically, as both governments face domestic fiscal constraints that make sustained Ukrainian aid politically difficult.
- The fate of US military aid authorisations in the new Congress — the midterm results will determine whether the next legislative package passes or stalls into 2027.
- Any indication that Russia is using the ceasefire's collapse as cover for a significant offensive push rather than merely consolidating — the distinction matters for Western force-posture decisions.
- Whether Ukraine's cyber authorities publicly attribute the air-raid app compromise and to which Russian intelligence directorate — the attribution politics will shape NATO's response posture.
