At least [72 people died](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/24/guyana-coastal-ferry-sinking-deaths-rescue) when a state-operated coastal ferry capsized off Guyana's coast last week, making it the worst maritime disaster in the South American nation's recorded history. Of an estimated 179 people aboard, only 76 were rescued as a multinational team worked to salvage the partially submerged vessel. The Guyanese government estimated the final death toll could reach 100. Guyana, a country of roughly 800,000 people, has been the fastest-growing economy in the world for the past several years, driven by the discovery and exploitation of substantial offshore oil reserves by ExxonMobil and its partners. The ferry accident has prompted questions about whether the country's infrastructure and public safety institutions have kept pace with the oil boom — and whether the governance model surrounding that boom will serve ordinary Guyanese citizens.

## The received wisdom

The progressive development-sector framing of a disaster like this centres on familiar themes: the "resource curse," the failure of post-colonial institutions, corporate extraction without local benefit, and the global inequality that leaves citizens of developing nations dependent on decrepit state infrastructure while foreign firms extract wealth. In this reading, ExxonMobil and Hess are implicitly responsible for the ferry — not in any direct legal sense, but in the broader sense that a better-governed, more equitably resourced state would have upgraded its transport infrastructure years ago. International attention to Guyana tends to focus on environmental concerns about drilling, not on whether the national ferry service is safe.

There is something to this. Guyana's oil revenue — [the country has become one of the world's highest per-capita oil producers](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/26/us-and-iran-hit-pause-on-strikes-for-second-day) virtually overnight — has accrued substantially to a National Resource Fund whose disbursement rules are politically contested. If those funds had been directed toward maritime safety, the ferry might not have sunk. And the broader point about institutional capacity lagging commodity booms is empirically robust: Norway is the exception in managing oil wealth well, not the rule.

## A different read

But the "resource curse" framing, for all its partial truth, obscures a more uncomfortable diagnosis: the failures that kill people in countries like Guyana are failures of governance and legal accountability, not primarily failures of revenue.

Guyana is not a poor country by the standards of its region. Even before the oil boom, it had sufficient revenue to operate a safe national ferry service — or to purchase one under credible concession terms from private operators. The vessel that capsized was a state ferry, which means its maintenance, crewing standards, and passenger capacity limits were state responsibilities. The question is not whether Guyana had the money, but whether it had the institutions to translate money into competent administration.

This distinction matters because it changes the prescription. If the problem is revenue, the answer is: redistribute oil wealth faster. If the problem is institutions, the answer is far harder: build a civil service culture, an independent inspectorate, enforceable safety standards, and a legal system capable of holding state operators accountable when people die. The latter project takes decades and cannot be imported.

The history of rapid commodity booms in small states is instructive. Nauru was once the richest country in the world per capita from phosphate; today it is dependent on Australian aid. Trinidad and Tobago has managed oil wealth better but still exhibits the classic symptoms — public sector bloat, service delivery failures, high crime — despite generations of substantial revenue. Botswana is the most frequently cited success case in Africa, and its lesson is precisely about institutions: it had a functional traditional governance structure, a small enough population to maintain accountability, and early decisions to invest revenue in education and infrastructure rather than consumption.

Guyana's challenge is that it lacks some of those advantages. Its population is ethnically divided along lines that have historically driven political competition to prioritise patronage over service delivery. Its National Resource Fund was designed partly to insulate oil revenue from that dynamic, but the political will to enforce the insulation is inevitably contested. None of this is destiny. Small nations with difficult institutional histories have built decent states — Cape Verde, Mauritius, Rwanda in its own complicated way. The ferry disaster may be the kind of event that galvanises domestic pressure for accountability: an immediate, visible, undeniable failure of the state to protect its own people.

That would require political leaders willing to make the unglamorous case for boring competence — for safety inspectorates, maintenance budgets, and civil liability. It is less exciting than declaring energy sovereignty or hosting international summits, but it is what actually determines whether ordinary citizens live or die on the water.

## What to watch

- Whether the Guyanese government orders a full independent inquiry with public findings — or whether the investigation is absorbed into the normal bureaucratic memory hole.
- The National Resource Fund disbursement debate: does the ferry disaster produce political momentum for directing oil revenue toward infrastructure and safety, or does it remain captured by higher-profile competing claims?
- International coverage: disasters of this scale in Latin America tend to receive a day's attention and then vanish. Whether sustained reporting follows will partly determine whether accountability pressure persists.
- ExxonMobil's local content obligations: the concession terms for Guyana's offshore blocks included local hiring and infrastructure commitments. How those are being enforced is worth examining.
