Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky described the first day of formal peace talks as "[constructive](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqpvgvrpjvv8o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss)" on Tuesday, marking the first direct multilateral diplomatic engagement between the parties since the war entered its fifth year. The talks, convened under the auspices of a European-led framework with Gulf state participation, brought Ukrainian, Russian, and mediating delegations to the table after months of back-channel preparation. No ceasefire agreement was announced, and both sides characterised the session as preliminary. European officials struck a cautious but notably less pessimistic tone than had been standard for most of the conflict's duration. The opening round's agenda reportedly covered prisoner exchanges and humanitarian corridors rather than territorial questions, which remain the fundamental point of contention. International observers, including American envoys, attended as observers rather than principals.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-internationalist mainstream has greeted the talks with cautious enthusiasm. The argument is that any diplomatic opening, however tentative, is better than the indefinite continuation of a war that has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced millions. The multilateral framework, involving European guarantors and Gulf mediation, is seen as a structural improvement over the failed bilateral contacts of 2022, which Russia used instrumentally to buy time for its military reorganisation. Zelensky's readiness to engage is interpreted as a sign of Ukrainian pragmatism — an acknowledgment that the battlefield situation, while not catastrophic, does not support indefinite resistance without a political horizon. The implicit argument is that a negotiated outcome, even an imperfect one, serves Ukraine's long-term interest better than a forever war.

## A different read

The word "constructive" is doing enormous diplomatic work in Zelensky's characterisation, and it is worth examining what that word has historically meant in the context of the Moscow-Kyiv relationship. In the Minsk process of 2014-2015, "constructive" talks produced agreements that Russia signed and immediately violated. The Kremlin's consistent pattern has been to use negotiating frameworks not as mechanisms for reaching durable settlements but as instruments for managing Western pressure, dividing European unity, and buying time for military consolidation. There is nothing in Vladimir Putin's strategic record to suggest this calculus has fundamentally changed.

The deeper structural problem is that the two sides' irreducible positions remain diametrically opposed. Ukraine cannot accept a settlement that ratifies Russian territorial gains under the threat of further aggression — doing so would simply reset the clock for a future attack, as Zelensky himself has argued for four years. Russia, having invested so heavily in the narrative that this is an existential war against NATO expansionism, cannot accept a settlement that looks like a defeat. These are not positions that split the difference easily. The agenda's focus on prisoner exchanges and humanitarian corridors is sensible as a confidence-building sequence, but it also risks creating an optical illusion of progress while the fundamental territorial questions fester.

Historical parallels are instructive here but not straightforwardly reassuring. The Korean Armistice of 1953 produced a durable ceasefire on the basis of a frozen conflict that neither side formally resolved — a model sometimes floated for Ukraine. But Korea's armistice was underwritten by the continued presence of American forces and a credible US security guarantee. The emerging Ukraine diplomatic framework involves European guarantors whose collective defence spending, with honourable exceptions, remains inadequate to backstop a serious deterrence commitment. If the talks produce a ceasefire without ironclad security guarantees, Ukraine would be locked in the most dangerous possible position: too weak to resist renewed aggression, too committed to the settlement to prepare for it.

The diplomatic timing also rewards scrutiny. Russia's decision to engage now, after three years of refusing multilateral talks, reflects a changed strategic calculation. The most plausible reading is that Russian forces have consolidated territorial control over most of the areas they currently hold, that Western military aid flows have slowed, and that the Trump administration has signalled a preference for ending the conflict — giving Moscow an opportunity to lock in gains through diplomacy that it could not achieve more completely through continued fighting. The talks are, in this reading, less a Russian concession than a Russian opportunity.

None of this means the talks should be abandoned. On the contrary: Ukraine's European supporters should engage seriously, push for maximum transparency about what Russia is actually prepared to concede, and use the negotiating period to accelerate rather than slow weapons deliveries — making clear that the cost of bad-faith negotiations is a better-armed Ukraine. The talks are a window, not a settlement.

## What to watch

Watch whether Russia agrees to any verification mechanism for a potential ceasefire — the absence of credible verification was the fatal flaw of Minsk I and Minsk II. Watch the position of Poland and the Baltic states, who have historically been the most sceptical of negotiated frameworks and whose buy-in would be essential for any European security guarantee to be credible. Watch whether the talks expand to include territorial questions in subsequent rounds, or remain confined to humanitarian issues indefinitely. And watch American domestic politics: a settlement that Trump can claim as a win before the midterm fallout fully crystallises is a powerful incentive for the administration to push Ukraine toward concessions it might otherwise resist.
