Today, four years after negotiations opened and a year after the agreement was signed, the [UK-India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10258/) enters into force. It is, by most measures, the most significant bilateral trade deal Britain has concluded since leaving the European Union. Indian whisky tariffs will fall from 150 per cent to 40 per cent by year ten; automotive duties will come down from 100 per cent to 10 per cent under quota. The UK government estimates a long-run GDP boost of [£4.8 billion](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/uk-india-trade-deal-worth-over-6-billion-start-july-15-2026-06-17/), and bilateral trade is projected to rise by £25.5 billion over time. The Commons Business and Trade Committee called it "the UK's most economically significant bilateral free trade agreement since leaving the European Union." That is an impressive headline. But the small print is where the story gets complicated.

## The received wisdom

The pro-deal consensus is enthusiastic, and understandably so. Here, finally, is evidence that post-Brexit "Global Britain" was not merely a slogan. India is the world's fifth-largest economy by some rankings, a democracy of 1.4 billion people, and a rising power whose strategic importance is self-evident. A free trade agreement with Delhi had eluded both the EU and the United States for decades, stymied by India's protectionist traditions and complex domestic politics. That Britain got it done — first across the line among major Western partners — is a genuine diplomatic achievement. Business organisations have broadly welcomed the deal. The Lords International Agreements Committee called it "a significant achievement." For a country that spent much of 2022 and 2023 being lectured that Brexit had made it irrelevant on the world stage, the deal provides a satisfying rebuttal. Scotch whisky producers, British carmakers, and professional services firms are all expected to see meaningful new market access over the next decade. The timing, too, is symbolically rich: the deal enters force just as global tariff turmoil triggered by Donald Trump's second-term trade war creates demand for stable, rule-based commercial relationships.

## A different read

And yet. Read the [House of Commons Library briefing](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10258/) carefully, and several qualifications accumulate. The tariff cuts on the Indian side are *phased*: it will be ten years before British exporters see the full benefit of reduced whisky or automotive tariffs. Meanwhile, Indian exporters gain more immediate access to the UK market in several key sectors. The asymmetry is not accidental — India's negotiators are formidable, and they secured the better timeline. The services provisions are, by general admission, "relatively limited." This matters enormously for Britain, whose economy is overwhelmingly services-based. Finance, law, education, and technology — the sectors where British firms hold genuine comparative advantage — did not receive the market-opening terms that goods exporters obtained. The Lords committee was polite but clear on this shortcoming.

There is also the question of what had to be swallowed to get the deal done. The implementation was nearly derailed in early 2026 by a dispute over Britain's new steel tariff regime, which threatened Indian steel exporters with sharp new duties. [The resolution](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/uk-india-trade-deal-worth-over-6-billion-start-july-15-2026-06-17/) involved reassurances that 85 per cent of India's steel exports would remain unaffected — a compromise that critics of British industrial policy noted somewhat undermined the protectionist rationale for the steel regime in the first place. These tensions are endemic to any serious bilateral deal: the concessions you grant are always more concrete and immediate than the benefits you receive.

None of this is to say the deal is bad. It is probably good. A £4.8 billion long-run GDP boost is real money, even if economists' projections for trade deals have a patchy historical record. More importantly, the relationship with India is strategically indispensable in a world where the Anglosphere is reorganising itself around democratic solidarity and supply-chain resilience. The deal sends a signal — to Beijing as much as to Delhi — that Britain is a serious interlocutor. The National Farmers' Union raised concerns about dairy sector exposure, and civil society groups pointed to gaps in labour rights and environmental provisions; these are legitimate concerns that deserve scrutiny in implementation. But the underlying proposition — deeper trade ties with a democratic, English-speaking, common-law-heritage partner in the world's most dynamic region — is sound.

The more important question the deal raises is one of domestic ambition. Britain's growth problem is not primarily a tariff problem. It is a planning problem, a skills problem, a capital investment problem. The Starmer government has spent considerable political energy on this FTA; it should spend at least as much energy ensuring that British firms are actually positioned to exploit the market access it creates. Trade deals open doors; they do not push you through them.

## What to watch

Watch whether Scotch whisky volumes to India meaningfully increase in the next two to three years — this will be the most legible early indicator of whether the deal is generating real commercial activity or remaining a paper achievement. Watch services: the limited provisions here will become a political sore point, and lobbying by the City and law firms for a supplementary services chapter is already under way. Watch the steel tensions, which were papered over rather than resolved, and could re-erupt if Indian steel volumes spike. And watch India's domestic politics — Modi's government has periodically used trade access as a foreign-policy lever, and what Delhi gives it can also take away.
