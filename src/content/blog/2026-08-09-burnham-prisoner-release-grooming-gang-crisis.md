Andy Burnham, in his first weeks as Prime Minister, has found that the prisons crisis inherited from his predecessor has become his [first significant political test](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cdewx3lpd2eo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). The row centres on a programme of early releases — ostensibly designed to manage prison overcrowding — that has included individuals convicted of serious offences, among them reportedly some convicted as part of grooming gang prosecutions. Burnham has [ordered a review of the grooming gang releases](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cg5lm3evpzzo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) specifically, having evidently concluded that the political and moral optics of this subset of the programme are untenable. An officer who led the investigation into the killing of PC Harper — another high-profile case — was reported to be [appalled](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9q9erpz953o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that killers from that case could also be eligible for early release.

## The received wisdom

The prison reformist case is real and should not be caricatured. England and Wales have among the highest incarceration rates in Western Europe. The prisons themselves are chronically overcrowded, under-resourced, and, by several independent metrics, producing more damaged people than they receive — running rates of violence, self-harm, and drug use that constitute, in the language of official inspectors, a "crisis." The argument for managed early release, with robust supervision, is not soft-heartedness; it is the view of most criminological evidence, which is that excessively long custodial sentences produce worse reoffending outcomes than community-based rehabilitation programmes.

The received wisdom would also note that prison overcrowding was an inheritance from years of "tough on crime" political competition — a bidding war in sentencing guidelines that successive Home Secretaries pursued to avoid being outflanked by the opposition — and that the consequences of that competition are now a structural problem the system cannot absorb. The reformers argue that early release, done properly, is simply the correction of that excess.

This case is not without merit. The evidence base for custodial rehabilitation is mixed at best.

## A different read

But governing is not an evidence seminar, and the distinction between "early release done properly" and "early release as it is actually implemented by an overstretched National Offender Management Service" is doing an enormous amount of work in the above argument.

The specific problem with the grooming gang releases is not that the principle of proportionate sentencing is wrong. It is that the victims of grooming gang crimes — overwhelmingly young, working-class women, predominantly from towns in the North and Midlands — have spent a decade and a half watching the justice system fail them at every stage: the police who didn't act, the social services who labelled them "lifestyle choices," the Crown Prosecution Service that was slow to prosecute, the courts that gave sentences that felt, and in some cases were, inadequate to the scale of the abuse. The early release of those convicted of those specific crimes into the communities where those victims still live is not an abstract policy question about recidivism statistics. It is a direct communication from the state to those women about how much the system values their suffering.

Burnham's order for a review is the correct immediate response. But it also exposes a contradiction in New Labour-inflected progressive governance that is worth naming. The same political tradition that correctly identified institutional racism in the police force as a structural failure finds it considerably harder to identify institutional class contempt as a structural failure when it manifests not in the treatment of minority communities but in the treatment of poor white communities in northern towns. The Alexis Jay reports, the Wood review, the Rotherham and Rochdale inquiries — the accumulated evidence is that the victims of these crimes were not taken seriously partly because of who they were and where they were from. The early release episode is a coda to that story, not a separate one.

The broader point for the Burnham premiership is about the relationship between progressive policy expertise and democratic legitimacy. The technocratic case for early release — constructed from recidivism data, overcrowding metrics, and comparative European data — is not wrong on its own terms. But democratic government is not administration. It requires that the people governed recognise themselves and their values in the decisions made on their behalf. A prison policy designed by criminologists and implemented by officials without adequate political oversight will produce exactly the kind of scandal that Burnham is now managing — precisely because the legitimate public stake in how the most serious offenders are punished was never adequately weighted against the managerial logic of capacity management.

This is the perennial tension in progressive governance: the instinct to trust the expert class, and the expert class's chronic underweighting of the values that are not easily expressed in policy models.

## What to watch

First: the scope of the Burnham review. Whether it is limited to grooming gang convictions or broadened to the entire early release programme will signal whether the government is managing a news cycle or addressing a systemic failure.

Second: the Clacton by-election, where [Reform and the Conservatives are competing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cdewx3lpd2eo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in part over exactly these themes. The early release scandal feeds directly into the electoral argument that neither major party has consistently prioritised public safety over managerial convenience. The result will be a gauge of how much political damage the row has done.

Third: Home Office implementation capacity. Even if the review concludes that certain releases were inappropriate, whether the government has the administrative capability to revoke licences and return individuals to custody without triggering further legal challenges is an operational question that will test bureaucratic competence as much as political will.

Fourth: the victims' organisations' response to the review. If they assess it as substantive, that is meaningful. If they assess it as cosmetic — the Burnham administration buying time — it will prolong and deepen the political problem.
