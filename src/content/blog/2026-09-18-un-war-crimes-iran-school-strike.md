A United Nations fact-finding mission has determined there are ["reasonable grounds"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/17/iran-school-bombing-un-mission-us-military-behind-attack) to believe the United States was responsible for military strikes in Iran in February that killed 156 civilians, including 120 children, at a primary school in Minab, Hormozgan province. The BBC [confirmed the report's release](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm9w4n5nverdo), noting that UN experts said there were grounds to believe the US committed war crimes in Iran, including in the school attack. NPR added that [the experts commissioned by the UN found "reasonable grounds"](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/17/nx-s1-5972934/us-iran-war-un-report-alleged-war-crimes) specifically regarding a strike on a school in the southern city of Minab. The Guardian reported that the school strike involved a US Tomahawk missile that collapsed the roof of the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school, which the UN said was ["clearly identifiable"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/17/iran-school-bombing-un-mission-us-military-behind-attack) as a civilian facility. Simultaneously, the BBC reported that the US has approved visas for [top Iranian leaders to attend the UN General Assembly](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6n8m72r13ngo) as the war passes the half-year mark. The diplomatic and legal situation around the Iran conflict is now entering a qualitatively different phase.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and internationalist case is straightforward: a UN body with human rights experts has documented specific strikes, identified the munition used (a Tomahawk cruise missile is not ambiguous about its national origin), and concluded that a clearly identifiable school was struck. International humanitarian law is explicit: civilian objects may not be targeted, and proportionality assessments must be made before each strike. When 120 children die in a building a UN mission describes as "clearly identifiable," the burden falls on those who conducted the strike to demonstrate either that the building was not what it appeared, or that there was a lawful military objective that was proportionate to the anticipated civilian harm. The fact-finding language — "reasonable grounds to believe" — is the standard threshold used by international bodies before a full judicial proceeding. It is not a casual accusation. For those who believe American power must be subject to the same rules it applies to others, this moment demands accountability.

## A different read

Let me be direct about what I think the serious right-of-centre response to this report should be — and what it should not be.

It should not be reflexive dismissal. The "UN is biased against the West" argument, however true in certain institutional contexts, does not address the specific factual claims here. A Tomahawk cruise missile has a known manufacturer and user. If it struck a school, that is a specific military event with forensic evidence. The UN fact-finding mission's conclusion deserves to be engaged with on the merits, not waved away because the Human Rights Council has a patchy institutional record on Israel or other flashpoints. Conservatives who correctly insist on rule-of-law standards in domestic governance cannot in good faith abandon those standards when American military power is under scrutiny.

That said, the responsible engagement with these findings requires acknowledging complexity that the most ardent critics elide. First, "reasonable grounds to believe" is a threshold designed for initiation of investigation, not conviction. It does not mean the full picture has emerged. Military targeting decisions involve intelligence assessments, time pressure, and classified information that a retrospective UN mission — operating after the fact, without access to US targeting files — cannot fully evaluate. The Vietnam-era My Lai massacre and the 1999 NATO bombing of the Chinese Embassy in Belgrade illustrate that both genuine war crimes and genuine intelligence failures can produce civilian mass casualties; distinguishing them requires access to actual targeting processes.

Second, and more uncomfortably for the war's critics, the broader strategic context matters for moral evaluation even if it does not negate individual incidents. [Russia and China vetoed a UN Security Council mandate to monitor Iran sanctions](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/17/russia-china-veto-un-mandate-to-monitor-iran-sanctions), demonstrating again that the adversaries of American power are not interested in multilateral accountability as a principle — only as a tool when it points at Washington. That asymmetry does not excuse what may have happened in Minab. But it should inform how we think about the institutional framework that produced this report, and who is using it for what purposes.

The genuinely conservative position here is not cheerleading and not performative contrition. It is this: the United States should investigate the Minab strike thoroughly and transparently, with results that can withstand scrutiny. If targeting errors occurred, they should be acknowledged and the procedures that produced them reformed. If the school was being used for military purposes — something the US would need to demonstrate with evidence — that information should be declassified to the extent possible. What must be resisted is the political incentive either to bury the investigation or to treat it as simply another front in partisan warfare. The credibility of American moral leadership — already severely strained — cannot survive a pattern in which accountability applies everywhere except to American conduct.

## What to watch

- The US government's formal response to the UN report: does it engage with the specific Tomahawk evidence, or offer only blanket denials?
- Whether the UN General Assembly session — which the US has now agreed to allow Iranian officials to attend — provides a diplomatic forum where the findings are formally raised.
- Congressional reactions: are there any members of either party willing to request a formal military Inspector General review of the Minab strike?
- Whether the "war crimes" determination affects ongoing US arms resupply discussions with Gulf partners who have provided basing for US operations.
