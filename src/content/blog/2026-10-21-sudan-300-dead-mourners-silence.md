[Sudanese army drones struck mourners gathered at a fuel market in El Fasher, killing more than 300 people](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr3el70jz1nqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), according to sources on the ground. A [separate drone strike near a market killed at least 80 people](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/19/drone-attack-market-al-dibab-west-kordofan-sudan) in West Kordofan province. The attacks are among the deadliest single incidents in a civil war that has displaced over twelve million people and killed tens of thousands since fighting between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces erupted in April 2023. Neither the United Nations Security Council nor any major Western government has convened an emergency session. The African Union has issued a statement. No sanctions have been proposed. Compare the global response to any comparable single incident in Europe or the Middle East, and the disproportion is not subtle.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive-humanitarian account attributes the muted international response to two factors: a combination of donor fatigue following the Ukraine and Gaza crises, and the structural marginalisation of African conflicts in Western media hierarchies. Both explanations are accurate as far as they go. Sudan sits outside the strategic calculus that animates Western foreign policy — it controls no major oil route, hosts no significant US bases, and is not proximate to Europe. The UN has struggled to maintain humanitarian access as both sides treat aid operations as military assets. Human rights organisations have consistently [documented the silence around Sudan's civil war](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr3el70jz1nqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) — reporting that this level of civilian killing would generate immediate international intervention if it occurred in Ukraine. There is a genuine racial and geographical hierarchy in Western empathy, and pretending otherwise is self-serving.

## A different read

The progressive framing, however diagnostically accurate, does not produce solutions because it focuses on Western guilt rather than local agency and external enablers. Sudan's army is using drones. Drones require supply chains, technical expertise, and financial backing. The primary external patrons of the SAF and the RSF — the UAE, Egypt, Russia, and increasingly Iran — are not mentioned in most Western press coverage because doing so would complicate the straightforward narrative of African conflict as orphan tragedy. [UAE support for the RSF in particular](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cr3el70jz1nqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) has been documented by UN experts, yet it generates no comparable pressure on Abu Dhabi to that which Ukraine's supporters place on Russia's arms suppliers.

This points to a deeper problem with the international humanitarian architecture. The framework built after the Cold War — R2P, the ICC, UN peacekeeping — was premised on the assumption that great powers could be embarrassed into action by public opinion and legal norms. That assumption has been refuted by Syria, Yemen, and now Sudan. The embarrassment threshold for permanent Security Council members has risen asymptotically. Russia and China will veto any binding resolution. The United States under the current administration has demonstrated [limited appetite for African engagement](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw203zx1x1rno?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), even exploring rapprochement with Myanmar's junta. The architecture produces statements, not outcomes.

There is also a question about what responsible external pressure would actually look like. Previous international interventions in Sudan — the Darfur peacekeeping mission, the CPA that created South Sudan — produced mixed results at enormous cost. The instinct of a conservative foreign policy realism is to be suspicious of interventions that substitute Western institutional presence for durable local political settlement. But that realism, if applied carelessly, becomes a rationalisation for abandonment. The 300 people killed at a fuel market in El Fasher on Monday were not abstractions in a strategic framework. They were people who went out to buy petrol and were killed from the air.

The minimum responsible position is sanctions targeting the specific commanders who authorised these drone strikes, export controls on drone technology reaching Sudan, and sustained diplomatic pressure on the UAE — not because these measures would end the war but because doing nothing is itself a policy choice with moral weight. It also sets a precedent: that drone strikes on civilian gatherings are acceptable if the country is poor, African, and strategically peripheral. That precedent will be noticed in every conflict zone where drones are available.

## What to watch

The critical marker is whether the UN Security Council convenes a session specifically on the El Fasher strikes. If it does not do so within a week, the silence becomes de facto authorisation. Watch for any movement in the UAE's posture following the strikes — Abu Dhabi has occasionally responded to international pressure when it calculates that reputational cost exceeds strategic benefit. The [US grants of deportation reprieve to Sudanese migrants](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/19/us-migrants-deportation-ukraine-sudan) announced last week suggest some residual awareness within the American executive of the humanitarian dimension; whether that translates into diplomatic pressure on Khartoum's backers is the test. If it does not, the war will continue, the death tolls will rise, and the next drone strike on a market will receive the same coverage as this one — a single news cycle, then silence.
