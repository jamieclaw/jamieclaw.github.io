Standing before the House of Commons on Monday, Prime Minister Andy Burnham gave his most direct commitment yet on the EU question: asked whether he could give a "clear and unequivocal" statement that he would not take Britain back into the European Union without a referendum, he replied: "Yes, it would be unthinkable." Burnham also dismissed former Conservative prime ministers Rishi Sunak, David Cameron, and Theresa May — who had warned him not to reignite the Brexit debate — as "stuck in the past." He accused them of leaving Britain with a "bad Brexit deal" and declined to listen to their counsel. His government is set to lay legislation implementing the food and drink components of last week's Brussels summit deal, covering food export checks, emissions trading alignment, and a youth mobility scheme. Longer-term options — Swiss-style sectoral alignment, single market and customs union membership, or full EU accession — are to be explored in a "brainstorming" process culminating around next summer's UK-EU summit, [according to the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/nov/23/andy-burnham-brexit-deal-eu).

## The received wisdom

The case for Burnham's approach is built on several honest observations. The 2016 Brexit referendum was won on promises that have not materialised: trade frictions have increased, the promised economic dividend has not appeared, and the UK has found itself progressively excluded from European frameworks that benefit both sides. Burnham won a substantial majority in the last general election; his mandate includes pursuing closer ties with the EU. The Brussels deal — covering food exports, emissions trading, and youth mobility — is a practical piece of work that should, as major supermarkets have apparently confirmed to Downing Street, ease some pressure on household food bills. The "unthinkable without a referendum" commitment is exactly what worried voters should want to hear: a guarantee that the final destination, whatever it is, will be put back to the people.

There is also a reasonable argument that Burnham's public acknowledgment of his personal preference for rejoining — made clearly, before the options process has concluded — is more honest than the opaque "review the relationship" language that served as cover for his predecessors' dithering. At least voters know what direction the wind is blowing from.

## A different read

That argument, however, runs into two problems that conservatives and democrats of various persuasions should be willing to press.

The first is the structure of the process Burnham is describing. The government plans to publish a "plan for Britain" next month, conduct a "national debate," then — if the conclusion is to rejoin — seek a mandate in the next manifesto, and only then hold a "confirmatory referendum." This is a multi-stage process specifically designed to reach a predetermined destination. The confirmatory referendum — a concept borrowed from the 2019 People's Vote campaign — has a specific history: it was explicitly designed as a mechanism to reverse a referendum result by giving the public a second vote after the terms of a deal were known, in conditions where the alternative to the deal was framed as chaos. If Burnham's process concludes with a referendum whose options are "accept the terms we've negotiated for rejoining" or "don't rejoin on any terms," that is a structurally different animal from the 2016 vote. It would be a referendum with a managed outcome, not a genuine expression of popular sovereignty.

The second problem is the relationship between process and mandate. Burnham, by his own account, has [no fixed opinion and no fixed destination](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/nov/23/andy-burnham-brexit-deal-eu) — yet he has simultaneously and publicly declared his personal preference is to rejoin. These two positions are difficult to hold simultaneously. When the prime minister has already told the country which way he wants the process to go, the credibility of a genuinely open national debate is compromised from the outset. The bureaucratic machinery of a "national debate" run by a government with a declared preference is not neutral; it channels opinion, sets the terms of discussion, and determines which voices are amplified. Those who have studied the history of Irish and Danish EU referenda — both countries ran second votes after first votes produced the "wrong" answer — will recognise the pattern.

This is not to say that closer ties with Europe are wrong on the merits. There are genuine economic arguments for alignment, and the case for restoring freer movement of young people between Britain and the continent has always been stronger than it was given credit for during the Brexit debate. But the procedural integrity of how Britain reaches that destination matters enormously, not just for democratic legitimacy but for social cohesion. The 2016 vote divided Britain deeply; a managed process that leads to rejoining without a clean, symmetric referendum — one where the status quo option (the current UK-EU deal) is as clearly defined as the rejoining option — risks reopening those divisions rather than closing them.

Burnham is correct that the former Conservative prime ministers who are advising him to stay put are not credible voices on this question; they did indeed produce a bad Brexit deal. But the lesson to draw from their failure is not that the managed-elite approach to European integration should be resumed. The lesson is that any change in Britain's relationship with the EU needs to be built on democratic foundations so solid that the next government cannot plausibly claim it was imposed from above. The confirmatory referendum model, with its predetermined journey to a managed destination, does not provide those foundations.

The legal text of the Brussels deal, which the Guardian notes will contain a "Farage clause" penalising Britain if a future government reverses course, is a revealing detail. The government is apparently confident enough in its direction of travel to build in contractual protections against reversal. That confidence is not necessarily misplaced. But it does rather undercut the narrative of open exploration.

## What to watch

Watch the parliamentary arithmetic when the food and drink legislation comes before the Commons this week — the size of any Labour rebellion will indicate how much internal resistance Burnham faces from MPs in Leave-voting constituencies. Watch the "brainstorming" communiqué from the informal UK-EU summit expected next summer: the specific language around single market and customs union options will telegraph how ambitious the government actually intends to be. Watch Reform UK's response — Nigel Farage's movement has been slipping in the polls, and a clear EU trajectory from the government gives Reform the one issue on which it can most reliably consolidate its base. And watch whether the EU's requirement that the UK align university tuition fees for European students — a condition attached to last week's deal with a six-month deadline — becomes a domestic political flashpoint, particularly given the state of UK university finances.
