Reform UK used its autumn conference to unveil the most ambitious fiscal programme any British party has published in a generation. The package, [reported by the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/czxz4dl1plwo), centres on £80 billion of public spending cuts — to be delivered through abolishing what the party calls bureaucratic waste — alongside a promise to [raise the tax-free personal allowance to £15,000](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cgk5g51xk57o). Leader Nigel Farage also unveiled a [small boats agreement with France's National Rally party](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c4gk35qnzp3o), pledging to use the Royal Navy to intercept Channel crossings and return migrants to France if Reform wins power. The conference came as the party absorbed the fallout from an [undercover sting operation that caught senior aides discussing access to foreign donors](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c86xj5z7x61o), with [Farage himself telling the BBC he "wasn't listening"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c0qxgn7q1vdo) during the conversations.

## The received wisdom

The media and political class consensus on Reform is, broadly, contemptuous. The party is read as a vehicle for Farage's ego, built on grievance rather than governance, attractive to voters who are angry but incapable of the serious policy work that holding office requires. The donor scandal fits the narrative neatly: a party that rails against establishment corruption turns out to have its own officials apparently offering access for foreign money. Internally, [party insiders are reportedly calling for a "shift in approach,"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cje87w9j342o) suggesting that even Farage's admirers recognise the operation is not yet ready for Whitehall. The £80bn cuts target, opponents argue, is a political confection — you cannot find that kind of saving without gutting the NHS, education, or the welfare state, none of which Reform has specified.

This critique has historical precedent behind it. Every insurgent populist party in British history has eventually collided with the administrative state's inertia. The SDP in the 1980s had more credentialled leadership than Reform and still couldn't crack the structural two-party dominance. UKIP at its peak polled brilliantly but won a single MP.

## A different read

And yet the mainstream dismissal is, in important respects, a mirror of the dismissiveness that attended Thatcherism in 1975. The conservative establishment found Thatcher's programme — privatisation, trade union reform, monetary discipline — too radical, too confrontational, insufficiently pragmatic. She was right about the substance even when she was wrong about the manner. The question worth asking about Reform is not whether its programme is palatably packaged, but whether the underlying diagnosis is correct.

On that test, the case is stronger than its critics allow. The UK public sector has expanded dramatically over the past decade without commensurate improvement in outcomes. The NHS waiting list peaked above seven million. Infrastructure delivery costs are among the highest in the developed world. A 2023 analysis by the Centre for Policy Studies estimated that administrative headcount in the civil service grew by around 25% between 2016 and 2023 while productivity flatlined. The proposition that significant savings are available through structural reform is not obviously wrong — it is uncomfortable, and politically very difficult to execute, but it is not innumerate.

The small boats agreement with Marine Le Pen's National Rally is more complicated. The optics of aligning with a party that carries substantial baggage from its historical far-right roots are genuinely problematic, and will be used against Farage relentlessly. But the policy substance — intercepting Channel crossings and returning migrants to France — is a version of what serious European governments including Denmark and Italy have implemented. [Andy Burnham's own government reached an agreement with Macron to "scale up action on small boats,"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c62ezg7gkzwo) suggesting that even the centre-left has concluded the status quo is untenable. Reform is pushing on a door that is already moving.

The donor scandal is more damaging than Farage's "loose pub talk" framing suggests. Not because foreign influence operations are exclusive to Reform — they manifestly are not — but because the party's entire brand proposition rests on being different from the venal, influence-peddling establishment. [Farage himself threatened to "overhaul Lords and judges"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyeq5jvd2po) to deliver his pledges, which is a constitutional red flag regardless of one's views on those institutions. A party that frames every obstacle to its programme as corrupt opposition, and that appears to have internal due diligence problems of its own, is not yet a governing party. It is an opposition party that has convinced itself it already governs.

## What to watch

Watch the party's polling following the donor story: if it holds above 25%, the scandal has not fundamentally altered its coalition. Watch whether Labour or the Conservatives are forced to respond to the £80bn figure with their own credible fiscal alternatives — the political danger of Reform is not that it wins power immediately but that it sets the terms of the debate. Watch the small boats numbers heading into autumn: if crossings fall, Burnham gets the credit; if they rise, Reform's pitch sharpens. And watch Farage's personal positioning — his comment that his family are "begging him to leave" politics is either a genuine signal of fatigue or a strategic bid for sympathy. He has used similar language before.
