The United States and the European Union are lurching toward a new confrontation — this time over the EU's long-running programme of fining American technology companies. President Trump has [vowed to investigate the European Union](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvgjenp4680o) over its penalties against US tech firms, framing the fines as unfair targeting of American businesses. The move comes alongside a [broader new wave of US tariffs hitting dozens of countries](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvgj61j6l08o), suggesting Washington is not treating the tech dispute as a narrow regulatory matter but as part of a wider reassessment of economic relations with nominal allies. The EU's fines against companies including Google, Apple, Meta, and Amazon have run into the hundreds of billions of dollars over the past decade, and Brussels has defended them as legitimate competition enforcement.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream view — articulated consistently by European regulators, most American centre-left commentators, and the tech-sceptical wing of the Democratic Party — holds that the EU's Digital Markets Act and related competition probes are simply the rule of law in action. The argument is that Silicon Valley monopolies have captured regulators in their home country, and that Europe, possessing both the political will and the legal architecture, has done the world a favour by doing what Washington refused to. The fines, in this reading, are a feature of open liberal governance, not a bug. Trump's threatened investigation, by contrast, is cast as crude protectionism dressed in the language of reciprocity — the kind of mercantilist instinct that has no place in a rules-based trading order that the US itself helped construct.

There is something to this. European competition enforcement is not nakedly protectionist; its legal reasoning is often careful, and several of the underlying competition concerns — abuse of dominance in app stores, self-preferencing in search — are real. The EU is not, in the formal sense, discriminating against American firms because they are American.

## A different read

But the mainstream framing papers over some inconvenient arithmetic. Of the European Commission's ten largest antitrust fines in history, the [vast majority](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvgjenp4680o) have gone to American companies. Europe has not fined a single major Chinese technology firm at comparable scale. The EU's enforcement calendar has an asymmetry that goes beyond coincidence: it is consistently vigorous against US platforms and substantially more cautious about Chinese or European incumbents.

This pattern has a structural explanation that has nothing to do with malice. European tech firms of comparable market power simply don't exist in most of these sectors. And Chinese firms — operating behind the Great Firewall and largely absent from European consumer markets — present fewer jurisdictional targets. But the effect, whatever the intent, is that Brussels has constructed a regulatory apparatus whose costs fall almost entirely on American shareholders and, ultimately, American consumers through higher prices passed through by the platforms.

Niall Ferguson has argued for years that the transatlantic relationship contains a fiscal free-rider problem — Europe spending less on defence and extracting more from American-led institutions while insisting on the moral high ground. The tech fine regime is a close cousin of that dynamic. The EU extracts revenue from companies that built their scale primarily in the American market, funds European regulators and courts with the proceeds, and then lectures Washington about the sanctity of the rules-based order when the US objects.

Trump's instinct here, characteristically blunt and characteristically aimed at a real grievance, is that the existing arrangement isn't neutral — it's a transfer. His threatened investigation into whether EU fines constitute an unfair trade practice is legally novel and probably won't produce a clean WTO ruling in America's favour. But the political argument is not frivolous. When the [new tariff wave hit dozens of countries simultaneously](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvgj61j6l08o), the White House signalled it views regulatory barriers and digital fines as tariff-equivalent. That framing will be contested — but it has already shifted the negotiating floor.

What the EU should fear most is not losing a legal argument but losing a political one. The Trump administration has demonstrated a willingness to run parallel tracks: threatening tariffs while dangling exemptions, as with the [Scotch whisky levy](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c7vgz680977o) that was reportedly lifted as part of the wider tariff round. Brussels now faces a choice between defending its regulatory autonomy at the cost of a trade confrontation, or quietly adjusting its enforcement posture in ways it cannot publicly acknowledge. Neither option is comfortable. The first risks genuine economic damage — the EU is still working through the downstream effects of post-Covid supply chain disruptions and cannot easily absorb an escalating tariff war with its largest export partner. The second would corrode the credibility of its competition regime, which is the EU's most powerful projection of soft power.

The deeper question is whether the EU's tech enforcement model was ever as principled as advertised, or whether it was always partly a revenue mechanism and partly an industrial policy dressed in competition language — a way of disadvantaging companies that Europe's own tech sector could not outcompete. Trump's challenge, however clumsy, puts that question on the table in a way that polite transatlantic summitry never did.

## What to watch

- Whether the EU accelerates any pending settlements or fine negotiations with US tech companies ahead of formal US investigation proceedings — that would signal Brussels is willing to blink.
- The WTO dispute mechanism: if the US formally files, watch whether other countries with similar grievances — particularly in the Indo-Pacific — line up behind Washington rather than Brussels.
- The UK angle: as a post-Brexit regulator with its own Digital Markets Unit now active, London will be watching carefully whether aligning with the EU on tech enforcement invites US retaliation against British firms.
- Oil prices: at [$100 a barrel](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2djnzrqk2o), every escalation in transatlantic trade tension carries a macroeconomic multiplier that neither side can easily absorb right now.
