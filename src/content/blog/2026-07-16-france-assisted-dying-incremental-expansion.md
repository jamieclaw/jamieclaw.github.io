France's National Assembly approved an assisted dying bill on Wednesday, [with parliament adopting the text by 291 votes to 241](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/15/french-parliament-approves-landmark-assisted-dying-bill). The law is among the most restrictive frameworks to pass in any Western democracy: it would allow assisted dying only for terminally ill adults who meet strict criteria, with multiple layers of medical and judicial review required before any procedure. The bill still requires approval from the Constitutional Council before entering force. [BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg7g2z4pv3o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) characterised the vote as the culmination of "years of argument" in a country with a predominantly Catholic heritage, a large medical profession traditionally aligned with the Hippocratic tradition of not hastening death, and a public that polls have consistently shown is more supportive of change than the political class has been willing to countenance.

## The received wisdom

The progressive framing of France's vote is compassionate and reasonably compelling: people facing terminal illness and severe, irremediable suffering should have the right to choose the timing and manner of their own death. Autonomy — the philosophical cornerstone of liberal modernity — argues that the state has no legitimate interest in compelling people to remain alive against their will when they are dying anyway. The criteria in the French bill are strict precisely to allay fears of abuse: only the terminally ill, only with capacity to consent, only with multiple sign-offs. The Netherlands and Belgium have had assisted dying for over two decades; Canada's MAID programme has been operating since 2016. The sky has not fallen. If anything, the evidence from these jurisdictions suggests that palliative care investment improves alongside assisted dying access, rather than being crowded out.

This case deserves to be taken seriously. There is something incoherent about a liberal state that prides itself on personal freedom but refuses to recognise autonomy at the end of life.

## A different read

The problem with strict assisted dying frameworks is not that they are insincere at passage — they generally are not. The problem is the trajectory. Every jurisdiction that has introduced assisted dying legislation under "strict" criteria has subsequently expanded eligibility over time. The original Dutch Termination of Life on Request Act was, at passage, described as a tightly bounded last resort for the terminally ill with unbearable suffering. By 2023, the Netherlands had extended MAID to children and was debating eligibility for dementia patients without immediate terminal prognosis. Canada's MAID programme, introduced in 2016 for natural death that was "reasonably foreseeable," had by 2021 removed that requirement and was debating extension to mental illness.

The pattern is consistent enough to have a name in bioethics literature: incremental expansion. The logic is internally coherent — once you accept the principle that suffering, not imminent death, is the threshold, there is no philosophically stable stopping point short of any serious, irremediable suffering. The "strict criteria" become a staging post, not a destination.

France's Constitutional Council review is worth watching for exactly this reason. The current bill's terminal illness requirement is defensible under existing constitutional principles; but France's constitutional jurisprudence on autonomy rights has been expanding for years, following a European human rights logic that increasingly finds restrictions on personal medical choices hard to justify. Once the law is in force and cases accumulate, the courts will face the same incremental pressure that has driven expansion elsewhere.

None of this means the French vote was wrong or that the bill's supporters are acting in bad faith. But the right-of-centre concern — one rooted not in religious dogma but in institutional realism — is that democratic legislatures tend to set up frameworks they believe are tightly bounded, then find that the logic of those frameworks pulls against the bounds. Palliative care remains underfunded in France relative to need. A system where assisted death is available but quality end-of-life care is inaccessible because of cost or geography does not represent genuine autonomy. It represents a false choice presented under the language of freedom. The historical record of welfare states finding it cheaper to offer assisted death than to fund adequate care is not speculative — it is already visible in Canada, where disability advocates have documented cases of patients citing inability to access care rather than terminal illness as their primary reason for seeking MAID.

France has made its democratic choice. The bill's strictness relative to neighbouring jurisdictions deserves acknowledgement. But European societies should watch the incremental expansion dynamic with clear eyes, and invest accordingly in the alternative: a palliative care system serious enough that assisted dying is always a genuine last resort, not a cost-efficient default.

## What to watch

Watch whether France's Constitutional Council upholds the bill, amends it to strengthen safeguards, or finds procedural grounds to return it to parliament. Watch how the French medical establishment responds — a significant portion of physicians remain opposed, and their cooperation is essential to any workable implementation. Watch whether the Belgian or Dutch expansion patterns appear in French parliamentary debate within a decade. And watch how the bill's passage affects Catholic and conservative political mobilisation in France's next legislative cycle, given the right's fragmentation since Macron's coalition politics reshaped the centre.
