Scott Colom became the first Black senator elected from Mississippi since John Roy Lynch left the chamber in 1883 — a gap of 143 years that is itself a commentary on the state's history. [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/11/g-s1-152954/russia-ukraine-war-talks) the race as the closest in the country, and [the Guardian confirmed](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/11/scott-colom-mississippi-senate-race) that Colom unseated the Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith in what the paper called a "shocking" win. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/democrat-scott-colom-wins-closely-watched-us-senate-race-in-mississippi?traffic_source=rss) and [the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw7vppyr80eeo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) both noted that Colom ran on a prosecutorial anti-crime platform, had previously won election as a district attorney, and had conspicuously avoided the national Democratic Party's messaging on immigration and social issues. He is a practical, district-level politician who talked about roads, rural hospitals, and public safety. He is, in other words, almost nothing like the progressive movement currently jostling for the Democratic Party's ideological soul.

## The received wisdom

The dominant narrative from Democratic media this week treats Colom's victory as part of a broader repudiation of Trumpism — a "blue wave" that [the Guardian's analysts described](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/11/is-the-us-blue-wave-a-turning-point-for-europe-too) as a possible turning point not just for American politics but for the global centre-left. On this reading, the midterms were a verdict on Republican immigration excesses, the Hegseth impeachment spectacle, and the fiscal recklessness of tax cuts paired with defence spending. The fact that a Democrat won in Mississippi — Mississippi! — is presented as evidence that the party has achieved something close to a national majority coalition. [NPR noted](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/11/nx-s1-6037432/whats-behind-democratic-socialists-historic-victories-in-the-midterms) that democratic socialists also scored historic wins in several urban districts, and [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/zohran-mamdanis-influence-grows-as-democrats-eye-2028-strategies?traffic_source=rss) that Zohran Mamdani's growing influence is reshaping how Democrats think about 2028. On this account, the coalition that produced Colom's win and the coalition that produced Mamdani's ascent are the same coalition — a diverse, progressive, energised majority.

They are not.

## A different read

The tension at the heart of the Democratic Party's current moment is the same tension that has plagued every broad electoral coalition in American history: winning requires coalitions, coalitions require internal contradiction, and internal contradiction eventually produces either a governing ideology or a civil war. The Democratic midterm victories are genuine, but the party is already engaged in a competition to misinterpret them.

Scott Colom won Mississippi — a state that has voted Republican in every presidential election since 1980 — by running away from his national party's brand. He did not campaign on student debt forgiveness, open borders, or the Green New Deal. He campaigned on locking up murderers and fixing highways. The swing voters who made his margin — working-class white Mississippians in small counties, and Black voters in the Delta who have grown frustrated with both parties — were not sending a message about democratic socialism. They were sending a message about public order and competent government. [The BBC's description of his "surprise victory"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw7vppyr80eeo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) rather undersells the degree to which Colom ran as a conservative Democrat in all but name.

The historical parallel here is the 1974 and 1976 Democratic landslides, when post-Watergate revulsion swept in a large class of what came to be called "Watergate babies" — moderate, district-focused Democrats who won in reliably Republican territory. That class was quickly captured by the national party's ideological apparatus, forced to vote for priorities that alienated their constituents, and largely wiped out in 1980. The lesson was forgotten by 2010, when the Obama-era Blue Dogs were sacrificed to pass the Affordable Care Act and subsequently decimated in the Tea Party wave.

The Mamdani wing of the party, which is now loudly claiming the midterms as a mandate for democratic socialism, won in Brooklyn and the Bronx, not in Mississippi. The seats that flipped the chamber — in suburban Pennsylvania, in coastal Georgia, in Colom's Mississippi — were won by candidates who sound nothing like Mamdani. If the party's apparatchiks force those candidates to vote for policies designed to satisfy New York City's activist base, they will engineer their own next rout.

Colom's win is a genuine and historically resonant achievement. The 143-year gap it closes speaks to something real about American racial history and its halting repair. But as a political signal, it points away from the left's preferred narrative, not toward it. The Democrats' most durable gains have always come when they sounded less like a faculty meeting and more like a county sheriff.

## What to watch

- **Committee assignments**: Whether Colom is placed on committees relevant to Mississippi — agriculture, rural development, judiciary — or is expected to sit on prestige committees that serve the party's national brand will signal whether Washington has learned anything.
- **The Mamdani-Colom tension**: Watch whether the progressive caucus attempts to enforce ideological discipline on the new class of moderate members; the first test will likely come on immigration legislation.
- **Hyde-Smith's response**: Whether she mounts a 2028 challenge and how she frames it will calibrate Republican strategy in the Deep South.
- **Democratic primary dynamics**: If Colom votes against progressive priorities, he will face a primary challenger. How the party machine responds to that challenge will reveal whether it has learned to protect its most valuable swing-state members.
