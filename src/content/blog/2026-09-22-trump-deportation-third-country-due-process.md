A major investigative report by [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2026/sep/21/trump-deportation-deals) has documented what it describes as a secret network of deportation agreements between the Trump administration and a range of countries — including some with documented human rights records — to receive migrants expelled from the United States without formal immigration hearings. Separately, lawyers for a Venezuelan national reported that their client was [shot by ICE agents in Austin, Texas](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/21/ice-shooting-austin-texas-venezuelan-man), with the bullet remaining lodged in his back as he sought medical treatment. [Men deported to Equatorial Guinea](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/18/us-men-deported-hotel-equatorial-guinea) described being held in a hotel and subjected to beatings upon arrival. These are not isolated incidents; they form a pattern of executive action on immigration enforcement that is systematically bypassing the legal structures that govern removal proceedings in the United States.

## The received wisdom

The progressive and civil liberties critique here is the correct starting point, and it should be stated in full. The United States has an elaborate legal architecture governing deportation: migrants are entitled to a hearing before an immigration judge, the right to legal representation, access to asylum claims processes, and appeal rights. These are not bureaucratic niceties; they derive from the Fifth Amendment's due process guarantee, which applies to all persons on US soil regardless of status. The administration's use of third-country deportation deals — shipping people to nations they have no connection to — appears designed specifically to circumvent these protections by removing individuals before courts can act. If the Guardian's reporting is accurate, this is not vigorous enforcement of existing law. It is the extrajudicial removal of human beings from the jurisdiction of courts that have authority over their cases. That is a constitutional crisis, not a policy dispute.

## A different read

None of that is wrong. And yet the full picture requires acknowledging something that the civil liberties framing tends to elide: the immigration enforcement system that the Trump administration is circumventing was itself, in significant respects, broken long before this administration arrived.

The immigration court backlog in the United States stands at well over three million cases. Wait times for hearings routinely exceed five years. The asylum system has been, by any honest assessment, overwhelmed — both by genuine refugees and by a large cohort of applicants who have learned that filing a claim, regardless of merit, buys years of legal residence. The legal architecture that civil libertarians invoke was not functioning as designed; it had become, in practice, a mechanism that made permanent a large population in legal limbo. That is not a justification for extrajudicial removal — it is a description of the political failure that created the conditions for it.

The Trump administration's response to this failure is itself a failure, but of a different and more dangerous kind. The deportation archipelago described by [the Guardian investigation](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2026/sep/21/trump-deportation-deals) — deals with Equatorial Guinea, with states that have no legal or cultural connection to the deportees — resembles nothing so much as the Australian government's offshore processing scheme in Nauru and Manus Island. That scheme was widely condemned by human rights organisations and eventually produced a series of appalling documented abuses, including deaths in custody. Australia's High Court found aspects of it constitutionally questionable, though it survived legal challenge on narrow grounds. The US version lacks even the limited parliamentary scrutiny that constrained the Australian model.

What distinguishes a legitimate hardline immigration policy from what is being described here is procedural. A government can set high bars for asylum. It can expedite removals for those who clearly do not qualify. It can negotiate safe-third-country agreements — but those agreements have legal requirements, including that the receiving country provides genuine protection. Shipping people to Equatorial Guinea, a country ranked among the world's most repressive, does not meet that standard by any interpretation.

The [ICE shooting in Austin](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/21/ice-shooting-austin-texas-venezuelan-man) raises additional questions about the operational culture within enforcement agencies that have seen a rapid expansion of authority and mandate under the current administration. Expanding any law enforcement agency quickly, with reduced oversight and a political mandate for aggression, is a recipe for exactly the kind of incident that the Austin case represents. This is not a critique unique to immigration enforcement; it applies to any rapid militarisation of civilian policing functions.

Conservative advocates of rigorous immigration enforcement — and there are serious ones, whose arguments deserve engagement rather than dismissal — have always distinguished between enforcement of law and the abandonment of law. The rule of law is not an obstacle to immigration control; it is what makes immigration control legitimate. A government that deports people by fiat, to countries they have no connection with, without hearings, and with physical abuse en route, is not enforcing the law. It is replacing law with administrative power. That is a precedent with implications that extend far beyond immigration.

## What to watch

- Federal court responses: multiple emergency injunctions have been filed. Whether the Supreme Court is willing to put limits on third-country deportation deals will define the legal landscape for years.
- Congressional oversight: Republican legislators who supported enforcement measures but not extrajudicial removal will face pressure to either investigate or acquiesce, with the latter setting a damaging precedent for executive power.
- The diplomatic fallout: countries that agreed to receive deportees under pressure from Washington will face their own domestic politics. If the Equatorial Guinea arrangement becomes a public scandal internationally, the administration's ability to extend similar deals will be constrained.
- Journalistic follow-up: the Guardian investigation is based on documents and lawyer testimony. If the full scope of the deportation archipelago is mapped, the political consequences will depend entirely on whether the mainstream US media picks up and sustains the story.
