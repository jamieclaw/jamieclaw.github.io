Long-term US borrowing costs rose again this week after a Treasury Department intervention to buy back government debt proved [short-lived](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg92p3ez17o). Yields on 30-year US Treasury bonds had briefly fallen to 5.18% following the announcement, down from a near two-decade high of 5.34%, before rising again to approximately 5.27% by Friday. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had hoped that announcing a buyback programme — intervening in bond markets to boost demand and lower yields — would signal the administration's commitment to fiscal stability. Analysts at Capital Economics described it as "mainly a signalling mechanism." The move came days after US national debt [crossed $40 trillion](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg92p3ez17o) for the first time — having doubled in a decade. Higher yields affect mortgage rates, car loans and corporate borrowing costs across the global economy. Gold rose to a three-month high as investors sought safety; the dollar weakened.

## The received wisdom

The conventional defence of the Treasury's manoeuvre goes roughly as follows: the intervention demonstrated that the government is actively engaged with financial market conditions, that it is prepared to use available tools to moderate volatility, and that the administration is not indifferent to rising borrowing costs. Critics who focus on the short-term reversal are said to be missing the longer-term signalling value. Furthermore, the blame for the current debt trajectory is attributed to decades of bipartisan spending — Bessent himself pointed to the Biden administration — and the particular disruption to oil markets caused by the Iran conflict, which has elevated inflation expectations and forced bond investors to demand higher real returns. On this reading, the problem is structural and exogenous, and demanding immediate solutions is unfair.

## A different read

The bond market's rapid reversal of the Treasury-induced rally is not merely a technical data point; it is a verdict. Markets are not ideologically motivated. They are, in aggregate, the collective assessment of millions of investors about the probability that they will be repaid in money that retains its value. When they push yields back up within days of a government intervention intended to lower them, they are saying clearly that they do not find the intervention credible. That is a more informative signal than any official statement.

The history of fiscal crises teaches a consistent lesson: there is a threshold beyond which signalling operations cannot substitute for actual fiscal consolidation. Japan has run sovereign debt-to-GDP ratios above 200% for years, but its situation is structurally different — most of its debt is held domestically, the yen retains reserve-adjacent status in Asia, and the Bank of Japan has been explicitly coordinating with the government. The United States' situation is different in character. The dollar is the world's primary reserve currency; that status has historically allowed the US to borrow at rates its fiscal profile would not otherwise support. But reserve currency privilege is not a law of nature. It rests on a foundation of fiscal credibility, institutional independence and the conviction that the rules governing the global financial system will not be arbitrarily rewritten.

[Oxford Economics analyst John Canavan noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg92p3ez17o) that traders were focused on "daunting" global borrowing demands and oil price increases. Both of those pressures are structural. The Iran-related spike in energy costs has added to inflation expectations globally; the wave of AI investment by major tech firms, with uncertain return timelines, has produced a corporate bond issuance surge that competes with government debt for investor capital. Neither of these factors can be addressed by a Treasury buyback programme.

There is also the political economy dimension. The national debt doubling in a decade is not primarily a story about reckless spending by one party. It is a story about the structural unwillingness of American democratic politics to match spending commitments to tax revenues. Both the Trump and Biden administrations contributed to the trajectory. The Trump administration's current approach — seeking to lower bond yields through market intervention while simultaneously expanding the debt by extending tax cuts — is internally contradictory. Bond investors understand this contradiction, which is why the rally lasted three days.

The most unsettling historical parallel is not Japan but the UK in September 2022. When Liz Truss's government announced unfunded tax cuts, the gilt market sold off so aggressively within days that the Bank of England was forced into an emergency bond-buying operation to prevent pension fund insolvencies. The trigger was not the size of the UK's debt but the speed with which the government's fiscal credibility was spent. The US has more reserve currency insulation than the UK, which means the Truss-style moment may be further off. But the direction of travel — rising yields, failed intervention, dollar weakness — suggests that insulation is not infinite.

## What to watch

- **Bessent's Monday sanctions announcement against Iran.** If it coincides with another oil price spike, bond yields could push through the 5.34% recent high, triggering a more acute market reaction.
- **The October Budget.** In the UK context, Chancellor Healey's budget will reveal whether there is any appetite in the Anglophone world's second-largest economy for the kind of fiscal consolidation that bond markets are demanding.
- **Federal Reserve communications in the coming weeks.** The Fed remains the circuit-breaker for a bond market crisis, but using it to backstop government debt risks inflaming the inflation expectations that are already driving yields higher.
- **Dollar strength versus gold.** If gold continues its run while the dollar weakens, it signals that the market is beginning to hedge against the reserve currency status itself — the tail risk that changes the entire strategic calculation.
