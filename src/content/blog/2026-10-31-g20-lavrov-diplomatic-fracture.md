The G20 foreign ministers' summit in Atlanta ended without its traditional group photograph after European delegations [refused to stand beside Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/30/g20-drops-group-photo-as-europeans-refuse-to-stand-with-lavrov), who is personally sanctioned by the European Union, the United Kingdom, and several allied governments. UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband [confronted Lavrov directly](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmd6dp2y576do), publicly accusing the Russian government of killing innocent civilians in Ukraine. The meeting, hosted by US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, was already freighted with tensions stemming from the ongoing Iran-Hormuz crisis and a sharpening divide between the Global South members and the Western bloc over Ukraine sanctions policy. The photograph incident, minor in itself, crystallised the deeper reality: the G20 format, designed for an era of cooperative globalisation, is now a venue where adversaries meet to register disagreement rather than build consensus.

## The received wisdom

The liberal internationalist view is that multilateral forums, however strained, remain valuable precisely because they keep adversaries talking. The alternative — a world of hermetically sealed blocs with no diplomatic contact — is demonstrably more dangerous. Even during the Cold War, US and Soviet foreign ministers met regularly, sometimes productively. The G20 was designed after the 1997-98 Asian financial crisis to include emerging economies in the management of global economic governance, and that logic remains valid: a world economy that includes China, India, Brazil, and Indonesia cannot be managed by a club that excludes them. The photo incident, on this reading, is regrettable theatre — a symbolic humiliation that damages the West's standing in the Global South by making it look petty — while the real work of G20 cooperation on debt restructuring, climate finance, and trade rules continues quietly in working groups.

This argument has genuine merit. The G20 has produced real deliverables on tax information exchange, pandemic finance, and debt relief under the Common Framework. Refusing to photograph beside Lavrov while continuing to sit in the same room is, at best, inconsistent symbolism and, at worst, the kind of performative moral grandstanding that impresses editorial boards while doing nothing to advance the actual coercive pressure on Russia.

## A different read

And yet. There is a point at which the insistence on maintaining multilateral forms becomes not constructive engagement but a subsidy to the revisionist powers gaming the system. The Soviet Union never attended G20 meetings because there was no G20; but the broader Cold War experience of détente-era multilateral diplomacy is instructive. [Lavrov's continued presence at international forums](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmd6dp2y576do) — as a personally sanctioned individual representing a government conducting what the International Criminal Court has characterised as war crimes — tests whether Western sanctions have any meaning at all outside the financial system. If a man the European Union has legally prohibited from entering EU territory can attend a G20 hosted in Atlanta with full diplomatic honours, the sanctions are performing, not functioning.

The deeper problem is what the format itself now represents. The G20 was designed on a theory of convergence: that as emerging economies grew richer and more integrated into the global trading system, their governments would gradually internalise the norms of the liberal order — rule of law, property rights, open capital accounts, peaceful dispute resolution. That theory, which had real intellectual force in the 1990s, has not survived contact with the 2020s. China has grown enormously wealthy while becoming more authoritarian under Xi. Russia used a decade of G8 and G20 participation to restore its military power before invading a neighbour. The multilateral forum did not transform these states; it gave them time, legitimacy, and market access while they pursued their own strategic objectives.

Henry Kissinger, who was not given to sentiment about international institutions, understood that balance-of-power management required acknowledging that adversaries had interests and that accommodation was sometimes preferable to confrontation. But Kissinger also understood that there was a difference between realistic diplomacy and wishful theatre. The G20 photograph incident is wishful theatre's curtain call: the Europeans cannot stomach the image of standing beside Lavrov, but they remain inside the format that gives his presence legitimacy. They have achieved the worst of both positions — moral discomfort without strategic consequence.

The Global South's reaction will matter here. India, Brazil, South Africa, and Indonesia have all declined to support Western-led Ukraine sanctions. For those governments, the photograph incident looks like European petulance rather than principled statecraft. Every time the Western bloc performs disapproval without translating it into effective policy, it weakens the West's credibility in the forums where the real post-American order will eventually be negotiated. [Canada and the EU's announcement of an "Alliance for the Future"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/30/canada-european-union-alliance) at a parallel summit in Montreal, explicitly designed as a vehicle for like-minded democracies, is the honest admission that the G20 has ceased to function as a governance body and is now simply a venue for managed antagonism.

## What to watch

- **Global South positioning:** Watch how India and Brazil characterise the Atlanta meeting in their domestic coverage. If the photograph incident features as Western arrogance, that tells us more about the state of the multilateral order than any joint communiqué.
- **The "Alliance for the Future" membership:** Canada and the EU have left the alliance open to other countries. Which G20 members apply — and which conspicuously do not — will define the contours of the emerging democratic club that may eventually replace G20 consensus as the primary venue for Western economic coordination.
- **Rubio's framing:** US Secretary of State Rubio's call for G20 "unity against threats to the economic order" while hosting Lavrov reveals a tension at the heart of the Trump administration's diplomacy — tough on rhetoric, ambivalent on enforcement. Watch whether Washington moves to restrict future Lavrov travel or accepts the status quo.
- **Ukraine aid momentum:** The backdrop to Atlanta is a US Congress still debating Patriot missile financing. European frustration at sharing a room with Lavrov will only intensify if American military aid packages falter heading into the midterms.
