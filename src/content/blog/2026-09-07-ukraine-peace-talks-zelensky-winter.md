US special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner travelled to Kyiv on Sunday for their first visit to Ukraine since beginning shuttle diplomacy between Washington and Moscow, meeting President Volodymyr Zelensky a day after lengthy talks with Vladimir Putin in the Russian capital. [Zelensky told reporters](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6248nvqy8po) he expected the war to continue into the winter and called for European countries to be included in any formal trilateral negotiating track. Witkoff described the conversations as "very meaningful" and said trilateral talks involving the US, Ukraine, and Europe could be announced "in short order." Analysts noted that [peace talks remained rich in symbolism but that fundamental differences between Russia and Ukraine persist](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c980j5m8mpro), with no indication of what a territorial compromise might look like or how it could be verified. The diplomatic choreography has intensified even as battlefield conditions remain stalemated across most of the front.

## The received wisdom

The optimistic read — shared by the White House and most of the European centre-left — is that the mere fact of Witkoff and Kushner visiting both Moscow and Kyiv in the same week represents genuine diplomatic progress. The argument is that after years of frozen contact, having a credible US interlocutor shuttle between the two capitals creates the conditions for a negotiated end to a war that has already consumed hundreds of thousands of lives and is devastating the Ukrainian economy. Scepticism, on this view, is defeatist. Every ceasefire in modern history began with talks that initially seemed futile; the Oslo process, the Good Friday Agreement, the Dayton Accords — all emerged from moments of apparent impasse. Zelensky's call for European inclusion is a sign that Ukraine is engaging pragmatically rather than rigidly insisting on maximum demands. The process is real; the outcome may take time.

## A different read

There is a telling phrase in Zelensky's public statement after the Kyiv meeting: he expects the war to continue "into the winter." This is a man who has been briefed on what Witkoff and Kushner actually obtained from Putin in Moscow the day before, and his assessment is sobering. Not "a deal is close" or "we have a framework." Into the winter.

The structure of this diplomatic initiative contains several familiar failure modes. First, consider the asymmetry of incentives. Russia is not under significant military pressure on the front; it is advancing slowly but consistently in Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia, and its economy, while stressed, has adjusted to wartime conditions. Putin has no urgent reason to accept terms that require withdrawal from occupied Ukrainian territory. The [shuttle diplomacy](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/06/nx-s1-5959657/us-envoys-witkoff-kushner-talks-in-kyiv-putin-moscow) Washington is conducting is not backed by any new military commitment to Ukraine, nor by any announced increase in weapons deliveries. Diplomacy without leverage is generally not diplomacy — it is the appearance of diplomacy.

Second, the domestic political logic in Washington encourages the performance of progress over its substance. The Trump administration wants to be seen to be ending wars; it has made this a central reputational claim. Witkoff and Kushner's visits generate photographs and quotable optimism regardless of whether any substantive concession has been obtained from Moscow. The [BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c980j5m8mpro) that fundamental differences remain on territorial integrity, the legal status of occupied regions, and security guarantees for Ukraine's future. These are not minor procedural gaps to be bridged by goodwill — they are incompatible maximalist positions on both sides.

Third, and perhaps most consequentially, Zelensky's call for European inclusion reveals a strategic imperative that cuts against Washington's model. The Trump administration has been conducting this process bilaterally, as if a Ukraine settlement were an American-Russian matter in which Europeans are consultees rather than parties. Europe's security is directly affected by the war's outcome; any settlement that freezes Russian territorial gains in Ukraine would reshape NATO's eastern flank permanently. The fact that Zelensky is insisting on European involvement is not incidental diplomatic housekeeping — it is his attempt to ensure that a deal not be imposed over European and Ukrainian objections by two great powers in a room. That the US envoys apparently agree "in short order" may indicate they are simply acquiescing to the request for optics without it changing the bilateral dynamic.

The Dayton comparison, which optimists often invoke, is worth examining carefully. Dayton worked partly because the US used significant military coercion — NATO air strikes — to force Milošević to the table, and because all parties faced genuine battlefield exhaustion. The present situation lacks both elements. Ukraine is exhausted; Russia is not. That asymmetry will show in any deal eventually struck.

## What to watch

- Whether Zelensky agrees to any preliminary ceasefire framework before the winter, and on what territorial terms — this would be the clearest signal of how much ground Ukraine is prepared to concede.
- Whether European governments, particularly France, Germany, and the UK, are actually invited into trilateral talks or merely informed after the fact.
- The state of Western munitions and aid commitments heading into autumn: any deal's durability depends on whether Ukraine retains deterrent capacity.
- Whether Putin actually engages with a specific territorial proposal or continues to use the talks as diplomatic cover for ongoing advances.
