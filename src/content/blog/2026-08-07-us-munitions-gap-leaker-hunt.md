President Donald Trump on Thursday denied that the United States faces a munitions shortage stemming from Operation Epic Fury — the US-Israel military campaign against Iran now in its sixth month — and vowed to seek "long term jail sentences" for those he called "leakers" of classified information. The remarks followed [a Washington Post report](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o) that Trump had personally confronted Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth over a possible weapons shortfall, and a CBS News report citing two unnamed sources with direct knowledge who said the US had gone through much of its global stockpile of long-range precision missiles. The Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), using publicly available data, estimated that [the US had between 759 and 827 Patriot missiles remaining by end of July](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o) — roughly a third of the 2,330 available before the conflict began. Hegseth requested $87 billion in emergency funds from the Senate last month to pay service members and "rapidly replenish equipment and munitions."

## The received wisdom

The left-liberal critique here writes itself: a president threatening to prosecute journalists' sources is an assault on the First Amendment and press freedom. The munitions story is a matter of vital public interest. The public has a right to know whether the military is running short of the weapons it needs to conduct and sustain a war. Hegseth's heavy restrictions on Pentagon press access, his labelling of shortage reports as a "manufactured story," and Trump's threats of prosecution are all, in this reading, part of a pattern of authoritarian information control. That reading deserves to be taken seriously. The tools used to harass sources today can be wielded against whistle-blowers tomorrow; the infrastructure of secrecy, once constructed, is rarely dismantled. Press freedom organisations are right to be concerned, and historical precedent — from the Nixon administration's targeting of Daniel Ellsberg to the Obama administration's record prosecutions under the Espionage Act — shows that the slide from national security justification to political suppression is always shorter than advertised.

## A different read

There is, however, a version of this story that is less comfortable for progressive media institutions to tell, and it deserves equal airing.

Wartime operational security has always existed in tension with a free press, and the tension is genuine, not manufactured. The CSIS estimates of Patriot missile stocks are drawn from [public contract data, budget documents, and analytical modelling](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o). They are educated inferences, not classified facts. But the gap between an inference and a precise classified figure is precisely what adversaries exploit. Iran's Defence Minister, Majid Ibn-e-Reza, was already citing "the enemy's diminishing capabilities" in a public post on X within hours of the CBS and Washington Post stories. Whether or not the reports are accurate, they provide Tehran with a propaganda and psychological operations windfall. That is not nothing.

The harder question is whether the munitions shortage reports are, at their core, accurate. The evidence strongly suggests they are. Hegseth's own request for $87 billion in emergency replenishment funding is not the action of an administration that believes its stockpiles are fine. The Tomahawk rebuild timeline CSIS modelled — suggesting the pre-war inventory won't be restored until around 2031 — aligns with known production constraints at Raytheon and the long lead times for precision munitions manufacturing. You cannot wish away the laws of industrial production.

This creates a genuine dilemma. The Trump administration's response — deny, threaten, classify — is the worst possible approach. It forecloses public deliberation about weapons production priorities, military industrial policy, and the viability of the Iran campaign at exactly the moment when such deliberation is most needed. The American public, Congress, and allied governments all have a legitimate interest in knowing whether the country's arsenals are adequate to the commitments it has made. The alternative to transparent accounting is a war run by press release and threat.

But the left's instinct — that all national security classification is either pretextual or performative — is equally mistaken. There is a reason the Founding Fathers gave the executive, not the legislature or the press, operational command of the armed forces in wartime. The question is not whether any state secrets exist; it is where the line falls between legitimate operational security and politically motivated suppression of inconvenient truth.

Trump's statement that he believes Washington Post reporting to be "treasonous" falls far outside that legitimate line. Treason requires the giving of aid and comfort to the enemy. Reporting on publicly available analytical estimates, citing anonymous officials, and holding the executive to account does not meet that standard — it meets the standard of the First Amendment. The threat of prosecution, even if never acted upon, has a chilling effect on sources and reporters that serves no legitimate public interest. The history of seditious libel prosecutions in Britain and the colonies is precisely why the framers of the Constitution chose to protect speech the powerful find dangerous.

## What to watch

- **Congressional response**: The Senate will need to vote on Hegseth's $87bn emergency request. Watch for whether Republicans on the Armed Services Committee press for honest accounting of stockpile levels before approving.
- **Source prosecutions**: Whether the Justice Department actually brings Espionage Act cases against the alleged leakers. The Biden administration's record on Espionage Act prosecutions was itself troubling; the Trump administration's would set a new benchmark.
- **Ukraine Patriot decisions**: Trump told reporters at a recent cabinet meeting that granting Ukraine's Patriot request was a "big step." If US stocks are genuinely depleted, [the Ukraine allocation decision](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o) becomes a geopolitical litmus test.
- **Iran's response**: Tehran is already publicising the shortage reports as validation of its strategy. If the perception of US munitions constraints hardens, it changes Iran's calculus on whether to accept or delay a Hormuz deal.
