Andy Burnham was received by King Charles III at Buckingham Palace on Monday and asked to form a government, becoming Britain's seventh prime minister since 2016 and the sixth in ten years. [In his first address from Downing Street](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg9w2e29eno), delivered without a lectern or notes, he pledged to "bring back hope" and described his arrival as a "circuit breaker for Britain, bringing forward the biggest changes in the last 40 years." His first cabinet appointments saw John Healey — who resigned from Starmer's government over defence spending — become Chancellor, Ed Miliband move from energy to foreign secretary, and Wes Streeting appointed defence secretary. Former housing secretary Angela Rayner returned to cabinet. Rachel Reeves, David Lammy and Steve Reed were removed. Burnham's first policy pledge, made the same afternoon, was to end rough sleeping — a promise he made but failed to deliver as Mayor of Greater Manchester. He also signalled he would "look at" the personal tax allowance ahead of an autumn budget, while acknowledging the fiscal difficulty. Nigel Farage called immediately for a general election. Kemi Badenoch described Burnham as a "people pleaser" with "airy fairy" plans who could not take difficult decisions.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic reading of Burnham's ascension is essentially this: Britain has been through a decade of political instability, economic underperformance and governmental chaos. Starmer's resignation, whatever its immediate cause, was the culmination of a leadership that lost public confidence and failed to build a governing coalition that could translate its enormous parliamentary majority into durable policy achievements. Burnham, by contrast, brings something Starmer lacked: genuine popular warmth, a coherent regional political identity, and a credibility with working-class northern England that no Labour leader since Blair — and arguably since Wilson — has possessed. His record in Manchester, whatever its limits, demonstrated that he could actually govern rather than merely theorise. His promise of a "new economic model" and "biggest changes in 40 years" speaks to a real hunger in the electorate for something substantive after years of austerity, COVID mismanagement and post-Brexit drift. The argument that he lacks a mandate, this reading continues, is constitutionally illiterate: Britain has a parliamentary system, not a presidential one. The mandate belongs to the party, and Labour won it in 2024.

## A different read

The constitutional argument is correct as far as it goes, and then stops going far enough.

Yes, Britain has a parliamentary system in which governments can change between elections. That is not in dispute. What is in dispute is whether a prime minister who has been MP for Makerfield for barely five weeks, who entered parliament through a by-election whose primary purpose was to make him eligible for the leadership, and who has promised — in his first speech — the "biggest changes in the last 40 years" is operating within the spirit of that system rather than merely its letter. Nigel Farage's call for an election is self-serving — but that does not make it wrong. [Al Jazeera noted](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/20/what-are-the-new-british-prim) the legitimacy question is among Burnham's biggest challenges. Even Lib Dem leader Edward Davey, not exactly a right-wing firebrand, expressed disappointment at the absence of any serious engagement with social care, Europe or the environment in Burnham's opening address.

The personnel decisions reveal the contours of Burnham's political project — and its risks. Healey as Chancellor is an interesting choice: a man who resigned over defence spending, now tasked with finding the money to honour UK commitments that his predecessor's government failed to fund. That is a formidable internal contradiction. The [BBC notes](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cvg9w2e29eno) Healey will face the challenge of honouring defence commitments while meeting fiscal rules "with a buffer against uncertainty." That buffer does not obviously exist. Miliband at the Foreign Office brings a man whose political instincts are firmly in the progressive international tradition to a moment when British foreign policy needs to navigate a Trump White House, an active war with Iran, and a Ukraine conflict whose resolution shape Britain's relationship with Europe. Whether Miliband's particular combination of idealism and personal ambition is what that moment requires is a reasonable question.

The most revealing detail is the roughsleeping pledge. Burnham made this promise as Mayor of Manchester. He did not deliver it. Ending rough sleeping in a mid-sized English city with a dedicated mayoral budget and focused political will is hard. Ending it nationally is an order of magnitude more difficult. That he chose this as his first public commitment — rather than, say, the defence spending gap, the fiscal position, the NHS waiting lists, or the relationship with the Trump administration — tells you something about what kind of prime minister he wants to be. He wants to be the PM who talks to people in bus shelters and means it. That is not nothing. But governing is not the same as being warm.

The historical parallel that keeps coming to mind is not Blair in 1997 but Brown in 2007: a man who took office mid-parliament with vast ambitions and an uncertain mandate, who found that the goodwill of the first weeks did not survive the first serious test. Burnham is more politically agile than Brown and considerably warmer in person. But the structural problem — a parliamentary supermajority assembled under a different leader for a different programme, now asked to implement "the biggest changes in 40 years" under a prime minister nobody voted for — is similar.

## What to watch

- **The autumn budget**: Healey's fiscal framework will be the first real test. If he cannot find room for both defence spending and the personal allowance review, one of them will be jettisoned. Watch which.
- **Thames Water and nationalisation**: Burnham has signalled openness to nationalisation. Lenders are already [preparing legal challenges](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx278ed09nzo). The legal and fiscal exposure of a contested nationalisation could define his first year.
- **Trump relationship**: Burnham's first call with Trump covered Ukraine security and World Cup hospitality. The relationship will be tested much harder when US-Iran conflict consequences hit the British economy and defence budget.
- **By-election results**: The Conservatives and Reform will be watching every local and by-election result as evidence of whether Burnham is consolidating Labour's base or haemorrhaging support on multiple flanks.
