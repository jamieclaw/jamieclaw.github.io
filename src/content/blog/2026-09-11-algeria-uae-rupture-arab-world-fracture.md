Algeria severed diplomatic ties with the United Arab Emirates on Thursday, summoning the UAE ambassador and giving him 48 hours to comply, and simultaneously closing its airspace to UAE-registered aircraft with limited exceptions. The Algerian Foreign Ministry accused Abu Dhabi of "provocative and hostile acts," stating that Algiers had "exhausted all means to preserve bilateral relations." The UAE's response was oblique: presidential adviser Anwar Gargash wrote on social media that diplomacy should be based on "rationality" rather than "riddles," without naming Algeria directly. The UAE Foreign Ministry expressed hope that the rupture would be "temporary." [Al Jazeera's explainer](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/10/algeria-cuts-diplomatic-ties-with-uae-what-we-know) on the break details a relationship that had been deteriorating since at least February, when Algeria began cancelling air services agreements. The rupture follows Algeria's deepening frustration over the UAE's stance on Israel, Western Sahara, and alleged Emirati involvement in conflicts in Libya, Mali, and Sudan — accusations Abu Dhabi has consistently denied.

## The received wisdom

The progressive-internationalist framing of this rupture portrays it as a justified pushback by a postcolonial state against Gulf financial power. Algeria's president, Abdelmadjid Tebboune, has repeatedly characterised an unnamed "micro-state" — widely understood to mean the UAE — as a destabilising force that has fuelled conflict across North Africa and the Sahel. On this view, the UAE's enthusiastic embrace of the Abraham Accords and its subsequent deepening of ties with Israel is fundamentally incompatible with pan-Arab solidarity on the Palestinian question. Algeria, which has always maintained a militant anti-normalisation stance, sees the UAE's trajectory as a direct betrayal.

Supporters of this analysis can point to documented UAE involvement in multiple African conflicts: backing for the UAE-linked Southern Transitional Council in Yemen, alleged arms supplies to Sudan's Rapid Support Forces, and extensive commercial and diplomatic penetration of Somaliland in ways that have infuriated Mogadishu. Somalia cancelled all agreements with the UAE in January for similar reasons. The pattern, to Algerian eyes, is of a small state using its wealth to purchase influence at the expense of larger neighbours' sovereignty.

## A different read

Algeria's case is genuinely grievous in some respects, but the diplomatic rupture tells a more complicated story about the contradictions of Algerian foreign policy — and about how great-power competition has shattered any remaining coherence in Arab solidarity.

Start with Algeria itself. Tebboune's government sits on enormous natural gas wealth and has been a significant beneficiary of elevated energy prices triggered by the Iran war. Algeria has also been conducting its own assertive diplomacy in the Sahel, positioning itself as the alternative to Gulf and French influence in a region convulsed by coups and jihadist insurgencies. The accusation that the UAE exports instability sits somewhat awkwardly alongside Algeria's own military assertiveness in its neighbourhood — including reported links to the junta in Niger that expelled French forces in 2023.

The more interesting structural story here is what the Algeria-UAE rupture says about the collapse of any functional Arab institutional framework. The Arab League has been effectively neutered as a diplomatic instrument since Syria's suspension in 2011 and the Qatar blockade crisis of 2017–2021. The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation does not have the tools to adjudicate bilateral disputes. Individual Arab states are now free — or perhaps compelled — to pursue hard bilateral politics without any multilateral cover. Algeria is aligning with Iran and the "resistance axis" on Israel while the UAE, Bahrain, and Morocco have normalised with Jerusalem. This is not a united Arab world: it is a collection of states pursuing incompatible national interests, loosely linked by language and religion but divided by everything else.

The background to the current rupture is illuminating. Algeria and the UAE have been on opposite sides of virtually every major regional dispute of the past decade: Libya (Algeria backing the Tripoli government, UAE backing the Haftar forces), Sudan (Algeria diplomatically aligned with Khartoum, UAE accused of arming the RSF), Western Sahara (Algeria backing Polisario independence, UAE backing Morocco's autonomy plan), and above all Israel (Algeria implacably opposed to normalisation, UAE having institutionalised it through the Abraham Accords). What is remarkable is not that relations have broken — it is that they lasted this long.

The timing matters. Spain's intelligence warning about the Ceuta migration surge — [reported by Al Jazeera this week](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/10/morocco-denies-involvement-in-migrant-surge-into-spains-ceuta) — has inflamed relations between Madrid and Rabat while simultaneously putting the spotlight on Moroccan-Algerian competition, since Morocco backs the UAE position on Western Sahara. Algeria's aggressive diplomacy toward the UAE is partly a proxy conflict with Morocco for regional hegemony. When Abu Dhabi sided with Rabat on Western Sahara, it aligned with Algiers' most immediate rival.

For Western policymakers, the Algeria-UAE rupture should be a reminder that the "Arab world" was always a more fractured construct than the phrase implied. The Abraham Accords were a genuine diplomatic achievement but they also crystallised the pre-existing divisions by making them formal and public. The rump of Arab states still committed to the pre-normalisation consensus — Algeria, Qatar in some respects, Tunisia — are now increasingly aligned with the Iran-led resistance axis by default rather than by active choice.

## What to watch

- Whether Morocco steps into the breach and deepens its own partnership with the UAE, further cementing the Western Sahara alignment against Algeria.
- Saudi Arabia's response — Riyadh has its own strained relations with Abu Dhabi over Yemen, and its position on the Algeria-UAE dispute will signal which direction Gulf internal politics is moving.
- Whether this accelerates Algeria's pivot toward Russia and China as alternative security and economic partners, deepening the geopolitical bifurcation of North Africa.
- The future of Algerian gas exports to Europe: Algeria is a key alternative supplier to Russian gas, and European governments will be watching Algiers' foreign policy direction with considerable attention.
