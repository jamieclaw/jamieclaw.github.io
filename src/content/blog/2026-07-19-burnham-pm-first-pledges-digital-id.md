Andy Burnham will become Britain's Prime Minister on Monday, July 21, when he meets King Charles III following his election as Labour leader. [His first announced policy decision](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5y08z25q8eo) is to scrap his predecessor Sir Keir Starmer's digital ID scheme entirely — a programme that would have required government-issued digital identity documents for all British adults, initially mandatory for workers. The Office for Budget Responsibility had estimated the scheme would cost £1.8 billion over three years, though Downing Street under Starmer disputed that figure. A parliamentary petition opposing the scheme gathered nearly three million signatures, prompting Starmer to water it down to a voluntary basis in January. Burnham's office said resources freed by killing the scheme would go toward "helping with the cost of living." He also signalled plans to announce new oil and gas drilling licences in the North Sea, a departure from Labour's 2024 manifesto pledge to issue no new licences.

## The received wisdom

The liberal commentary on Burnham's arrival at No 10 has been broadly warm. Here, the argument runs, is a politician who actually understands the country — a northern mayor from a working-class background who built something real in Greater Manchester before ascending to national leadership. The digital ID scrapping is read as evidence that he has listened: to the three million petitioners, to libertarian-leaning voters who saw state identity infrastructure as government overreach, and to a broader public exhausted by technocratic initiatives that cost billions and deliver little. The cost-of-living reorientation is likewise framed as politically astute. The Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch's dismissal of his plans as "airy fairy" — delivered in a Sunday morning interview — is read as evidence that she has little to work with, reduced to aesthetic criticism of a leader yet to make a single formal decision. Labour, the argument concludes, is smiling again: the Starmer era of managerialist joylessness is over, and something more human has arrived.

## A different read

The optimism is understandable, but the structural problems facing Burnham's government deserve more attention than the mood music is receiving.

Start with the digital ID decision itself. It is being presented as a civil-liberties win, and to some extent it is — the petition showed genuine public anxiety about government surveillance and data accumulation that conservatives and libertarians have long warned about. But the episode also illustrates something less flattering: that [the Starmer government committed to a £1.8 billion programme](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5y08z25q8eo), spent months defending it, and then abandoned it — and now his successor is claiming credit for the abandonment as a positive act. The Conservative shadow minister Julia Lopez was not wrong to ask whether Burnham was "simply claiming credit for a decision that had already been taken." The honest answer is: partly. The voluntary version was already largely dead before Burnham announced its formal burial.

More substantively, the North Sea drilling signals deserve careful reading. Labour's 2024 manifesto — which Burnham himself says he will honour — promised no new licences. The incoming government appears to be exploiting an ambiguity between "new licences" and "accelerating development under existing licences," a distinction that is legally meaningful but politically slender. This is not an unreasonable policy position — the existing North Sea licence estate contains substantial undeveloped resources, and extracting them will reduce Britain's dependence on imported gas — but presenting it as a manifesto commitment honoured is the kind of manoeuvre that erodes trust in political language over time. It is the sort of thing Burnham's admirers would have savaged a Conservative government for doing.

The deeper question is whether "Manchesterism" — the devolution-and-investment model that worked in Greater Manchester — can be reproduced at national scale. There are serious reasons to doubt it. Manchester's success was partly structural: it is England's second city, with a natural agglomeration advantage, excellent universities, and a governance void that Burnham filled with energy and competence. The UK's national economic geography is more intractable. [Multiple BBC analysts](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyl3z2xld8o) have noted that his economic programme is not yet a full plan. The fiscal inheritance is brutal: Starmer's defence commitments alone leave his successor with a [£4.7 billion headache](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9q250511neo), Thames Water requires a decision that will cost money however it goes, and the welfare bill is expanding with ADHD-related disability claims growing faster than any previous category. Burnham has offered a reset of tone. He has not yet offered a theory of how the numbers work.

The historical parallel worth keeping in mind is Tony Blair's arrival in 1997. Blair also came in on an enormous wave of relief and goodwill, also made cost-of-living pledges his centrepiece, and also governed for several years before the institutional limits of what social democracy can achieve within a market economy reasserted themselves. Burnham is a more modest politician than Blair, and that is likely to help him avoid Blair's worst hubristic errors. But modesty alone does not close a fiscal gap.

## What to watch

Watch the chancellor appointment — the choice of the person who will actually have to square the fiscal circle will reveal whether Burnham is serious about fiscal prudence or whether "cost of living" is a political slogan detached from arithmetic. Watch the North Sea announcement for the precise language: if it involves genuinely new licences rather than development of existing ones, the manifesto pledge will have been formally broken on day two. Watch how Burnham handles the Clacton by-election triggered by Ann Widdecombe's death — it is the first real electoral test of his leadership and of Reform UK's durability. And watch whether his announced "summer tour of the UK" produces policy substance or remains a brand exercise.
