France's parliament voted on Tuesday to pass a landmark bill barring children under the age of 15 from using social media platforms, making France the first country in the European Union to approve a blanket ban of this kind, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/21/french-parliament-passes-social-media-ban-for-under-15s). Both chambers — the National Assembly and the Senate — approved the legislation, which also bans students from using mobile phones in schools. The ban is expected to be rolled out in two stages: children under 15 will be blocked from creating new accounts from September 1, with the ban applying to existing accounts from January 2027. It will not apply to access to online encyclopaedias, educational platforms or scientific directories. President Emmanuel Macron, who championed the measure as a signature initiative of his second term, called parliament's approval "a major step forward" and said France was "leading the way in Europe when it comes to protecting our children and teenagers." The Constitutional Council must still rule on whether the legislation complies with the French constitution before it enters full force, a process that could delay implementation. France's public health watchdog said last year that platforms including TikTok, Snapchat and Instagram were harmful to adolescents, particularly girls. Several families in France have sued TikTok over teen suicides they say are linked to harmful content on the platform. Lawmakers from France Unbowed opposed the bill, arguing that its constitutionality was unclear, that it would effectively end online anonymity for minors, and that enforcement would be practically impossible.

## The received wisdom

The civil libertarian and techno-progressive critique of this law is coherent and deserves to be stated clearly. Age verification at scale is technically very difficult. Any system that confirms a child's age necessarily collects identifying information — creating a database that can be breached, weaponised or misused. The argument that France's ban will effectively end online anonymity for minors is not hyperbolic: to prove a user is over 15, a platform must know something real about them, and real information is exactly what privacy advocates have spent decades trying to keep out of the hands of platforms that have repeatedly abused it. The enforcement sceptics are also correct that children are sophisticated users of workarounds: VPNs, borrowed devices, falsified birthdates. Australia enacted a similar ban and [closed 4.7 million accounts](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/1/16/australia-declares-child-social-media-ban-victory-as-4-7m-accounts-closed), but the extent to which determined underage users found alternatives is an empirical question that has not been fully answered. Left-wing parliamentarians from France Unbowed have a point that the state rarely makes itself more trustworthy by gathering more information. All of this is real, and none of it should be dismissed.

## A different read

And yet: something had to give.

The argument against regulation has dominated tech policy in Western democracies for roughly two decades, and its record is not strong. The self-regulatory model — in which platforms promise to enforce age limits, moderate harmful content and prioritise user wellbeing — has produced the documented reality that teenagers, including very young teenagers, are exposed to eating disorder content, self-harm communities, suicide method descriptions, and algorithmic systems specifically designed to maximise engagement regardless of psychological consequence. France's public health watchdog cited TikTok, Snapchat and Instagram as harmful to adolescents in findings released last year. Families have lost children and are suing. The social experiment of giving unrestricted algorithmic media access to 12-year-olds has been running for fifteen years, and the results are in.

The deeper argument for France's approach is not that government knows best in general, but that the specific failure mode here — profit-motivated corporations optimising for engagement metrics with psychologically vulnerable minors — is precisely the kind of market failure that regulation exists to correct. A 12-year-old cannot meaningfully consent to the terms of service that grant TikTok's algorithm unrestricted access to her attention and emotional responses. Her parents are, in practice, unable to monitor the full content of what she sees. The platform has no financial incentive to moderate content that keeps her scrolling. This is a classic externality problem: the costs are borne by the child and her family; the benefits accrue to the platform and its shareholders. The normal conservative argument for limited government rests on the presumption that individual actors can make and bear the consequences of their own decisions. It does not obviously apply to minors who cannot legally drive, drink, vote, or sign a contract.

The Australian model, which France appears to have borrowed from and extended, is the relevant comparator. When Australia passed its ban in late 2025, it was dismissed by tech companies as unenforceable and by civil libertarians as an overreach. The closure of 4.7 million accounts — a voluntary response by platforms under regulatory pressure — suggests that the companies, when faced with real legal liability, find enforcement mechanisms that they insisted were impossible when no liability existed. The lesson is not that the law solved the problem. It is that liability changes incentives, and changed incentives change behaviour.

The Constitutional Council review is the critical near-term variable. If it upholds the law, France will have created a working template for the EU — one that arrives as the bloc debates its own Digital Services Act enforcement and as several member states consider analogous measures. If the Council strikes it down on anonymity grounds, the political pressure for an alternative mechanism that achieves the same goal without the privacy exposure will intensify rather than dissipate. Either way, the era of unrestricted platform access to children's attention has, in France at least, formally ended.

## What to watch

- The Constitutional Council's ruling, expected within weeks: if it upholds the law, September 1 implementation begins. If it strikes it down, Macron will need a revised version.
- Platform compliance: TikTok, Meta's Instagram, and Snapchat will need to develop technically credible age-verification systems — the specifications for which will likely become the de facto European standard.
- Whether other EU member states accelerate their own legislation, citing France as the template, or wait for EU-level action under the Digital Services Act.
- The enforcement reality: how many accounts are closed versus how many underage users successfully migrate to workarounds, which will determine whether the law's public health objective is actually achieved.
