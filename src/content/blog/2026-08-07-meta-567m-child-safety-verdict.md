A federal judge in New Mexico on Thursday ordered Meta to pay [$567 million](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cd7lz3wr2rlo) for its failure to warn the public about dangers its platforms posed to children — the largest ruling against a social media company over child safety in US history. Judge Bryan Biedscheid's ruling comes on top of a $375 million verdict in the same case handed down earlier, bringing the total judgment to $942 million. Biedscheid compared Meta to a factory, characterising its advertising and content systems as its product and the "psychological harm and sexual exploitation of children" as the pollution that must be abated. He ruled that Meta constitutes a "public nuisance" — a legal theory more commonly applied to lead paint manufacturers, opioid distributors, and environmental polluters. Meta, which had already signalled it would appeal the $375 million portion of the verdict, has been contacted for comment on the new ruling.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading of this verdict is straightforwardly celebratory: Big Tech has for too long operated outside the accountability structures that govern every other industry affecting public health and safety. Internal documents revealed in litigation — much of it spearheaded by state attorneys general across the country — have shown that Meta executives were aware their platforms were causing harm to young users and made deliberate choices to prioritise engagement over wellbeing. Frances Haugen's testimony and subsequent reporting on Instagram's own internal research showing it worsened body image issues in teenage girls has become the canonical reference point. In this view, the $942 million judgment is a down payment on accountability that should have arrived years earlier. Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which has shielded platforms from liability for user-generated content since 1996, has functioned as a structural subsidy for harmful business models. Courts and legislatures are finally beginning to pierce that veil.

## A different read

None of that analysis is wrong. But the public-nuisance theory Judge Biedscheid applied deserves scrutiny on its own terms — because it is legally aggressive in ways that could have consequences well beyond social media companies.

The public nuisance doctrine, as applied to corporations in recent decades, has had a chequered history. It was the theory that allowed Oklahoma courts to impose a $465 million judgment on Johnson & Johnson for opioid-related harms — a judgment subsequently overturned by the Oklahoma Supreme Court on the grounds that the doctrine was being impermissibly stretched beyond its historical scope. The West Virginia Supreme Court similarly rejected public nuisance as a basis for opioid liability. The Supreme Court of the United States has not yet definitively ruled on the limits of corporate public nuisance liability, but the lower-court record suggests a genuine doctrinal tension.

The factory analogy Judge Biedscheid used is evocative, but it glosses over a fundamental difference: the harm that Meta's platforms cause is mediated by human behaviour, not direct physical pollution. A factory emitting toxins poisons the air regardless of individual choice. Instagram's algorithmic feeds cause harm through a complex interaction of design choices, adolescent psychology, social dynamics, and individual vulnerability. This is not an argument for immunity — it is an argument for precision in legal theory. Laws that attribute harm too broadly risk chilling legitimate speech and expression, and social media platforms, whatever their flaws, are also genuine public squares.

The more durable approach to platform accountability is probably not judicial but legislative. Congress enacted the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) in 1998 but has never meaningfully updated it. The Kids Online Safety Act has stalled repeatedly in the Senate, a casualty of both tech industry lobbying and genuine civil liberties concerns about government-mandated age verification. Several states — including California, Utah, and Arkansas — have passed their own children's online protection statutes, though these too face constitutional challenges.

There is also a conservative critique of the Meta litigation that is rarely articulated clearly: the state attorney general campaigns against social media companies have sometimes blurred the line between genuine child safety concerns and political pressure on platforms for their content moderation decisions. Several Republican AGs who have joined the Meta suits have simultaneously pressured platforms to reinstate conservative accounts or reduce their moderation of right-wing speech. This instrumentalisation of child safety litigation as a political tool serves no one — least of all children.

The better case for holding Meta accountable is the one that focuses on design choices: the decision to algorithmically amplify content that maximises engagement regardless of its effect on user wellbeing, the resistance to parental oversight tools, the creation of deliberately addictive feedback loops in products explicitly marketed to users as young as thirteen. Those are product design decisions, and they should be subject to the same product liability principles as any other consumer product. That legal theory — narrower than public nuisance, more grounded in established precedent — is likely to be the basis on which appellate courts ultimately uphold any lasting accountability.

## What to watch

- **Meta's appeal**: The company has signalled it will contest the earlier $375 million verdict. The full $942 million will likely be stayed pending appeal, making the appellate record at the Tenth Circuit and eventually the Supreme Court the ones that matter.
- **Section 230 reform**: Congress has now had multiple legislative sessions in which bipartisan child safety bills have stalled. The accumulation of state verdicts increases pressure for a federal legislative solution.
- **Public nuisance doctrine at SCOTUS**: If the opioid litigation's doctrinal history is any guide, the Supreme Court will eventually need to clarify the scope of corporate public nuisance liability. Meta's case may provide the vehicle.
- **AI platforms**: The child safety cases against Meta were built on internal documents showing corporate knowledge of harm. As AI-driven platforms accumulate their own internal research, the evidentiary landscape for future litigation is already forming.
