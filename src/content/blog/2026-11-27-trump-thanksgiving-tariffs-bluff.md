The Trump administration has [announced a new set of tariffs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c893r9ke5p97o) targeting China and a range of other countries, timed — deliberately, it appears — to the Thanksgiving holiday period. The announcement sent global equities lower. Al Jazeera's analysis asks [what Trump's latest tariffs mean for the world](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/inside-story/2026/11/26/what-will-trumps-latest-tariffs-mean-for-the-world), noting the breadth of countries now in the crosshairs. BBC economics editor Faisal Islam, meanwhile, [argues that the tariffs are less of a threat](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6r7g4e3yep1o) than they appear, suggesting that markets have absorbed a degree of tariff noise from this administration that would have caused panic under any predecessor. The timing — a holiday announcement designed to minimise immediate political accountability — fits a pattern of using tariffs as ambient pressure rather than targeted negotiating tools. What has changed from the first Trump term is the scale of habituation: markets, trading partners, and domestic importers have now had time to price in a degree of permanent uncertainty.

## The received wisdom

The standard critique of Trump's tariff policy is well-rehearsed and largely accurate in its narrow terms. Tariffs are, in economic effect, taxes on domestic consumers and importers. They raise prices. They invite retaliation that damages American exporters — particularly in agriculture and manufacturing. They do not, on the evidence of the first Trump term, durably shift supply chains back to the United States because that relocation requires capital investment decisions made over decades, not years. The economist consensus — from the Peterson Institute to the IMF — is that tariffs of this kind produce modest domestic gains for protected industries while distributing costs broadly and invisibly across the whole economy. On this reading, the Thanksgiving tariffs are a political gesture dressed up as industrial policy, and Faisal Islam is right that [their practical threat is limited](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6r7g4e3yep1o) by the administration's own track record of announcing tariffs and then quietly granting exemptions.

## A different read

The economic critique is correct in its empirics but limited in its analytical frame. It treats tariffs as instruments of optimising trade flows, when Trump is using them as instruments of political communication — to allies, adversaries, and his domestic base simultaneously. Measured in those terms, the policy is not obviously irrational.

Consider the structural challenge the United States faces in its economic relationship with China. The bilateral trade deficit with Beijing is not merely an accounting identity: it reflects decades of market access asymmetries, state subsidies for Chinese exports, technology transfer coercion, and intellectual property appropriation that conventional free-trade frameworks proved inadequate to address. The Obama administration acknowledged this problem and largely failed to solve it diplomatically. The Biden administration's response — targeted export controls and the CHIPS Act — was more sophisticated but also more narrow. Trump's tariff-first approach is blunt; that does not necessarily make it wrong.

There is also a reasonable strategic case for unpredictability. The game-theoretic literature on deterrence suggests that a reputation for being willing to impose costs — even economically suboptimal ones — has signalling value that a reputation for measured, proportionate responses does not. Richard Nixon's "madman theory" was, famously, partly theatre. So are the Thanksgiving tariffs. The question is whether the theatre achieves results.

The honest answer is: sometimes. The first Trump term produced the Phase One trade deal with China in January 2020, which, [whatever its subsequent implementation failings](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c893r9ke5p97o), represented a genuine shift in Chinese commitments on agricultural purchases and intellectual property norms. It was not transformative. But it was something. The Mexico-Canada negotiation during USMCA renegotiation similarly produced real, if modest, improvements to American terms. The tariff bluff has worked often enough to sustain itself politically, even if the economic costs accumulate in the background.

Where the critique bites hardest is on the question of duration. A tariff deployed as a negotiating tool has logic. A tariff deployed as a permanent regime — which is what the current announcements increasingly resemble — loses its pressure function and becomes simply a tax. There is a difference between wielding a hammer and living inside one. The [Christmas toy supply chain analysis](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5djvyde0y1jo) from earlier this autumn showed how the cumulative effect of sustained China tariffs is being absorbed — expensively — across American retail, with costs passed to consumers in ways that are politically invisible precisely because they are diffuse.

## What to watch

Watch the exchange rate responses in currencies of the tariff-targeted countries, which will indicate whether markets expect negotiated resolution or escalation. Monitor whether the [UK government's calculations about Trump's tariff threat](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6r7g4e3yep1o) affect its Brexit reset calculations — London's willingness to absorb EU regulatory alignment is partly driven by a desire to diversify away from US trade dependency. Track whether China retaliates or absorbs: Beijing's response will determine whether this is another round of theatre or the beginning of a genuinely new trade war phase.
