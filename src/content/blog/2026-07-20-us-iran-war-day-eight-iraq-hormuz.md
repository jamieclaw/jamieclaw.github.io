On Saturday, a United States service member was killed in northern Iraq after an Iranian attack drone was shot down and its unexploded ordnance detonated during disposal. A second service member was wounded. The incident followed an attack the previous day in Jordan that killed two US soldiers and left a third missing — with Centcom subsequently finding "unidentified remains" at the site. The conflict, now entering its eighth consecutive day of mutual strikes, has claimed 17 American lives since hostilities resumed after a preliminary ceasefire collapsed less than a month after it began. [US Central Command confirmed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cgk417jp83po) that its strikes were intended to "degrade Iran's ability to threaten commercial shipping" in the Strait of Hormuz and to "punish" Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. Tehran, meanwhile, has declared the Strait closed and is targeting US allies across the region, including strikes on a Kuwaiti electricity and water plant.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of this conflict places the blame squarely on the Trump administration's unilateral decision to tear up the June ceasefire. The argument runs as follows: a painstaking diplomatic process had finally produced an agreement that halted hostilities; Washington's maximalist demands and habitual unreliability — embodied in Trump declaring the deal "over" on 8 July — destroyed a fragile peace; and the deaths on all sides, including at least 50 Iranians killed and 500 injured in US strikes over three weeks, are the direct consequence of American impulsiveness. There is something to this reading. Ceasefire agreements are inherently fragile, and trust between Washington and Tehran is essentially nil. The death of the agreement matters. The progressive consensus adds a layer: all military action is inherently wrong, Iran has legitimate grievances, and the Strait of Hormuz closure is a rational defensive response to aggression. This framing has the merit of moral consistency even if it struggles with the question of what, precisely, should follow from it.

## A different read

The collapse of the June agreement deserves scrutiny, but not in the direction most commentators have taken. Washington and Tehran have been negotiating under duress before — most recently during the original JCPOA process — and the pattern has been consistent: Iran treats preliminary agreements as breathing space to reconsolidate rather than as genuine commitments. [Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei's statement](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cgk417jp83po) that America's "repeated breaches" proved its "signature is utterly worthless" is rich coming from a regime that spent the 2015-2018 period exploiting the JCPOA's sunset clauses while accelerating centrifuge research, a pattern documented extensively by the International Atomic Energy Agency.

More revealing is the geography of the current escalation. Iranian strikes are no longer limited to US forces in Syria or Yemen-based proxies — they are now hitting Jordan directly, prompting Amman to summon Iran's chargé d'affaires and warn that Jordanian security is a "red line that cannot be crossed." Jordan has been a quiet stabiliser in the region for decades, and bringing it into the conflict's blast radius is a profound strategic miscalculation by Tehran, one that suggests the regime is responding emotionally rather than strategically. The Gulf Cooperation Council — Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE — has accused Iran of targeting civilian infrastructure in violation of international law. These are not American allies expressing obligatory solidarity; these are Sunni Arab states that have spent years attempting a cautious rapprochement with Tehran. Their formal denunciations mark a significant regional realignment.

The Strait of Hormuz closure is the sharpest escalation. Approximately 20 percent of global oil supplies transit the strait; the IRGC's warnings that vessels "influenced by the US" face "accidents" is not rhetorical — two ships were already involved in such "accidents" this weekend. The economic consequences of a prolonged closure would be felt not in Washington but in Beijing, Tokyo, and Delhi, all of which depend heavily on Gulf crude. Iran may be counting on Asian pressure to restrain American action, but it is also alienating the very powers whose geopolitical support grants it strategic cover.

The hardest question — one the progressive framing sidesteps — is what a successful outcome looks like from the American perspective. Containment without strategic clarity has been the default for twenty years, and it has produced an Iran that is closer to a nuclear threshold than ever before, that controls the world's most important waterway, and that has now killed 17 US service members in open conflict. The argument for sustained military pressure is not that it is pleasant but that every previous ceasefire has produced a more dangerous Iran. This does not mean the current approach is working — the absence of a stated end-state from Washington is alarming — but the narrative that diplomacy was working fine until Trump ruined it requires a very selective reading of the last two decades of American-Iranian relations.

What the situation calls for, and conspicuously lacks, is a precise strategic objective. Degrading Iran's ability to threaten shipping is a tactical goal, not a strategy. Without clarity about whether Washington seeks a revised nuclear agreement, regime change, or simply deterrence re-established, US forces will keep absorbing casualties in a conflict whose direction is opaque.

## What to watch

- Whether the Asian powers — China, Japan, South Korea — publicly pressure either Washington or Tehran to reopen the Strait, which would signal the conflict is beginning to destabilise global energy markets in a way that forces third-party intervention.
- Whether Jordan's formal complaint to Iran signals a broader regional shift, or whether Amman retreats to its habitual caution.
- US Centcom's language around the "unidentified remains" found in Jordan — formal confirmation of a third death would trigger domestic political pressure for either escalation or a diplomatic off-ramp.
- Whether the IAEA's call for restraint leads to any formal Security Council session, and whether China or Russia block it — both would be telling.
