The International Court of Justice in The Hague ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya Muslim minority during a military campaign in 2017, in what [the Guardian described as a historic and unprecedented decision](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/19/genocide-rohingya-international-court-of-justice-ruling-myanmar). The court, which had never previously found a state responsible for genocide, issued a nine-to-six majority ruling that found "intent to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population." More than 10,000 Rohingya were killed in the 2017 campaign and 700,000 fled to neighbouring Bangladesh. The case was brought by The Gambia under the 1948 Genocide Convention. The ruling was [greeted with tears and applause in the refugee camps of Bangladesh](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/nov/19/everyone-struggled-for-this-justice-tears-in-court-as-rohingya-vindicated-in-myanmar-genocide-ruling) where many Rohingya remain nearly a decade later. The United States [responded with caution](https://www.theguardian.com/law/2026/nov/20/us-eu-reaction-myanmar-rohingya-genocide-icj-ruling-trump-international-law), while China raised concerns about judicial overreach.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive framing is that this ruling represents a triumph of international law over impunity. For a decade, the Rohingya — stateless, dispersed, without a government to advocate for them — have waited for formal recognition that what happened to them was not merely ethnic violence or military excess but the most serious crime in international law. The ICJ's ruling delivers that recognition and sets a precedent for how the genocide convention should be applied to future situations where states use military force against minority populations. For advocates of the international rules-based order, this is precisely the moment when institutions justify their existence: they provide recourse for those with no other advocate. The ruling is also, as [legal analysis in the Guardian noted](https://www.theguardian.com/law/2026/nov/19/myanmar-ruling-new-relevance-genocide-convention-messages-israel), "full of messages" for other ongoing cases, including the South African case against Israel at the same court — a precedent-setting roadmap for how intent can be inferred from state conduct.

## A different read

The right-of-centre case here is not to defend the Myanmar military's campaign, which was monstrous by any standard, or to deny the Rohingya their moment of legal recognition. It is to ask the harder question: what does this ruling actually do?

The answer, examined honestly, is: very little in the near term. Myanmar's military junta — which came to power after a 2021 coup and is currently engaged in a bloody civil war — has no incentive to comply with any ICJ order. The court has no enforcement mechanism of its own. Enforcement would require action by the UN Security Council, where China, Myanmar's principal patron, sits with veto power. [China's response to the ruling](https://www.theguardian.com/law/2026/nov/20/us-eu-reaction-myanmar-rohingya-genocide-icj-ruling-trump-international-law) — raising concerns about "judicial overreach" — signals exactly what kind of Security Council response is coming. The Rohingya, in other words, have won in the court and remain stateless in reality. That gap between legal recognition and practical remedy is not a bug in the international system; it is a central feature that the advocates of international law have consistently underweighted.

The American reaction is worth examining for what it reveals about the current administration's posture. The Trump administration's cautious welcome of the ruling is freighted with self-interest: an enthusiastic endorsement of the genocide convention's expansive application would be awkward given the pending South African case against Israel, an ally whose military operations the US has actively supported. [As the Guardian observed](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/18/us-sanctions-icj-judges-myanmar-genocide-ruling-israel-gaza-court), there were fears before the ruling that the US might even impose sanctions on ICJ judges if the precedent were set too broadly. The fact that it has not done so immediately is a minor concession to appearances rather than a principled endorsement of international judicial authority.

There is a deeper structural problem here that the ruling forces into view. The genocide convention was drafted in 1948 precisely because the great powers — fresh from Nuremberg — believed that legal prohibition could prevent future atrocities. Seventy-eight years on, the evidence for that thesis is mixed at best. Genocide has occurred in Cambodia, Bosnia, Rwanda, and now Myanmar, all in the convention's lifetime. Each time, the international legal response has followed the killings rather than prevented them. The convention's real function has been less deterrence than retrospective accountability, which is valuable — the Rohingya deserve their legal vindication — but insufficient as a system for protecting vulnerable populations in real time.

The right-of-centre lesson is that the protection of minority populations ultimately depends not on international courts but on the domestic political culture and institutional constraints of the states in which those populations live, and on the willingness of great powers with actual leverage — including China — to condition their relationships with client states on decent treatment of minorities. Neither of those conditions was met in Myanmar, and the ICJ ruling, however historic, does not change that underlying reality. What would actually help the Rohingya is sustained Chinese pressure on the junta — which Beijing is unwilling to apply — and a credible international mechanism for refugee resettlement and statelessness resolution, which no government is currently offering.

## What to watch

Watch whether The Gambia or any other state asks the Security Council to enforce the ICJ ruling, and what happens when China vetoes any such action — that veto will clarify for everyone where the actual limits of international law lie. Watch the Israeli case at the ICJ: the Myanmar ruling's nine-to-six majority and its detailed roadmap for assessing genocidal intent will now become the central reference document in that proceeding. Watch whether the US revisits its threat to sanction ICJ judges if the Israel case proceeds unfavorably. And watch Bangladesh: the country is hosting over a million Rohingya refugees and its own political situation, with [protests and unrest surrounding former PM Sheikh Hasina's planned return](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/19/bangladesh-violent-protest-sheikh-hasina-planned-return), is increasingly fragile. The Rohingya crisis has a human dimension that no court ruling resolves.
