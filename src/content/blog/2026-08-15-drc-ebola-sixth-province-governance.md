The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has spread to a sixth province after a man died in Bas-Uele having travelled from the outbreak's original epicentre in Haut-Uele, officials confirmed Thursday. The development came one day after the head of the World Health Organization [described the outbreak as on track to surpass the deadliest in recorded history](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/13/ebola-drc-democratic-republic-congo-sixth-province) — a grim threshold set by the 2014-16 West African epidemic that killed at least 11,000 people. The current outbreak has already killed more than 2,100 of the 4,500-plus confirmed and probable cases recorded in Congo. By comparison, at the same stage of the 2014-16 outbreak, only 755 cases had been recorded. The virus's expansion across provincial boundaries raises acute logistical challenges for a country whose eastern provinces have been in intermittent armed conflict for decades and whose public health infrastructure has been systematically degraded by both violence and underfunding.

## The received wisdom

The global health establishment's response framework is well-worn: declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, mobilise WHO emergency funding, deploy specialist response teams, accelerate vaccine distribution, and issue coordinated appeals for donor support. The narrative is one of technical challenge: the DRC's geography is formidable, the population's trust of government health workers is fragile (partly due to historical abuses, partly due to disinformation), and the security situation in the east makes vaccine delivery genuinely dangerous. The solution, on this reading, is more resources, better community engagement, more robust WHO coordination authority, and continued commitment from wealthy donor nations.

This framework has genuine substance. The Merck rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine, which proved highly effective in the 2018-2020 Kivu outbreak, has been part of the response. Community engagement protocols developed after the Kivu experience — including working with traditional healers and community leaders rather than around them — represent real learning. The mainstream case for more international support is not wrong.

## A different read

But the Ebola crisis in Congo is now, in its essential character, a governance failure dressed up as a technical problem — and the international community's preferred solutions are, at best, repeating the same interventions and expecting different results.

Congo has experienced multiple Ebola outbreaks. The 2018-2020 Kivu outbreak was the second-largest ever at the time; the current outbreak is now larger. Between each outbreak, the same international machinery mobilises, responds, contains, and withdraws — leaving behind improved local capacity that tends to atrophy when the international staff leave, the funding dries up, and the attention moves elsewhere. The pattern is structurally similar to the post-conflict reconstruction cycles in Afghanistan, Haiti, and South Sudan: intensive external intervention, apparent success, withdrawal, regression.

The fundamental problem in Bas-Uele and Haut-Uele is not, at its core, a shortage of vaccine doses or a lack of WHO protocols. It is that the Congolese state — in those provinces, at this moment — lacks the territorial control, the logistical capacity, and the community legitimacy to implement any public health response effectively. Armed groups control movement corridors. Health workers have been [killed during previous outbreaks](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/13/ebola-drc-democratic-republic-congo-sixth-province) while attempting to conduct contact tracing. Communities in active conflict zones have reasons — rational reasons, rooted in lived experience — to be deeply suspicious of any government-affiliated health intervention.

The international community has spent three decades and tens of billions of dollars attempting to build Congolese state capacity through aid programmes, peacekeeping missions (MONUSCO has been present since 1999), and governance reform initiatives. The result is a state that, in its eastern reaches, remains among the weakest in the world by any measure. This is not an argument for abandoning the DRC; it is an argument for honesty about what international engagement has and has not achieved, and for rethinking the model.

What would a different approach look like? It would start with the recognition that health security and political security are inseparable in this context — that no amount of WHO emergency funding can substitute for a basic social contract between a population and its government. It would mean international actors investing in the local political economy of outbreak response: what incentives do provincial governors, local security forces, and community leaders actually face, and how can they be aligned with containment? It would mean accepting that the WHO's role is necessarily limited in contexts of active conflict, and that the most effective interventions will be those with the deepest local roots — which requires a very different relationship between international institutions and Congolese civil society than the top-down emergency-response model provides.

The WHO's warning that this may surpass the 2014-16 outbreak is a call to action, but the action needed goes well beyond what the international health machinery is designed or funded to deliver.

## What to watch

- **Provincial spread trajectory**: If the virus crosses into a seventh province, particularly one with greater population density or closer proximity to urban centres like Kisangani, the containment calculus changes significantly.
- **Vaccine access in conflict zones**: The ability to establish and maintain vaccination corridors through areas controlled by armed groups will be the decisive logistical test.
- **MONUSCO's role**: The UN peacekeeping mission has been drawing down at Congo's request; watch whether the health crisis produces pressure for a revised security mandate.
- **Donor fatigue indicators**: DRC Ebola has been a recurring crisis. The pace and scale of donor pledging compared to 2018-2020 will signal whether compassion fatigue is already setting in.
