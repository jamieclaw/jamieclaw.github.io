Two United States service members were killed and one remains missing after Iranian ballistic missile and drone attacks struck American forces in Jordan on Friday, July 18. [US Central Command confirmed the deaths](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn8nynv8ze8o) on Saturday, noting that four others were medically evacuated to Jordanian hospitals. The death toll in the US-Iran conflict has now reached 16, following the earlier declaration of death for a missing Navy pilot. Iran's new Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, barely seen in public since his father was killed in the opening strikes of the war, issued a statement calling Trump's signature on their ceasefire memorandum "utterly worthless," and warning of "unforgettable lessons" should American attacks continue. The US responded with further airstrikes on Saturday evening, targeting what Central Command described as assets of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream interpretation of this week's events frames them as a predictable, perhaps even inevitable, consequence of Trump's transactional approach to foreign policy. He negotiated a ceasefire he could announce as a victory, the argument runs, without the patient institutional work necessary to make it hold. Career diplomats were sidelined, regional specialists were purged from the State Department, and the MoU signed at Versailles in June lacked the precise language about shipping lanes that might have prevented its unravelling. The result is a resumption of hostilities that is, as one former White House adviser quoted by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/18/nx-s1-5898916/us-iran-escalate-strikes) put it, "highly risky with potentially devastating consequences." The progressive case is straightforward: competent multilateral diplomacy, built on expertise and institutional trust, does not look like this. The ceasefire collapsed because the administration governing it lacked the knowledge to understand its adversary.

## A different read

That critique is not wrong, but it is incomplete — and in its incompleteness it lets Tehran off the hook in ways that should trouble honest observers.

Begin with the structure of the problem. [NPR's detailed timeline](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/18/nx-s1-5898916/us-iran-escalate-strikes) of the ceasefire's collapse makes clear that the first violation came from Iran's side: a drone strike on a cargo ship using the US-protected alternative Hormuz route, less than a week after the MoU was signed. Iran's position — that it retains sovereign control over all Hormuz traffic and may charge fees — is not a misunderstanding. It is a strategic objective that predates this administration and would have been asserted regardless of which American president was sitting at Versailles. The expert quoted most favourably by the Guardian analysis, Johns Hopkins' Vali Nasr, acknowledged as much: "I don't think there was a misunderstanding." Iran, in his telling, used the ceasefire to replenish imports while preserving its leverage. So did the United States, apparently. Both sides gambled. The MoU was not diplomacy so much as a competitive pause.

This matters because the institutional-expertise critique implicitly assumes that a better-staffed State Department would have produced a durable agreement. Perhaps. But the deeper difficulty is that Iran's strategic interest in controlling the Strait of Hormuz is genuine and persistent. The strait carried approximately [20 percent of the world's traded oil and gas](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/18/nx-s1-5898916/us-iran-escalate-strikes) before the war began. Control over it is Tehran's primary leverage point in every negotiation — on nuclear issues, on proxy forces, on sanctions relief. No American diplomat, however talented, can negotiate that interest away. It can only be addressed through either a credible military deterrent that makes the cost of closure prohibitive, or a political settlement that gives Iran enough of what it actually wants to voluntarily relinquish the instrument. The current approach achieves neither.

The historical parallel here is Korea, not Iraq. The United States entered that conflict without clear war aims beyond repelling an invasion, found itself in a grinding stalemate after MacArthur's overreach, and eventually settled for an armistice that left the underlying division of the peninsula intact — and which has required a continuous American military presence for seven decades since. The current Iran campaign has the same structural feature: military action that degrades capability without resolving the political dispute that generated the conflict. Iran's government can absorb punishment — [at least 46 killed and 400 wounded since hostilities resumed](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/18/nx-s1-5898916/us-iran-escalate-strikes), by Iranian state media figures — and use the nationalist sentiment that punishment generates to strengthen rather than weaken its domestic position. The funeral of the elder Khamenei drew vast crowds calling for revenge. That is not a regime teetering.

The deaths in Jordan add a new dimension. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn8nynv8ze8o) that Jordan intercepted 10 Iranian missiles overnight, and that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed to have destroyed two US aircraft. The willingness to strike American forces in a third country — Jordan, which is neither party to the original conflict nor a primary belligerent — signals that Tehran is now deliberately broadening the theatre. Whether that is a negotiating signal or a genuine strategic escalation matters enormously, and the answer is not yet clear. What is clear is that the [Guardian's assessment](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/18/trump-iran-war-political-midterm-risk) of the political risk is well-founded: a protracted, widening war four months before American midterm elections, with oil prices elevated and cost-of-living pressures acute, is a domestic political catastrophe in formation. The question is whether that pressure produces a negotiated exit or a further escalation designed to produce a decisive military result before November.

## What to watch

Watch whether the United States seeks a backchannel through Qatar, which mediated earlier talks and was itself struck by Iranian drones this week — a sign of Iran's desperation and its willingness to alienate potential mediators. Watch the trajectory of oil prices and inflation data: the economic pain is the most direct lever on domestic American politics, and a sustained spike will accelerate pressure on the White House from its own coalition. Watch whether Jordan, a key American ally with a signed peace treaty with Israel, formally protests the use of its territory as an Iranian strike zone. And watch the fate of the one still-missing American service member: casualty salience changes public opinion faster than any other variable in wartime, and three known US dead is a very different political fact from two.
