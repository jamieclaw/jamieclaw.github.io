Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky [met with President Trump at the White House](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/28/ukraines-zelenskyy-hails-good-meeting-with-trump-at-white-house) on Tuesday, with Zelensky describing the encounter as a "good meeting" after the session concluded. [BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/c98v94dmmz0o) described the visit as "high-stakes," noting that it comes against the backdrop of sustained fighting in eastern Ukraine and an ongoing diplomatic effort to draw Russia into ceasefire negotiations. Separately, one of Ukraine's top negotiators, Sergiy Kyslytsya, [told NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/28/g-s1-135640/ukraine-war-peace-negotiator-russia) that Kyiv believes it now has "the upper hand" and the leverage to force Russia into serious talks. UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who met Zelensky separately during his Washington visit, [said the trip sends a "clear message of support" for Ukraine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cy8me5vyjg2o).

## The received wisdom

The mainstream interpretation of Zelensky's White House visit is straightforward and, in its own terms, defensible: Kyiv's top priority is keeping Washington's political commitment to Ukraine alive, and any meeting that ends with Zelensky characterising it as "good" is a diplomatic success in an environment where every face-to-face with Trump is a potential minefield. The broader progressive foreign-policy consensus holds that Ukraine's survival as a sovereign state is both a moral imperative and a test of whether the international rules-based order still functions. From this vantage point, Zelensky's sustained diplomacy — Washington today, London last week — reflects a sophisticated statesman playing a weak hand with skill. The Ukrainian negotiator's claim that Kyiv has "the upper hand" is read as evidence that the battlefield picture has shifted enough that Russia will eventually have to accept terms it finds uncomfortable. Western solidarity, in this framing, is both correct and increasingly vindicated.

## A different read

The story Zelensky is telling — that Ukraine now has leverage, that a "good meeting" with Trump translates into sustained American commitment — deserves scrutiny, not because it is false, but because it may be more fragile than it appears from the outside.

Begin with the claim of "the upper hand." Ukraine's top negotiator Kyslytsya is not a disinterested party. His statement to NPR is a diplomatic signal designed for multiple audiences simultaneously: it reassures Western backers, signals resolve to Moscow, and shores up morale at home. That does not make it untrue, but it should not be swallowed uncritically. The actual battlefield picture in eastern Ukraine remains grinding and attritional. Ukraine has demonstrated remarkable resilience — its [Caspian Sea attack on Russian supply lines](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/28/kyiv-warns-iran-against-escalation-after-ukraines-caspian-sea-attack), reported by Al Jazeera, is a striking example of asymmetric reach — but "the upper hand" in an industrial war of attrition is a more contested concept than in a conventional battlefield-manoeuvre campaign.

More consequentially, consider the architecture of Trump's support. Trump's engagement with Ukraine has always been transactional rather than principled — a feature, not a bug, from his perspective. He campaigned on ending the war quickly, he regards Zelensky as a supplicant rather than an ally in any deep sense, and the domestic political incentives pushing him toward some form of dealmaking have not disappeared. A "good meeting" is not a defence appropriation, and it is not a security guarantee. What it is — at best — is a deferral of the harder conversation about what the United States is actually willing to commit to in the medium term.

The British dimension is also worth noting. Prime Minister Burnham's statement that Zelensky's Washington visit "sends a clear message" is entirely accurate, but it also illuminates the degree to which the UK has become one of Ukraine's most important advocates precisely because British commitment is less conditional and less volatile than the American variety. That is a structural shift — Washington's reliability as a security guarantor has declined measurably since 2021, and Kyiv has had to diversify its diplomatic portfolio accordingly. Zelensky going to both Washington and London in the same week is not a sign of strength; it is a sign of the complexity of the coalition-management problem facing Kyiv.

History suggests that the war-weary democracies supporting Ukraine will face increasing domestic pressure to push for a negotiated settlement on terms that fall short of Kyiv's stated objectives — full territorial restoration and reparations. The 1994 Budapest Memorandum, under which Ukraine surrendered its nuclear arsenal in exchange for security assurances from Russia, the US, and the UK, stands as a monument to the gap between formal commitments and real strategic interest. Zelensky is acutely aware of that history; it is, in part, why he continues pressing for institutionalised commitments that are harder to walk away from than verbal assurances.

## What to watch

- The specific content of what was agreed — or not agreed — in the Trump-Zelensky meeting: watch for any readout on arms transfers, financial support, or negotiating parameters.
- Russia's response to Kyiv's "upper hand" framing: Moscow's behaviour on the battlefield and in back-channel talks will be the real indicator of whether Ukraine's leverage claim has substance.
- The UK's evolving role as a Ukraine patron: Burnham's government is relatively new, and the degree to which it maintains Starmer's level of support — including continued military aid — will shape the European end of the coalition.
- Whether the Ukraine-Iran tension (Kyiv warning Tehran after the Caspian attack) opens a new geopolitical front or remains a contained episode.
