Brent crude [crossed $100 a barrel](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2djnzrqk2o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) for the first time since May on Thursday, rising more than 6% in a single session as the conflict between the United States and Iran showed no sign of cooling. President Trump simultaneously warned that the [biggest American strikes on Iran yet](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/23/trump-warns-of-biggest-strikes-yet-on-iran-as-tehran-lashes-out-across-gulf) were coming, while Tehran demonstrated it still retained meaningful offensive reach by [attacking targets in Bahrain, Kuwait, and Jordan](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/23/trump-warns-of-biggest-strikes-yet-on-iran-as-tehran-lashes-out-across-gulf). Simultaneously, [Yemen's Houthis attacked a Saudi tanker](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cpw9xzx9r4ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as part of a declared maritime embargo against Riyadh. The US war on Iran has already cost [close to $110 billion](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/23/us-war-on-iran-the-110-billion-price-tag?traffic_source=rss), with the Trump administration seeking a further $67 billion from Congress. The economic and strategic consequences of this price shock are now arriving simultaneously.

## The received wisdom

The dominant reading in foreign-policy and financial press circles is largely one of regret — but a regret directed firmly at Tehran. The argument runs roughly as follows: Iran, through its support for Hezbollah, the Houthis, and various Iraqi militia proxies, had for years been conducting a slow-motion war against the American-backed regional order. American restraint, so the narrative goes, only emboldened that campaign. When the Trump administration finally chose to respond with force, it was doing what previous presidents — Obama and Biden notably — had declined to do: call the regime's bluff. The price spike, on this reading, is painful but finite. Once Iranian leadership calculates that the military cost exceeds any gain from continued escalation, a negotiated pause or even a lasting agreement becomes more likely. Energy markets, deep and adaptive, will re-route. The $100 price is a cost of finally taking Iran seriously, not a symptom of strategic failure.

There is something to this. Iranian adventurism in the Gulf has never been consequence-free; the question has always been whether the cost imposed matched the provocation. Previous American administrations demonstrably let the asymmetry widen.

## A different read

That said, the current trajectory deserves more scepticism than Washington's hawkish commentariat is currently supplying.

The oil price is not simply a market inconvenience. At $100 a barrel, the cost of the war is being socialised globally and borne disproportionately by economies with no stake in the conflict. [UK mortgage rates rose to a one-month high](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c70gknr7z1eo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) partly as a consequence of Middle East tensions feeding into gilt yields and lender costs. That transmission mechanism runs through every importing economy on earth — from India to South Korea to every country in sub-Saharan Africa that runs diesel generators to make up for grid deficits. The war's costs, in other words, are not merely American.

More troubling is the strategic logic. Trump has now threatened the "biggest strikes yet" — language that suggests escalation dominance is the operating theory. The assumption is that Iran will eventually break under pressure. Historically, this assumption has had a mixed record. The Iran-Iraq War of 1980–1988, which Iran fought to something approaching a draw despite being outgunned, outnumbered, and internationally isolated for most of the conflict, is a reminder that the Islamic Republic has demonstrated unusual capacity for absorbing punishment. Regimes that derive their legitimacy from resistance narratives are peculiarly difficult to coerce through air power alone.

The Houthis attacking a Saudi tanker — part of a declared maritime embargo — illustrates how the conflict is simultaneously widening its geography and its target set. [The Houthis have now announced a formal maritime embargo against Saudi Arabia](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cpw9xzx9r4ko?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), a remarkable escalation that directly threatens the Bab al-Mandab strait, through which roughly 15% of global trade passes. This is not peripheral. The Red Sea corridor is not easily defended, and American naval assets are already stretched between multiple theatres.

What Iran's attacks on Bahrain, Kuwait, and Jordan reveal is that Tehran is deliberately widening the circle of pain — drawing Gulf states that had hoped to remain bystanders into the conflict. This is standard Iranian deterrence logic: make the cost of American alliance membership high enough to induce neutrality or defection from the US-led coalition. Kuwait and Bahrain hosting American bases suddenly looks less like a security guarantee and more like a liability. That psychological shift — if it takes hold — is far more damaging to American regional architecture than any single Iranian missile strike.

The $110 billion bill, with another $67 billion request pending, also deserves scrutiny at a moment when the US fiscal position remains precarious. The war is not funded; it is borrowed. Future generations of American taxpayers will pay interest on debt incurred to conduct strikes whose long-term strategic effect remains unproven. A school of hard-nosed realism — Kissinger-ian rather than neoconservative — would ask whether Iran's nuclear programme has actually been set back more by this campaign than by the JCPOA diplomacy it replaced, and whether the answer is honestly knowable.

None of this is to say the strikes were wrong — that debate is genuinely open. But the trajectory from "limited strikes on Iran's nuclear infrastructure" to "biggest strikes yet" against a regime still capable of attacking multiple Gulf states suggests the original mission scope has already expanded considerably. Wars that begin with clear, limited objectives and end with "biggest yet" rhetoric have a history of turning into something their architects did not intend.

## What to watch

- **The $67 billion supplemental request**: whether Congress funds it, and on what conditions. The House already passed a non-binding resolution to end the war; watch whether that sentiment hardens into appropriations riders.
- **Gulf-state signalling**: any quiet approach by Kuwait or Bahrain toward Tehran would indicate the coalition is under more strain than public statements suggest.
- **OPEC+ emergency meeting**: Saudi Arabia and the UAE have capacity to partially offset the supply shock; whether they deploy it signals their assessment of the war's duration.
- **The $100 floor**: if crude stays above this level for more than a few weeks, watch for inflationary pass-through in Western central bank guidance — a second-order cost of a war that was sold to the public as primarily a national security matter.
