Wildfires are burning simultaneously across at least six European countries this week, forcing mass evacuations, killing civilians, and prompting France to deploy military assets. [French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu was heckled on Monday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2rzx5g5yro) during a visit to Le Porge, a town in the Gironde region where 183 homes were destroyed, after residents accused him of failing to meet with them and of mismanaging fire operations. Lecornu announced €12 million in emergency aid for affected French regions. In Belgium, a wildfire in the Hautes Fagnes nature reserve — the country's largest — entered its fourth day, with firefighting support arriving from the Netherlands, Germany, Norway, and Sweden. In Greece, 41 fires broke out in the past 24 hours, two people died and hundreds were evacuated from the island of Salamina. In Spain's Aragón region, a soldier died fighting the Peñas de Riglos fire, [with nearly 16,000 hectares burned](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2rzx5g5yro), and in Croatia, four people were arrested on suspicion of arson. [The EU's climate monitoring service Copernicus confirmed](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2rzx5g5yro) that western Europe experienced its hottest June-to-July period on record.

## The received wisdom

The overwhelming narrative from European media and political class this week is that the fires are, at root, a climate story: human-caused warming has extended drought seasons, raised temperatures, and created conditions in which previously manageable fire risks become uncontrollable. From this perspective, the political lesson is clear — accelerate the net-zero transition, fund climate adaptation measures, and hold national governments accountable for failing to invest sufficiently in both emissions reduction and resilience. The Guardian and much of the European press have run alongside fire coverage long features on satellite imagery of "scorched landscapes" and on farmers facing an "unprecedented crisis" due to successive heatwaves. [European farmers themselves have called for urgent government action](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/aug/17/european-farmers-unprecedented-crisis-successive-heatwaves), warning of "catastrophic" harvests. Politicians who, like UK Reform deputy Richard Tice, [suggested people should "enjoy" the heatwave](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/aug/17/reform-uk-richard-tice-stop-tackling-climate-crisis-enjoy-heat) have been roundly criticised by nurses and environmental campaigners.

## A different read

None of this is dishonest. The climate signal is real: Europe's summers are hotter, its soils drier, its fire seasons longer. But the "climate made them do it" frame, applied wholesale to every fire season, has become a mechanism for avoiding the harder and more politically awkward questions about land management, forestry policy, building codes, and emergency preparedness that are equally implicated in why fires are this destructive. Climate changes the ambient risk level; governance determines whether that risk level translates into catastrophe.

Take the French situation. The fires around Bordeaux and Gironde are burning through exactly the kind of dense pine monoculture — largely maritime pine planted for timber in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries — that fire scientists and land managers have been warning about for years. Monoculture plantations with minimal controlled burning regimes are tinderboxes. The EU's Common Agricultural Policy has historically subsidised the maintenance of these landscapes over their active management. France, like most of southern Europe, does not have a culture of prescribed burning comparable to what Indigenous Australians practiced for millennia, and what Western Australian and Californian authorities have belatedly begun to institutionalise. If France had invested a fraction of its Gironde fire-emergency budget into the kind of mosaic landscape management that breaks up contiguous fuel loads, some of those 183 homes would still be standing. That is a governance failure, not a climate failure.

The Belgian case is different in kind but similar in structure. The Hautes Fagnes is a peat bog ecosystem — in normal climatic conditions, one of the wettest landscapes in northern Europe. The fact that it is now burning is a genuine climate anomaly: peat fires require soil moisture deficits of extraordinary depth. But the response capacity gap — Belgium needed to call in aerial assets from four other countries — points to a defence and emergency preparedness budget that has been, like those of most European states, raided over decades to fund social programmes. The NATO two-percent defence spending commitment was only recently achieved by most European members, and even that figure does not include civil emergency preparedness. When Belgium's largest nature reserve requires Swedish and Norwegian helicopters to fight its fires, something has gone wrong with the basic architecture of national resilience, and it is not primarily a story about greenhouse gas emissions.

The political economy of these fires also deserves scrutiny. Property developers, agricultural lobbies, and tourism authorities have all historically resisted the kind of firebreak maintenance and building setback regulations that reduce structural vulnerability to fire. In Greece — [where dozens of fires broke out in 24 hours](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2rzx5g5yro) — illegal construction in wildland-urban interface zones has been a documented problem for decades, with successive governments reluctant to enforce demolition orders against politically connected landowners. The fires that kill people in Greece most often burn through exactly those illegal structures that should never have been built. Climate does not permit illegal building. Politicians do.

This is not a counsel of inaction on emissions — getting the long-run temperature trajectory under control matters enormously. But the conflation of every immediate fire catastrophe with the need for accelerated net-zero targets, to the exclusion of the land-management, preparedness, and legal-enforcement conversations, is a form of political convenience. It allows governments to point to a problem that requires decades to solve (emissions) rather than admit the failures they could have fixed last year (building codes, prescribed burning, emergency capacity).

## What to watch

- Whether the EU's Civil Protection Mechanism triggers formal reviews of member state emergency capacity — the Belgian case may be the catalyst that forces a serious audit.
- French political fallout: Lecornu's heckled visit is politically significant; whether it becomes a defining image of his premiership depends on whether the rebuilding commitments are kept.
- Arson prosecutions in Croatia: if the four arrests yield convictions with evidence of coordinated fire-setting, it raises separate questions about organised arson that are distinct from the climate conversation.
- The European farmers' lobbying push: if the harvest shortfall is severe enough, expect pressure for emergency CAP payments that will test the EU budget's fiscal headroom.
