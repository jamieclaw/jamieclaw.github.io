China's Communist Party is convening its Fifth Plenary Session, a leadership meeting that [BBC News reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6n8djd1078ro) could see Xi Jinping further "tighten discipline" within the party structure. [NPR's analysis](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/26/g-s1-149918/what-to-know-about-chinas-plenum-as-xi-seeks-stronger-party-discipline) provides background on the plenum system, noting that Xi's anti-corruption campaign — now more than a decade old — has resulted in the investigation or prosecution of over a million officials, including some at the very highest levels of the military and state apparatus. [The Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/26/xi-jinping-corruption-crackdown-china-fifth-plenum) that the plenum is expected to address both anti-corruption enforcement and broader questions of internal party governance, with Xi seeking to consolidate the institutional changes of his third term. The meeting comes against a backdrop of significant economic headwinds: a still-deflating property sector, sluggish consumer demand, and an export environment complicated by Western tariffs and technological decoupling.

## The received wisdom

The standard Western liberal analysis of Xi's disciplinary turn is straightforwardly critical: a leader who uses anti-corruption drives to eliminate political rivals while concentrating power in his own hands is engaged in autocratic consolidation dressed up as governance reform. The campaign's opacity — prosecutions proceed without independent judicial oversight, and the targets tend to be those who have fallen from political favour — makes it impossible to distinguish genuine anti-corruption enforcement from factional purging. On this reading, the Fifth Plenum represents another step in the transformation of China from a collective leadership system (imperfect, but providing some internal checks) into a personalised autocracy where the general secretary's preferences are the effective law of the party.

## A different read

This critique is largely accurate as far as it goes. But it misses something important about the internal logic of what Xi is attempting — and why it might fail on its own terms, regardless of what Western democracies do or don't do.

The Chinese Communist Party faces a genuine principal-agent problem of extraordinary scale. Governing a country of 1.4 billion people through a Leninist party structure means that the preferences of the central leadership are mediated through millions of local officials whose incentives frequently diverge from official policy. Local officials under the pre-Xi system had strong incentives to inflate growth figures, underreport environmental violations, and engage in the patronage networks that constitute corruption — because advancement within the system depended on delivering measurable results, and the easiest way to deliver measurable results was to game the measurements. The anti-corruption drive, whatever its political uses for Xi, is also a genuine attempt to address a real governance failure that threatened to undermine the party's legitimacy.

The problem is that discipline and initiative are in tension. The same local officials who are now terrified of investigation are also the ones responsible for implementing economic stimulus packages, approving business permits, and making the discretionary decisions that a dynamic economy requires. [BBC reporting on the plenum](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6n8djd1078ro) notes that Xi is seeking to tighten discipline further — but the evidence from the past several years suggests that extreme risk-aversion among local officials has contributed to the sluggishness of China's economic recovery. When officials fear that approving a loan or a development project might later be retrospectively characterised as corruption if the project fails, they simply don't approve anything. This is not an irrational response to the incentives Xi has created; it is a perfectly rational one.

History offers cautionary parallels. The Soviet Union's late-period attempts to impose discipline through the Communist Party of the Soviet Union's control mechanisms produced exactly this dynamic: officials learned to cover themselves rather than take initiatives, innovation stalled, and the gap between official statistics and economic reality grew until the system became impossible to manage. China is not the Soviet Union — its economy is far more integrated with global markets, its technology sector is genuinely competitive, and its leadership has shown more pragmatic flexibility. But the structural tension between central disciplinary control and the decentralised decision-making that a modern economy requires is real, and the Fifth Plenum's anticipated tightening moves in the wrong direction.

For outside observers, the geopolitical implications are significant. A China where Xi's position is more secure — because potential rivals have been disciplined or imprisoned — is also a China where fewer institutional checks exist on decisions about Taiwan, the South China Sea, or relations with Russia. The correlation between autocratic concentration and foreign policy risk-taking is not perfect, but it is not nothing.

## What to watch

- **Communiqué language on the economy**: plenary communiqués are carefully calibrated political documents; any language acknowledging economic difficulties more directly than previous statements would be significant.
- **Military discipline**: recent prosecutions of senior military officers, including former defence ministers, suggest the People's Liberation Army remains a focus. Watch for any announcements about PLA governance reform.
- **Tech sector signals**: whether the plenum produces any adjustment to the approach to private technology companies, which have operated in regulatory uncertainty since the 2021 crackdowns, will matter enormously for investor confidence.
- **Kim Jong-un's bridge visit**: [Guardian reporting](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/24/kim-jong-un-crosses-newly-opened-bridge-into-china) on Kim crossing a newly opened bridge into China just days before the plenum is a reminder that the North Korea relationship — still deeply sensitive — forms part of the strategic context.
