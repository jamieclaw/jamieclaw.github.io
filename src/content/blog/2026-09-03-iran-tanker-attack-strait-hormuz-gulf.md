Saudi Arabia's Foreign Ministry confirmed on Wednesday that Iran had targeted the Sidr, a tanker owned by Saudi national carrier Bahri, while it was transiting the Strait of Hormuz on Monday. [Two Filipino sailors were killed](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/2/saudi-arabia-condemns-deadly-iranian-attack-on-tanker-in-strait-of-hormuz) in the attack; a second tanker was also struck. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed the vessels had hit mines. Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Qatar all issued formal condemnations, with Qatar calling the attack a "flagrant violation of the rules of international law and freedom of maritime navigation" and rejecting the use of the Strait as a "bargaining chip." The incident is the latest in a series of Iranian attacks on Gulf-state vessels — including a supertanker hit in July and two Bahri ships struck in the Red Sea — since the US-Israeli war with Iran began on February 28. Before that war, roughly 20 percent of the world's oil passed through the Strait of Hormuz.

## The received wisdom

The dominant analysis of Iran's tanker campaign treats it as an act of desperation — the behaviour of a regime under extreme military and economic pressure, lashing out at its neighbours because it cannot directly retaliate against the United States at scale. On this reading, the attacks are strategically counterproductive: they alienate Gulf states that might otherwise have remained neutral, they give Washington additional justification for continued operations, and they have not succeeded in shutting down Strait traffic entirely, since the US maintains a "counter-blockade" and insists the waterway remains passable. The condemnations from Riyadh, Doha, and Kuwait City are treated as proof that Iran is losing the regional information war as decisively as it is losing the military one. Saudi Arabia's condemnation is particularly notable, given that Riyadh has historically been cautious about openly confronting Tehran.

## A different read

That analysis may be too sanguine. Iran's tanker campaign is better understood as a coercive signalling strategy than as either desperation or pure retaliation. The targets are not random: Bahri is the Saudi national shipping carrier, and the Wedyan, Masa, and Amzan — all Bahri vessels — have been struck at different points over the summer. Iran is sending a specific message to Saudi Arabia: the cost of permitting US operations from Saudi territory, or of maintaining energy exports that fund the coalition against Iran, will be borne by Saudi shipping and Saudi lives. The two Filipino sailors who died on the Sidr are a human tragedy; they are also, from Tehran's perspective, a demonstration that Gulf states cannot simply observe this war from the sidelines.

There is a historical parallel in the "Tanker War" of the 1980s, when Iran and Iraq both targeted each other's oil infrastructure and shipping — and eventually each other's Gulf neighbours' vessels — to impose economic costs and fracture international neutrality. That conflict ultimately drew the US Navy into the Gulf (Operation Earnest Will, 1987-88) and ended with a ceasefire that left neither side having achieved its maximalist objectives. The difference today is that the US is already a direct party to the conflict, which removes the ambiguity that once gave Tehran some diplomatic cover.

What should concern analysts more than the individual attacks is the aggregate effect on Gulf-state calculations. Saudi Arabia [condemned](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/2/saudi-arabia-condemns-deadly-iranian-attack-on-tanker-in-strait-of-hormuz) the Sidr attack in firm diplomatic language. But the kingdom has also made clear for months that it is not a formal party to the US-Israeli campaign and has privately expressed discomfort with the war's open-endedness. Qatar, which hosts the largest US air base in the region, called the Strait a bargaining chip — language that implies some sympathy for the argument that the waterway's closure is a consequence of the war rather than simply Iranian aggression. Each tanker attack incrementally raises the domestic political cost for Gulf leaders of remaining aligned with Washington.

Trump's suggestion earlier this week that the Strait of Hormuz should be renamed the "Trump Strait" — reported by Al Jazeera — is a reminder that American strategy in the Gulf is not being driven purely by strategic calculation. Symbolic gestures about renaming international waterways do not reassure Gulf allies; they generate headlines and signal to Riyadh and Doha that Washington is operating on a different register. A war that began with clear, if contested, strategic objectives — degrading Iran's nuclear programme — has expanded into a conflict with no articulated endgame, conducted by an administration that appears to regard the Middle East primarily as a venue for domestic political theatre.

The Strait of Hormuz remains the chokepoint on which the global economy depends. Before the war, approximately 20 million barrels of oil per day transited it. [Traffic has slowed considerably](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/2/saudi-arabia-condemns-deadly-iranian-attack-on-tanker-in-strait-of-hormuz), contributing to the global bond market turmoil that BBC Economics Editor Faisal Islam has described as a "wildfire." Every tanker attack is a reminder that the war's costs are being borne not just by the combatants but by the entire global economy.

## What to watch

- Whether Saudi Arabia moves from condemnation to concrete steps — withdrawal of logistical support, diplomatic mediation, or quiet back-channel communication with Tehran — as tanker losses mount.
- The US counter-blockade: if Iran's mining campaign degrades it enough to reduce safe transit materially, the pressure on Washington to either escalate or negotiate will intensify.
- Qatar's diplomatic signalling: Doha has historically maintained back-channels to Tehran, and its language about the Strait as a "bargaining chip" may be a signal of willingness to facilitate talks.
- Oil price behaviour: a sustained Strait closure or major tanker incident would push prices well above current levels, accelerating the global bond yield spiral that is already threatening fiscal stability in the UK, US, and France.
