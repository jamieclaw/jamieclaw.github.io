Serbia goes to the polls this weekend in an election that has brought the country to one of the most genuinely uncertain moments in its post-Yugoslav history. After months of student-led protests that have drawn hundreds of thousands into the streets, President Aleksandar Vučić — who has dominated Serbian politics for more than a decade with a brand of nationalist populism that maintains EU aspirations while keeping the Kremlin close — faces a neck-and-neck contest that could produce the first transfer of power since his Serbian Progressive Party consolidated its grip in 2012. [The BBC describes it](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm1dwz311476o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) as a student movement taking Vučić "to the wire." [Al Jazeera frames it as a country where students may actually take power](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/10/24/serbian-elections-a-country-where-students-may-actually-take-power). The scale of civic mobilisation is remarkable; whether it translates into institutional change is another question.

## The received wisdom

The dominant Western commentary on Serbia runs roughly as follows: Vučić is a soft authoritarian who has captured the media, weaponised the judiciary, cultivated ethnic nationalism, maintained an ambiguous relationship with Vladimir Putin — accepting EU money while refusing to sanction Russia after the invasion of Ukraine — and rigged the political environment sufficiently to ensure his own longevity. The student protests, sparked initially by a deadly infrastructure collapse at Novi Sad railway station last November that killed fifteen people and which protesters attributed to corruption, represent an authentic civic awakening. If the opposition wins or even comes close, it would signal that even hybrid autocracies are vulnerable to mass civic pressure. The European Union and Western capitals would celebrate it as vindication of democratic norms in the Balkans.

This framing is largely fair. Vučić has systematically undermined independent institutions. Press freedom in Serbia has deteriorated sharply. His government's corruption problem is not invented by students; it is structural, tied to a construction sector riddled with politically-connected contracts. The Novi Sad disaster was not an accident — it was the predictable consequence of that system.

## A different read

But the celebration of the student movement, however warranted in its diagnosis of Vučić's failures, needs to be accompanied by a harder question: what comes after?

The history of Eastern European transitions is littered with civic uprisings that successfully removed one form of concentrated power only to install another. The 2004 Orange Revolution in Ukraine produced Viktor Yushchenko, who proved incapable of governing, and eventually Yanukovych returned. Georgia's Rose Revolution of 2003 brought in Mikheil Saakashvili, who combined genuine reformist energy with a tendency toward authoritarian improvisation. Romania's long series of anti-corruption protests produced a political class that remained stubbornly resistant to real reform. Removing a corrupt government requires a civic coalition; building the institutions that prevent the next one requires something much harder to sustain: patient, boring, technocratic work over years.

Serbia's opposition is ideologically diverse in ways that make governing together difficult. Some factions are genuinely pro-European and reform-minded. Others share Vučić's ethnic nationalism but want the benefits without the baggage of his specific corrupt networks. The Kosovo question — Serbia's refusal to recognise Kosovan independence, a posture backed by Russia and China, which keeps Belgrade in an uncomfortable position relative to EU membership — does not go away with a change of government. Any new Serbian government will face the same structural bind: its electorate does not want to recognise Kosovo; the EU requires recognition as a condition of membership. Vučić managed this contradiction through studied ambiguity. A new government will have to manage it too.

There is also a geopolitical dimension that Western commentary tends to understate. Serbia sits between the EU, Hungary's Orbán bloc, Turkey, and Russia. It has historical ties to China that go back to Tito's non-aligned movement. A democratic Serbia is genuinely in the West's interest; but pushing too hard — conditioning every interaction on EU membership benchmarks, treating every Balkan election as a test of loyalty — tends to produce backlash nationalism of exactly the Vučić variety. The lesson of the post-Cold War Balkans is that conditionality works when it is backed by credible membership prospects and tangible economic benefits. When Brussels talks of "European values" while keeping Serbia in a twenty-year accession waiting room, its moral authority is limited.

The student protesters deserve respect and solidarity. But outside observers should resist the temptation to treat this election as a morality play in which the right side winning solves Serbia's problems. What it potentially does is open a space in which those problems become addressable. That is significant — but only significant if what follows is serious institution-building, not just a change of faces.

## What to watch

- **Coalition durability**: If the opposition wins, watch whether the student movement's energy sustains into a governing coalition or fragments along pre-existing ideological lines within months.
- **Kosovo signal**: The new government's first statement on Kosovo recognition will tell you immediately how much foreign policy space a change of government actually creates.
- **Vučić's response**: Whether he accepts a defeat cleanly — or, in the event of a narrow loss, contests it — will reveal how much of Serbia's democratic infrastructure has actually survived his decade in power.
- **EU response**: Watch whether Brussels translates an opposition victory into concrete acceleration of accession talks, or delivers another round of "encouraging progress" language without substance.
