The COP31 climate summit has [hit a wall over fossil fuels](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6ly5w40eg07o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), with negotiations stalling as developing nations and petrostates contest the terms under which coal, oil, and gas should be phased out. The deadlock mirrors the collapse of key sessions at earlier COP summits and comes despite — or arguably because of — China's separate announcement that it [commits to low-carbon electrification](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/21/china-commits-low-carbon-electrification-help-others-improve) while pledging to help others improve, a formulation Beijing has used before to great diplomatic effect. Thousands of Europeans have been [protesting over living costs and housing](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/thousands-protest-across-europe-over-living-costs-and-housing?traffic_source=rss), a reminder that the political constituency for rapid energy transition is competing with an equally vocal constituency demanding affordable heating and petrol. Meanwhile in Washington, [a Senate permitting bill that would speed up renewable energy deployment](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/21/nx-s1-6046482/senate-permitting-bill-speeds-up-renewables-divides-greens-over-oil-and-gas) is itself dividing environmentalists because it also facilitates oil and gas projects — a microcosm of the fundamental tension that is paralyzing Bonn.

## The received wisdom

The standard climate commentary will attribute the deadlock to the usual villains: oil-producing states protecting their balance sheets, a Trump administration that has abandoned American climate leadership, and an international system insufficiently empowered to override narrow national interests. China's pledge, in this reading, is a genuine and welcome commitment from the world's largest emitter, and the failure of the summit to build on it reflects the structural veto power of fossil fuel interests over multilateral institutions. The solution, the argument runs, is stronger binding commitments, more robust climate finance for developing nations, and — always — greater political will. The protests across Europe are a temporary friction that better policy design and green job creation can address.

## A different read

The deadlock at COP31 is not a failure of will; it is a collision with arithmetic. The global energy transition is proceeding — solar and wind deployment figures are genuinely impressive — but it is proceeding alongside continued growth in fossil fuel demand, not instead of it. The world added more renewable capacity last year than ever before and also consumed more oil and gas than ever before. These facts coexist because energy demand is rising faster than the renewable buildout, particularly in the Global South, where the expectation that the West will fund an accelerated transition has repeatedly met with underwhelming cheque-writing.

The COP process was not designed to resolve this tension. It was designed to produce communiqués. The difference between a successful COP and a failed one is not whether emissions fall — they are measured over decades — but whether the assembled diplomats can agree on language. That language game has real costs: it consumes enormous political bandwidth, it creates the illusion of action where there is mostly process, and it generates accountability-free commitments that can be — and regularly are — revised or abandoned without consequence.

China's announcement deserves particular scrutiny. Beijing has become adept at making climate pledges that are significant enough to earn diplomatic credit while remaining consistent with continued coal expansion at home. Its commitment to help others improve their low-carbon electrification is admirable in principle; in practice, the BRI energy infrastructure China is building across Asia and Africa remains heavily weighted toward coal and gas. Calling this a commitment to the global transition while continuing to export the infrastructure of fossil dependence is not dishonesty precisely — it is the kind of strategic ambiguity that multilateral forums reward.

The European cost-of-living protests carry a political message that climate negotiators ignore at their peril. Energy policy is not an abstract good; it is paid for in electricity bills and petrol prices. The voters demanding relief from housing and living costs are the same voters whose governments are committing to carbon taxes, appliance bans, and accelerated fossil fuel phase-outs. The political coalitions sustaining ambitious climate policy in Europe are fragile, and each winter of high energy prices frays them further. That is not a reason to abandon decarbonisation — it is a reason to build a transition that people can actually live through, which requires being honest about the pace at which fossil fuels can realistically be replaced.

The American Senate permitting bill is in this sense more interesting than the COP process. It is trying to do something practically useful — speed up the construction of transmission lines and renewable plants — while accepting the political reality that it must also ease some oil and gas permitting to pass. That it divides environmentalists who prefer purity to progress is a feature of the political landscape that the COP framework cannot change by passing another resolution.

## What to watch

The key indicator at COP31 is not whether a final communiqué is reached — some form of text almost always emerges — but what it says about the timeline for fossil fuel phase-out and what enforcement mechanism, if any, accompanies it. Any language that lacks a binding mechanism is worth approximately the paper it's printed on; any language that includes one will be blocked by the states that have not ratified it. Watch also for the climate finance numbers: the gap between what developing nations were promised in earlier summits and what has been delivered is the central grievance driving the deadlock, and no amount of rhetorical ambition from wealthy countries will close it without actual money.
