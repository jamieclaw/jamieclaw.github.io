The United Kingdom and France have quietly scrapped what was marketed as a reciprocal migrant returns arrangement — the so-called "one in, one out" scheme under which the UK would accept one asylum seeker legally for every irregular Channel crosser returned to France. The [BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c64gvnv4eqylo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the scheme has been abandoned without a replacement framework announced. The collapse comes as the UK government faces continued pressure from both the Home Office and domestic public opinion on small-boat crossings, which have remained stubbornly high despite successive bilateral and multilateral initiatives. The scrapping follows what officials on both sides described as implementation difficulties — a diplomatic circumlocution that covers a range of political failures, from disagreements on eligibility criteria to French domestic sensitivity about being seen to cooperate with British externalisation of migration management.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-humanitarian reading of this collapse is that the scheme was always inadequate to the scale of the problem, offered no safe legal pathways worth the name, and treated asylum seekers as bargaining chips in a bilateral political dispute rather than as individuals with rights under international law. On this view, the energy wasted on deterrence theatre — "one in, one out," offshore processing schemes, pushback rhetoric — would be better directed toward addressing root causes in countries of origin and building a coherent EU-UK post-Brexit migration framework. The argument is not without merit. The evidence base for deterrence as a durable solution to refugee flows is thin, and much of what passes for border policy in Westminster is better described as political performance for domestic audiences.

## A different read

Granting all that, the collapse of this scheme illustrates a different failure — the persistent inability of progressive governance to construct an alternative that actually reduces irregular migration, as opposed to one that is morally superior on paper. The UK has now been through the Dubs Amendment, the Rwanda policy, the "one in, one out" arrangement, various bilateral French-payments-for-enforcement deals and multiple treaty conversations, all of which have either been judicially blocked, politically abandoned or simply failed to reduce crossings. That three-consecutive-government failure — spanning Conservative and Labour administrations — should prompt some reflection on whether the problem is primarily one of policy design or of structural limits.

The structural limit is this: the English Channel is a short, crossing-feasible stretch of water between a wealthy country with a common-law asylum system, English as its language and a large informal economy, and a continent where secondary movement of asylum seekers is both widespread and difficult to stop at source. No bilateral "returns" scheme can address that arithmetic without French willingness to operationalise returns at scale — willingness that French domestic politics has consistently failed to generate. France has its own reasons for this: returns require receiving-country cooperation at origin, which is diplomatically expensive; they require domestic legal infrastructure that intersects uncomfortably with French constitutional protections; and the French electorate, while increasingly hostile to irregular migration, has not yet produced a government willing to absorb the reputational costs of being seen as Britain's enforcement arm.

The historically literate comparison here is with the Sangatte protocol of the late 1990s, when the first Eurotunnel-adjacent camp was generating comparable political heat. That crisis was resolved not by a durable bilateral framework but by the closure of a specific facility — which displaced the problem rather than resolving it. The pattern since has been consistent: each new agreement addresses a specific node in the migration network, driving flows to adjacent routes rather than reducing aggregate numbers. This is a systems problem, and bilateral deals are, by design, subsystem solutions.

There is a harder question lurking here about what British and French governments actually want. The [BBC's political coverage](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c64gvnv4eqylo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) of the scheme's collapse does not dwell on this, but it is the relevant question: both governments benefit, in different ways, from the continuation of a problem that allows them to signal toughness without delivering results. The Conservatives built an entire electoral strategy around Rwanda that was never meant to be implemented at scale. Labour has inherited the same dynamic. Meanwhile, the people crossing in dinghies remain in genuine danger, and the communities receiving them in Kent and elsewhere face genuine pressures that are not addressed by diplomatic communiqués.

What a serious policy would look like is a longer conversation — it would involve fast-track asylum processing, genuine safe-and-legal pathways linked to managed labour migration, and a post-Brexit relationship with the EU on returns that is currently politically impossible. That conversation is not happening in Westminster. What is happening instead is a succession of schemes, each announced with fanfare and collapsed in silence.

## What to watch

- **Replacement framework announcements**: Whether Burnham or the Home Secretary announce a successor bilateral arrangement before the autumn statement; the silence so far suggests no replacement is imminent.
- **Channel crossing numbers through October**: The autumn weather window typically closes in November; any surge in the remaining weeks will ratchet up parliamentary pressure.
- **Labour conference rhetoric vs. policy substance**: Whether the party's home affairs language translates into any concrete legislative proposal, or remains aspirational.
