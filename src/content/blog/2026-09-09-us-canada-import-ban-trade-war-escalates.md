The United States has imposed an import ban on Canadian alcohol and a range of other goods, the latest and most sweeping escalation in a trade dispute that has been building since the Trump administration's tariff offensive earlier this year. [Canada had already responded with its own retaliatory tariffs](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/07/canada-tariffs-us-trump), which took effect this week, prompting Washington's counter-move. Beer producer Sapporo, which operates significant Canadian brewing capacity, [has already announced it will shift some production from Canada to the United States](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c87ve09pgqzo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) to avoid the tariff friction. Former Canadian finance minister Chrystia Freeland, who once sat across the negotiating table from Trump's team, [has been watching the deterioration closely](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/08/nx-s1-5955613/chrystia-freeland-canada-us-trade-war-tariffs), noting that the current trajectory was foreseeable but that the speed and totality of the breakdown has surprised even seasoned observers.

## The received wisdom

The liberal-internationalist reading of this moment is straightforward and not without merit: Donald Trump is engaging in economic nationalism of the most disruptive kind, weaponising trade policy against America's closest ally and largest trading partner in ways that damage both economies. Canada did not start this fight; it responded proportionately and within the norms of international trade law. The import ban on Canadian alcohol — culturally loaded, economically significant — is a punitive measure that hurts Canadian producers, yes, but also raises costs for American consumers who enjoy Canadian whisky and beer. The costs of this trade war, [as analysts have noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z4dv6vn2o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), will fall disproportionately on working-class Americans who buy cheaper imported goods and middle-income Canadians employed in export industries. From this view, Trump is indulging a zero-sum mercantilist fantasy that mainstream economists across the ideological spectrum rejected decades ago.

## A different read

All of that is substantially true, and yet it misses the more important question: *why* is this happening, and what does it reveal about the structural weaknesses that made it possible?

Begin with the economic architecture. The US-Canada-Mexico Agreement, the renegotiated NAFTA that Trump himself championed in his first term, was supposed to provide durable rules for North American trade. It demonstrably has not. What we are witnessing is not the failure of free trade per se, but the failure of trade agreements that lack credible enforcement mechanisms and rely on the goodwill of the parties. When one party decides the rules no longer suit it, the rules dissolve. This is the oldest lesson in international relations, and liberal internationalists have been stubbornly reluctant to learn it.

Second, consider the domestic politics on the American side. Trump's tariff offensive is not irrational from a political-economy perspective. The communities that feel most aggrieved — deindustrialised Rust Belt towns, agricultural regions that lost export markets, manufacturing workers who watched factories close — have legitimate grievances that predated Trump and will outlast him. The bipartisan consensus on free trade from the 1990s through the 2000s genuinely failed to account for distributional effects. When Chrystia Freeland [reflects on her own negotiations](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/08/nx-s1-5955613/chrystia-freeland-canada-us-trade-war-tariffs), she is in part grappling with the limitations of technocratic trade management, the belief that if the aggregate numbers are positive, the political fallout can be managed. They couldn't.

Third, and most uncomfortably for the free-trade right, Canada is not entirely an innocent party. Its supply management system for dairy and poultry is a protectionist regime that has long infuriated American agricultural exporters. Its cultural content rules have been a persistent source of friction. None of this justifies an import ban on alcohol, which is retaliatory rather than structural, but it does complicate the narrative of a righteous Canada versus a bullying America. Trade disputes of this kind almost always involve accumulated grievances on multiple sides.

The historical analogy that comes to mind is the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930, which is invariably invoked in these moments as a cautionary tale. The parallel is real but imprecise. Smoot-Hawley accelerated a global trade collapse that was already underway due to the Depression; the current moment is not that, yet. What is more apt is the comparison to the trade disputes of the 1980s — Reagan-era steel tariffs, the Japan trade wars — which caused significant bilateral friction but were ultimately managed through negotiation. The question is whether the institutional scaffolding for that kind of negotiated resolution still exists, or whether it has been comprehensively dismantled. The signs are not encouraging.

The Sapporo production shift is a small but telling indicator. When companies start physically relocating production in response to tariffs rather than waiting out a political storm, it suggests they have concluded the disruption is structural rather than temporary. Supply chains, once reconfigured, do not easily snap back. The economic damage from this trade war may prove longer-lasting than the political controversy that caused it.

## What to watch

- Whether the Canadian government escalates further or seeks a negotiated off-ramp; an election calendar in both countries will shape the political incentives.
- Consumer price data in the United States over the next two quarters — if inflation ticks up in categories linked to Canadian imports, the political cost of the tariffs will become harder to ignore.
- Corporate relocation announcements: the Sapporo decision may be the first of many if the standoff persists through the autumn.
- Whether other US trading partners — the EU, Mexico — draw lessons from Canada's experience and begin hedging their own economic exposure to Washington.
