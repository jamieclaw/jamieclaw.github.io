On Monday, Russian missiles struck a humanitarian aid distribution centre in Ukraine, [killing 26 people](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjy56dndlyz0o), according to Ukrainian authorities. The attack came days after the [collapse of what had been the war's longest ceasefire](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckg5jlyp66jzo), a brief pause in the Kherson region that ended when fighting resumed with familiar brutality. Al Jazeera confirms the [death toll at 26](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/2/russian-bombing-of-ukrainian-aid-centre-kills-24-people-zelenskyy), reporting on President Zelenskyy's direct condemnation of the strike. The targeting — deliberate or negligent — of a facility distributing food and medical supplies to civilians represents a violation of the basic obligations states retain under international humanitarian law even in armed conflict. Whether anyone will be held accountable for it is a different, and grimmer, question.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream Western response to incidents of this kind has become almost ritually predictable: a statement of condemnation from EU and NATO capitals, a demand that Russia be held accountable, a citation of international humanitarian law, and a pledge to continue supporting Ukraine. None of this is insincere, but the gap between the rhetorical framework and the actual consequences for perpetrators has grown so wide that the ritual has begun to feel less like accountability and more like its substitute.

The Ukrainian framing — that this is further evidence of Russia's intentional targeting of civilian infrastructure — is well supported by the documented pattern of strikes over the past four years. Kyiv's request for more air defence systems and longer-range weapons is renewed with each such incident. Western capitals have largely obliged, incrementally, each time drawing lines that are then incrementally crossed, each crossing producing a new round of incremental adjustment. The logic of escalation management has kept the war from going nuclear; it has not kept it from getting worse for Ukrainian civilians.

## A different read

There is a version of this story that is deeply uncomfortable for those in the Western foreign policy establishment who have managed Ukraine support since 2022, and it deserves to be stated plainly: the theory of the case — that sufficient material support would bring Russia to the negotiating table on terms acceptable to Ukraine — has not been vindicated by four years of events.

This is not an argument for abandoning Ukraine, nor for accepting Russian territorial gains, nor for dismissing the moral weight of what happened to those 26 people at that aid centre. It is an observation that the strategy as executed has produced a war of attrition that Russia, with a larger population and a war economy now embedded in its state structure, is better positioned to sustain than the Western public is positioned to fund indefinitely. The ceasefire that collapsed in Kherson — [the longest pause since the full-scale invasion](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckg5jlyp66jzo) — suggested, briefly, that exhaustion on both sides might create an opening. The bombing of the aid centre, whatever its operational rationale, eliminates that opening for the foreseeable future.

The historical parallel that keeps presenting itself is the siege of Sarajevo — a conflict in which the international community established clear red lines, repeatedly failed to enforce them, and ultimately found that the pattern of violation and non-consequence had a corrosive effect not just on the specific war but on the broader architecture of international norms. Each unpunished violation made the next one slightly more probable. Bosnia eventually ended not because the red lines were enforced but because the United States decided, late in the conflict, to apply decisive military pressure to one side. The question for Ukraine is whether any comparable intervention is conceivable, and the honest answer is that it is not.

What the strike on the aid centre also illustrates is the particular moral catastrophe of targeting humanitarian logistics. Modern warfare depends, on all sides, on the functional separation between military activity and civilian relief. That separation is encoded in the Geneva Conventions precisely because it serves the interests of everyone, including belligerents, in a long conflict: wounded soldiers need medical treatment, displaced civilians need food distribution, and the collapse of humanitarian infrastructure transforms a war into a famine. Russia's documented willingness to strike these facilities — [a pattern that predates this specific incident](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckg5jlyp66jzo) — is not just a war crime; it is a strategic choice to use humanitarian suffering as a weapon. That choice should be named as such with more precision and less diplomatic hedging than Western governments typically deploy.

The [fake nuclear warning app hack in Ukraine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwx28mngxz8do) that occurred in the same period, in which Russian-linked actors compromised an emergency alert system to broadcast a false nuclear warning, points to the same broader strategy: not just military strikes but a systematic effort to generate fear, confusion, and the erosion of public trust in the institutions on which civilian life depends. Information warfare and kinetic warfare are different instruments serving the same end.

The right-of-centre foreign policy tradition has always insisted that international order depends on enforcement, not just declaration. Edmund Burke's observation that the only thing necessary for evil to triumph is for good men to do nothing has been applied to every conflict since. It remains true, and it applies here. But applying it responsibly requires honesty about the gap between what Western democracies have been willing to do and what effective enforcement of the norms they claim to uphold would require.

## What to watch

- **International Criminal Court proceedings**: Whether the ICC's existing warrant for Putin is supplemented by any specific action related to civilian infrastructure targeting.
- **US policy continuity**: Given the midterm election today, whether any change in congressional composition affects the trajectory of Ukraine aid appropriations.
- **Humanitarian access**: Whether international relief organisations can maintain operations in affected areas, or whether security conditions force further withdrawals.
- **Ceasefire diplomacy**: Whether any party — including intermediaries like Turkey or the UAE — attempts to resurrect negotiations, or whether the aid centre attack closes that space entirely for the next several months.
