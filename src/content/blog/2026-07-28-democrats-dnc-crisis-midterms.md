Less than 100 days before the November midterm elections, the Democratic National Committee is in visible trouble. [The Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/27/dnc-chair-ken-martin-scrutiny) that DNC chair Ken Martin faces mounting scrutiny over a cash-flow problem, management concerns, and widening internal divisions about the party's direction. The New York Times reported that Martin became the subject of an internal HR inquiry after throwing his phone toward a junior aide's desk during a heated exchange, an incident that reportedly nearly cost him his job. The broader context: the party is simultaneously trying to fight a Supreme Court battle over mail-in voting restrictions, reckon with Trump's continued popularity on economic messaging, and manage a Hakeem Jeffries-led congressional strategy that [Al Jazeera describes](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/27/hakeem-jeffries-puts-affordability-at-centre-of-democrats-midterm-campaign) as centred on "affordability" — a pivot away from the cultural terrain where Democrats have found themselves most exposed.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic reading of the Democratic Party's situation is that it is confronting structural headwinds beyond any chairman's power to solve: a deeply unfavourable Senate map, a Republican opponent benefiting from incumbency advantage in a period of genuine if uneven economic recovery, and a media environment in which the national infrastructure leans either sharply right (Fox, Rumble, the broader conservative podcast ecosystem) or has shifted toward a studied neutrality that no longer amplifies Democratic messaging the way it once did. Ken Martin's leadership problems, on this reading, are a symptom of a broader stress that any chair would be experiencing. The affordability pivot to Jeffries represents a serious attempt to compete on the economic terrain where the 2024 losses were most painful.

There is genuine merit in this. The DNC has never been a powerful independent political force — it is primarily a fundraising and logistics operation that the presidential wing controls when a Democrat holds the White House. In opposition, it tends to be a battleground for ideological factions and a fundraising body without clear strategic purpose. The expectation that a party committee chairman can reverse years of structural drift in a single electoral cycle is probably unrealistic.

## A different read

The problem is that "this is structurally hard" does not tell us whether the party is responding to that hardness with the seriousness it requires. And there are accumulating signals that it is not.

The cash-flow crisis is more than a management embarrassment. Political parties fight elections with money, field staff, and data infrastructure. A DNC that is cash-constrained 100 days out has less capacity to register voters, train organisers, and run the get-out-the-vote operations that determine outcomes in close races. The Senate map may be unfavourable, but unfavourable maps have been overcome by organisations that execute well. The 2018 Democratic wave did not happen because the map was easy — it happened because the party's grassroots infrastructure performed at an exceptionally high level.

The ideological incoherence problem is deeper. Jeffries's "affordability" pivot is correct in diagnosis but it arrives against a backdrop of a party that has not settled its internal argument about whether the 2024 and subsequent losses were caused by moving too far left on cultural issues, not far enough left on economic ones, or simply by being the party in power during a period of elevated inflation and disrupted expectations. The answer matters because different diagnoses produce different candidate recruitment strategies, different messaging priorities, and different decisions about which races to contest.

The historical comparison that is not being drawn enough is to the British Labour Party between 1983 and 1992. Labour lost four consecutive general elections during that period, each time convincing a portion of its coalition that the problem was insufficient purity on one dimension or another. The party that eventually won — Tony Blair's 1997 Labour — had concluded, after sustained internal argument, that the losses reflected real voter preferences that required real policy changes, not just better messaging. That process of internal reckoning took a decade. The Democrats, who lost in 2024 and appear to be heading toward another difficult cycle, have not yet had an equivalent reckoning. The party's various factions are still, fundamentally, blaming each other.

There is also a geography problem that neither cash nor messaging can easily solve. The Democratic coalition is heavily concentrated in large metropolitan areas — New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, the major coastal cities. The party continues to lose ground in small-to-medium cities and rural areas in ways that compound in the Senate, where Wyoming has the same two votes as California. Hakeem Jeffries running on affordability is rational for a House campaign in swing suburban districts. It does not address the fundamentally different terrain of Senate races in Montana, West Virginia, and Ohio.

None of this means the midterms are over. A hundred days is a significant amount of time, and external events — the Iran war's costs, wildfire disruption, any economic shock — can reshape the electoral environment quickly. But a party with a cash crisis, an embattled chairman, and an unresolved ideological argument is not well positioned to capitalise on those opportunities when they arise.

## What to watch

- The DNC's fundraising numbers in August: if the party can close its cash-flow gap before the sprint begins, Martin's personal credibility matters less. If it cannot, the structural disadvantage compounds through October.
- Whether Jeffries's affordability framing finds traction in polling, particularly in the handful of Senate races that will determine chamber control.
- Any challenger to Martin's chairmanship: the fact that he reportedly "nearly" lost his job suggests there is a real coalition within the party that would replace him if an alternative were credible.
- The Supreme Court's ruling on mail-in voting: if restrictions are upheld, the party's ground game and voter mobilisation infrastructure become even more critical, increasing the cost of the current cash shortage.
