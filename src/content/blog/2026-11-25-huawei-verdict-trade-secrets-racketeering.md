After more than two months of testimony and eight years since the original indictment, a federal jury in Brooklyn returned a split verdict against [Huawei Technologies](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmy49ngydqzpo) on Tuesday. The Chinese telecommunications giant was found guilty on ten of twelve counts, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets from American firms such as Cisco Systems, T-Mobile, and CNEX, as well as bank fraud and conspiracy to violate US sanctions against Iran. Jurors heard evidence that a Huawei employee slipped a piece of a T-Mobile testing robot into his backpack, and that engineers photographed competitor equipment at trade shows. Prosecutors had also alleged that Huawei deceived HSBC and other banks about the company's business dealings in Iran, funnelling dollars through the Iranian market in breach of US sanctions. The jury [declined to convict](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/24/chinese-tech-giant-huawei-found-guilty-of-conspiracy-to-steal-trade-secrets) on the capstone racketeering conspiracy charge — the count that required jurors to find the firm had operated as a criminal enterprise in the manner of an organised crime syndicate.

## The received wisdom

The dominant interpretation, particularly in Washington's national security community, is that the verdict represents a satisfying, if imperfect, vindication of American efforts to hold China's technology sector accountable for predatory behaviour. Huawei has been on the US trade blacklist since 2019, banned from selling equipment to American carriers, and largely frozen out of Western markets following similar actions by Canada and the United Kingdom. The Biden and Trump administrations alike treated Huawei as Exhibit A in the broader argument that Chinese state-linked companies do not compete through innovation alone but through the systematic theft of intellectual property developed at great expense by Western firms. The guilty findings, in this reading, confirm what policymakers already believed: that behind Huawei's remarkable rise from a mid-tier telecoms supplier to the world's largest maker of wireless network equipment was a pattern of deception and theft that Western capitals were right to treat as a national security concern.

## A different read

There is a different, and uncomfortable, story to be told here — one that the triumphalists in the racketeering camp should sit with for a while.

The racketeering acquittal was not a technicality. It was a considered verdict from a twelve-person jury that heard more than sixty prosecution witnesses and fifteen defence witnesses over two months. The jury found that prosecutors had proved isolated acts of corporate wrongdoing — a stolen robot finger, a memorised circuit board, false statements to banks — but had not proved the grand unified theory of a criminal enterprise. Defence attorney Brian Heberlig's framing, that prosecutors had decided Huawei was a criminal organisation and built their case backwards from that conclusion, found enough purchase with at least some jurors to defeat the most serious charge.

This matters for how the United States should think about its technology competition with China. The two framings — legitimate rule-of-law prosecution versus geopolitically motivated lawfare — carry very different strategic implications. If American courts can prove specific acts of theft, as they did here, then the proper response is a rules-based one: prosecute the theft, seek damages, tighten export controls, demand reciprocity in intellectual property enforcement. That is a defensible posture that Western allies and international institutions can rally behind.

If, on the other hand, the goal is to treat a 200,000-person company operating in 170 countries as a criminal organisation — as the racketeering theory required — the legal instrument strains under the weight of geopolitical ambition. Courts are not designed to adjudicate great-power rivalry. Juries are not equipped to weigh arguments about Chinese Communist Party influence over nominally private firms. The mismatch between the tool and the task is part of why the US government first indicted Huawei in 2018, then spent nearly a decade building a case that ultimately convinced a jury only on the narrower claims.

The [strategic consequences](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/24/chinese-tech-giant-huawei-found-guilty-of-conspiracy-to-steal-trade-secrets) for Huawei are likely modest in any case. The company was already shut out of the US market long before this verdict. It has spent the intervening years diversifying away from Western suppliers, emerging as a major player in China's artificial intelligence sector. The criminal case was always more about precedent and deterrence than about damaging Huawei's commercial position, and by that measure the racketeering acquittal limits its deterrent value considerably.

Conservatives who favour a tough line on China are right that intellectual property theft is a serious problem that cannot be wished away with appeals to free trade orthodoxy, and that the asymmetric openness of Western markets to Chinese firms — while Chinese markets remain largely closed to Western ones — represents a genuine structural imbalance. The Huawei case demonstrates that American courts can find and convict on specific bad acts. The lesson of the acquittal, though, is that the strongest case against China's technology strategy is ultimately economic and diplomatic, not criminal. Maintaining coalitions, building alternative supply chains, ensuring reciprocal market access, and enforcing intellectual property law systematically — these are the instruments that fit the challenge. Reaching for the RICO statute, designed for the Mafia, to prosecute a telecommunications company risks looking like exactly the kind of overreach that defence attorneys argued it was. The verdict was not a vindication. It was a warning.

## What to watch

Watch whether the Justice Department appeals the racketeering acquittal or accepts the split verdict as a workable outcome. Watch also whether Huawei's planned appeal of the ten guilty counts produces further narrowing — a successful appeal on even a subset of the trade-secret counts would reshape the precedent considerably. The sentencing phase will determine what, if any, fines or penalties are imposed on the firm; the government's ask, and the judge's response, will signal how seriously Washington intends to press the remaining convictions. Finally, watch Beijing's response: Chinese officials are likely to frame the racketeering acquittal selectively as vindication while saying little about the ten guilty counts, and that selective framing will itself be revealing.
