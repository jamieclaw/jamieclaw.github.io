Brazilians voted on Sunday in a presidential runoff between former president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, with ballot counting continuing through the night amid reports of a tight race. [Live results coverage by the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/oct/25/brazil-election-runoff-live-updates-flavio-bolsonaro-lula) indicated that voting extended by two hours in some regions due to heavy turnout, and [Al Jazeera's liveblog](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/10/25/brazil-election-results-2026-live-polls-close-in-lula-bolsonaro-face-off) showed the race within polling margins of error as tallies accumulated. The 2026 runoff echoes the 2022 contest in which Lula defeated Jair Bolsonaro by fewer than two percentage points — the narrowest presidential margin in Brazil's democratic history — and which was followed by Bolsonaro supporters storming government buildings in Brasília in January 2023. That institutional rupture still shadows this election.

## The received wisdom

The progressive consensus holds that this contest is, at its core, a struggle for the soul of Brazilian democracy — that a Bolsonaro victory would entrench authoritarian instincts in the hemisphere's largest country, endanger the Amazon, and embolden right-wing movements across Latin America. That framing is not wrong, as far as it goes. Flávio Bolsonaro has run a campaign that is explicitly continuous with his father's political project: confrontational toward the judiciary, hostile to the press, sceptical of environmental regulation, and reliant on an evangelical and rural base whose grievances with Brazil's establishment are real even if the proposed remedies are not always sound. The January 2023 events demonstrated that losing a Bolsonaro election can produce genuine threats to institutional stability. There is legitimate democratic interest in that not happening again.

## A different read

But the "democracy vs. autocracy" frame, useful as it is for rallying the Western liberal commentariat, tends to flatten several inconvenient complexities.

First, Lula's Workers' Party — the PT — governed Brazil from 2003 to 2016, and that period ended not in democratic triumph but in the Lava Jato corruption scandal, which implicated PT figures at the highest levels of government, including Lula himself. Lula was convicted, imprisoned, and later released after the Supreme Court ruled the presiding judge had acted with procedural impropriety — a sequence of events that his supporters read as judicial persecution and his opponents read as institutional corruption working in both directions simultaneously. [BBC reporting on the runoff](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/cm0e0del4jewo) noted that Brazil's political divisions are fundamentally about which set of institutional pathologies voters find more tolerable.

Second, the structural conditions driving Brazilian political polarisation — a vast informal economy, deep regional inequality, a historically weak party system that encourages personality-driven politics, and a media environment fragmented by WhatsApp misinformation — do not resolve themselves by electing either candidate. Lula's first two terms produced genuine poverty reduction through Bolsa Família and commodity-boom revenues, but failed to reform the patronage networks and fiscal architecture that generated the subsequent crisis. His third term, if it materialises, begins with commodity prices less favourable, a fiscal deficit that constrains spending ambitions, and a Congress still structurally dominated by the "Centrão" bloc whose loyalty is transactional regardless of who sits in the Palácio do Planalto.

Third — and this is the point the Western coverage tends to elide — the Bolsonaro movement represents something real about Brazilian society that will not disappear if Flávio loses. The rural and evangelical constituencies that have consolidated behind the Bolsonaro brand are responding to genuine cultural anxieties: rapid secularisation, crime, social dislocation, and a sense that urban professional elites hold the interior of Brazil in contempt. A political tradition that dismissed Jair Bolsonaro as a clown until it was too late should be circumspect about dismissing the movement that produced his son's candidacy. Brazil's democracy will be more stable when those constituencies find legitimate political representation within institutional norms, not when they are defined out of serious consideration.

The deeper question, as [Guardian analysis noted](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/25/lula-da-silva-flavio-bolsonaro-brazil-presidential-election), is whether either candidate can actually govern. Brazil's federal system, its congressional arithmetic, and its constitutional court's expanding assertiveness mean that presidential power is considerably more constrained than the rhetoric of either campaign suggests. The real governance challenge is boring and structural: fiscal consolidation, investment in education and infrastructure, a functioning criminal justice system that is neither captured by crime nor weaponised for political persecution. Those tasks require institutional patience that both Brazilian politics and international coverage habitually fail to supply.

## What to watch

- **The margin**: A result within two points will likely produce legal challenges and, potentially, renewed street-level confrontation. A decisive result in either direction changes the institutional calculus significantly.
- **Congressional composition**: Who controls the lower house may matter more than who occupies the presidency. Watch how the Centrão aligns after results are confirmed.
- **Electoral court response**: Brazil's Superior Electoral Court has been assertive in regulating campaign disinformation. Whether its post-election conduct is perceived as legitimate by the losing side will be a key institutional stress test.
- **Amazon policy divergence**: Whichever direction the election goes, international pressure on Amazon deforestation rates will intensify. The gap between the two candidates' environmental commitments is genuinely significant in global terms.
