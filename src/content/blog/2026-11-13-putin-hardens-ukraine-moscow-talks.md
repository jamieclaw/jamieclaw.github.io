American diplomatic envoys met with Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin on Wednesday after holding separate talks with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky in Dublin, in the latest attempt by Washington to construct a viable path toward ending the war in Ukraine. [The BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwrkgmymk55zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that the Moscow session followed the inconclusive Abu Dhabi talks from earlier this week, in which Ukrainian and Russian delegations negotiated prisoner exchanges but made no progress on territorial or security questions. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/12/vladimir-putin-russia-target-foreign-troops-ukraine-elon-musk-starlink) that Putin took a "hard line" in the US talks, reiterating Russian demands that include Ukrainian neutrality, territorial recognition of Russian-held areas, and limitations on Western arms deliveries — positions Ukraine has consistently rejected as conditions for surrender rather than negotiation. Simultaneously, Ukraine alleged that its Starlink satellite internet service had been disrupted again, a recurring vulnerability that has drawn renewed attention to the fragility of Kyiv's communications infrastructure. [A Ukrainian drone strike on a Russian warship in the Gulf of Finland](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmn0d33zz34zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) underscored that the military conflict shows no sign of pausing to accommodate diplomatic activity.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream diplomatic interpretation of the current moment is guardedly optimistic, at least in its public expression. The argument goes: the mere fact that US envoys are now shuttling between Moscow and Kyiv represents a qualitative shift from the years of near-total diplomatic paralysis. The Abu Dhabi prisoner exchange, however modest, establishes a precedent of tangible deliverables. European capitals have begun speaking again about "post-war security architecture" — language that implies an eventual settlement rather than indefinite conflict. And the Biden-era axiom that any outcome involving territorial compromise was morally unacceptable has been quietly retired; the current US position allows for a negotiated settlement that reflects the military reality on the ground. The optimists argue that both sides are sufficiently exhausted that an off-ramp, if carefully constructed, could find acceptance. Regional security discussions dominating the European talks in Dublin signal that the continent has accepted co-ownership of the outcome.

## A different read

The optimism deserves interrogation, because the structural logic points in a different direction from the diplomatic atmospherics.

Putin's position at the Kremlin talks was not the position of a man who believes he is losing. Russia has absorbed extraordinary costs — military, economic, demographic — but has retained and in some areas extended its territorial grip in eastern Ukraine. Western sanctions, while genuinely damaging, did not produce the economic collapse that many analysts predicted. The battlefield has stabilised, at considerable human cost to both sides, in a configuration that gives Russia more than it held before February 2022. From Putin's perspective, time is not obviously his enemy. A frozen conflict that acknowledges Russian territorial gains and prevents Ukrainian NATO membership achieves most of what Moscow went to war for.

There is also a structural problem with the US diplomatic architecture. [NPR's reporting on the Moscow-Kyiv shuttle](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6038809/us-envoys-meet-putin-russia-ukraine-war-talks) notes that the format is "parallel rather than triangular" — Americans talking separately to each side, neither of which is directly negotiating with the other. This is not how durable peace agreements are made. It is how ceasefires are sometimes assembled, and ceasefires in post-Soviet conflicts have a poor track record of transforming into settlements. The Minsk agreements of 2014 and 2015, which France and Germany brokered with considerable fanfare, produced eight years of low-level war before the full-scale invasion. The lesson of that experience — that a ceasefire Russia uses to consolidate and rearm is not a peace — has not been sufficiently absorbed.

Then there is the European dimension. The talks in Dublin focus on hybrid security threats — cyberattacks, election interference, disinformation — rather than the territorial question at the war's core. This is understandable: it is easier to build European consensus around shared threat perceptions than around the extraordinarily divisive question of what Ukrainian territory, if any, can legitimately be conceded. But a security architecture that addresses the symptoms of Russian revanchism while leaving the core question unresolved is not a stable settlement. It is a waiting room.

The Ukrainian drone strike on the Russian warship is, in this context, not incidental. Kyiv is signalling, through the language of military action, that it does not intend to negotiate from a position of passivity. The Starlink disruption, meanwhile, is a reminder that Ukraine's military effectiveness depends on a commercial communications network controlled by a single American entrepreneur — a dependency that no serious strategic planner would have designed, and which remains a source of extraordinary strategic vulnerability.

## What to watch

Watch whether the "new talks" framework announced by Washington after the Dublin session produces an agreed format for direct Ukrainian-Russian negotiation — or whether it remains a series of US-mediated parallel conversations with no mechanism for closing the gap. Watch European defence spending announcements in the coming weeks: the gap between NATO rhetorical commitment and actual defence industrial capacity remains one of the war's largest unacknowledged problems. Watch Zelensky's domestic political position, which depends heavily on his ability to demonstrate that diplomacy is not cover for capitulation. And watch the Starlink situation — if Ukraine moves to diversify its satellite communications, it will signal that Kyiv has drawn the same lesson from the disruptions that its Western backers have been privately drawing for months.
