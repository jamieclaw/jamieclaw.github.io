[US Representative Ro Khanna (D-California) was detained by armed Israeli settlers for more than an hour](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/11/world/video/ro-khanna-detained-west-bank-digvid-vrtc) during a visit to the southern West Bank this week, after his delegation's vehicle was blocked at a settler checkpoint. [The Hill reports](https://thehill.com/homenews/house/5964095-khanna-detained-israeli-settlers/) that Khanna's group appealed to the US Embassy in Jerusalem, and that Israeli military forces initially refused to intervene. [PBS NewsHour confirms](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/politics/rep-ro-khanna-says-he-was-detained-by-the-israeli-military-and-settlers-in-the-west-bank) that the Israel Defense Forces subsequently denied detaining any visitors, attributing the incident to settler behaviour beyond their immediate control. The settlers involved were reportedly armed with US-manufactured weapons. Khanna, a long-standing critic of Israeli policy toward Palestinians, called on Israel to prosecute those responsible. The incident drew condemnation from across the political spectrum and raised pointed questions about the terms on which American officials — to say nothing of Palestinian civilians — can move through Areas B and C of the West Bank.

## The received wisdom

The progressive framing of this story is clear: a sitting US congressman was physically stopped and detained by agents of a settler movement that is armed, subsidised, and structurally protected by the Israeli state, in territory under Israeli military administration, and the IDF's initial response was to do nothing. This is treated as evidence of a broader accountability gap — settlers act with de facto impunity because successive Israeli governments have treated the settler project as politically untouchable, because the US has provided weapons and diplomatic cover regardless of settler behaviour, and because the Palestinian civilians who face similar treatment daily have no congressman's visibility to compel intervention. The incident is being used, appropriately, to call attention to conditions in the West Bank that rarely reach American media except in connection with major escalations.

The argument goes further: the US funds Israel to the tune of [billions in annual military assistance](https://www.jpost.com/middle-east/article-902160), and that leverage has never been systematically applied to settler violence. If the detention of a member of Congress does not produce any accountability — prosecutions, settler permit revocations, changes to IDF rules of engagement in Areas B and C — it will confirm that American leverage is asymmetric: always available to reassure, never available to restrain.

## A different read

The Khanna incident is being read primarily through the lens of settler impunity, and that reading is not wrong. But it also tells us something important about the political economy of American Middle East policy that the progressive narrative tends to flatten.

Khanna is an outspoken critic of Israeli policy and a vocal advocate for Palestinian rights — positions that place him in the minority of his own party's caucus, let alone in the broader Congress. His decision to make a high-profile visit to the West Bank during the current escalation was politically deliberate: he wanted to document conditions on the ground and generate exactly the kind of news coverage that this incident has produced. That is a legitimate thing for a congressman to do, and his account of events should be taken seriously on its merits. But the incident will be mobilised most effectively not by those who want structural change in US policy, but by those who want to register symbolic opposition while preserving the underlying relationship.

The harder question — why does American leverage over Israeli settler policy remain essentially unused — cannot be answered by pointing at this week's incident alone. The structural answer involves three interlocking factors. First, the bipartisan coalitional logic of US politics means that any president who seriously conditions aid or diplomatic support risks significant political costs, particularly in primary elections where pro-Israel voters and donors remain disproportionately influential. Second, the settler enterprise has been steadily institutionalised over five decades to the point where dismantling it, even partially, would require a confrontation not just with Netanyahu but with a significant share of the Israeli electorate and coalition arithmetic. Third, American administrations of both parties have consistently treated the Palestinian Authority as too weak and too internally divided to be a credible partner for the kind of settlement that would require settler rollback — a circular logic in which Palestinian institutional weakness, partly the product of Israeli policy, is used to justify the continuation of that policy.

None of this makes Khanna's detention acceptable, or should make it politically cost-free for Israel. But it does suggest that the path from this incident to structural change runs through American domestic politics as much as through Israeli government decisions — and that the progressives most animated by this story are, at present, not the faction that controls US foreign policy.

## What to watch

— **Congressional response**: Whether the incident produces a formal State Department protest, a suspension of any settler-related assistance, or hearings on Area C conditions will reveal whether symbolic condemnation is all that follows.

— **IDF accountability process**: Israel's Military Advocate General has an obligation to investigate incidents involving foreign nationals in the West Bank. Whether any settler faces legal consequences will test the IDF's claim that it was not involved.

— **Administration positioning**: The White House's response to Khanna's account — or its silence — will signal how much diplomatic friction the current administration is willing to absorb on settler accountability.

— **West Bank violence trend**: The summer period has historically correlated with increased settler violence; monitoring groups track incidents weekly. Any escalation following this incident's publicity would be a significant signal.
