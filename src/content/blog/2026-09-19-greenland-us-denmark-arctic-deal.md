The United States and Denmark announced on September 18, 2026 that they had reached a security deal granting Washington expanded access to Greenland — an agreement that both governments described in careful diplomatic language as a partnership rather than a transfer of sovereignty. [According to the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c63d7lexyym1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss), the arrangement would involve American military presence and economic investment on the island, with a formal Arctic security pact expected to be signed the following week. The Guardian reported the same day that [Trump claimed the deal gives the US "permanent control" over Greenland](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/18/united-states-denmark-greenland-security) — language that Copenhagen quickly sought to soften. Al Jazeera noted Trump's own framing: he [told reporters the US had achieved "permanent control"](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/18/trump-claims-deal-with-denmark-gives-us-permanent-control-over-greenland?traffic_source=rss) of the Arctic territory, while Danish officials spoke only of deepened security cooperation.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream reading of this deal is broadly positive. Progressive and liberal commentators tend to frame Trump's Greenland obsession as an embarrassing throwback — imperial bluster from a president who once tried to buy the island outright as if it were a Manhattan condominium. When a deal actually materialises, the narrative shifts to relief: better an agreement than confrontation between NATO allies, and the partnership language at least preserves the form of Danish sovereignty. Internationalist voices point to the genuine strategic rationale — Greenland sits astride vital trans-Arctic shipping lanes and early-warning radar corridors — and argue that Washington's interest is legitimate, even if its style has been boorish. The left-liberal press tends to see this primarily through a climate lens, worrying that Arctic militarisation will impede scientific research and accelerate resource extraction in a fragile ecosystem.

This reading is not wrong, exactly. It is simply incomplete.

## A different read

What is being glossed over is the degree to which this deal — whatever its precise legal contents, which neither government has fully disclosed — represents a substantial revision of the post-1945 norm against territorial revision by great powers. That norm has been honoured more in breach than in observance, certainly; but it has served as an anchor of the international order since the UN Charter was drafted. When Russia violated it in Crimea in 2014 and again in 2022, the Western response was to treat those violations as categorically illegitimate. The uncomfortable question now is: what exactly is the categorical difference between Moscow pressuring Kyiv and Washington pressuring Copenhagen?

The differences are real. Denmark is a NATO ally that negotiated freely, not a country being invaded. The deal involves access and investment, not annexation. And yet the [Al Jazeera account of Trump's framing](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/18/trump-claims-deal-with-denmark-gives-us-permanent-control-over-greenland?traffic_source=rss) — "permanent control" — is revealing about what the American side believes it has obtained. Permanent control is not the language of a base-access agreement. It is the language of absorption.

History offers a useful parallel. In 1940, the United States secured basing rights in British Caribbean colonies in exchange for fifty destroyers. The deal was voluntary in the formal sense; Britain was desperate and had no real alternative. Historians have since debated whether those bases constituted a form of informal American imperialism — what Charles Kindleberger called "hegemonic stability" dressed in the clothes of contractual partnership. The Greenland arrangement has a similar flavour. A small democracy adjacent to a continental superpower, facing intense economic and diplomatic pressure, eventually reaches an agreement that the superpower describes in terms the smaller partner conspicuously avoids.

For right-leaning analysts who take national interest seriously, there is a temptation to simply endorse this outcome. American strategic reach into the Arctic is genuinely valuable; Russia is genuinely threatening; Greenland genuinely matters for deterrence. The harder question is whether accepting this logic consistently — great powers get what their strategic interests require, provided they pay something for it — is a principle we are prepared to apply universally. If so, China's claims in the South China Sea, pressed through economic rather than military means, acquire a new legitimacy. If not, we need to articulate more carefully what distinguishes legitimate from illegitimate forms of great-power pressure, beyond the identity of the power doing the pressing.

The Danes, to their credit, are trying to thread this needle. Their [official position](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c63d7lexyym1o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) emphasises partnership and economic development rather than strategic subordination. Greenlandic leaders have their own interests — substantial investment and greater autonomy from Copenhagen — which partially align with American objectives. That complexity is genuine. But it does not dissolve the deeper tension: a small territory is being drawn into a great-power sphere of influence under the pressure of coercion that was made explicit in Trump's earlier annexation threats, even if the final instrument is more diplomatically dressed.

For those of us who care about rule of law in international affairs, the appropriate response is neither to celebrate American strategic success nor to reflexively condemn it as imperialism. It is to insist that the full terms of the agreement be made public, that Greenlandic self-determination be genuinely respected, and that this deal not be cited — as it will inevitably be cited — by other great powers who want to press their own territorial claims through a combination of economic incentive and implicit menace.

## What to watch

- **The formal pact signing**: The specific legal language matters enormously. "Permanent control" versus "long-term strategic partnership" are not merely rhetorical differences — they have implications for Danish sovereignty, Greenlandic autonomy, and international precedent. Watch what Copenhagen actually signs next week.
- **Chinese and Russian reactions**: Beijing in particular will be watching closely. Any Chinese statement framing the Greenland deal as a precedent for its own territorial assertions should be treated as a serious signal.
- **Greenlandic parliamentary debate**: Greenland's Inatsisartut (parliament) has not yet publicly ratified the terms. Internal Greenlandic politics — especially the independence movement — could become a significant complication.
- **NATO legal review**: Whether the deal passes muster with NATO's own legal frameworks for territorial arrangements among members will be a useful test of institutional seriousness.
