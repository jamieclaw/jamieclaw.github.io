US forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday in what [the BBC reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z72x5z1po) as the first known American attack on Iran since late July. US Central Command said Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps forces had been "observed preparing to launch rockets with sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz." The IRGC confirmed the attack killed and wounded several people and vowed "response and punishment." IRGC spokesman Hossein Mohebbi called it a "strategic and fatal mistake by the Trump regime" and warned the "enemy will pay the consequences of this miscalculation." Larak Island sits directly on the strait, adjacent to the key port of Bandar Abbas, and Iran has reportedly been forcing vessels to pay $2 million to cross past it. Trump said last week that all mines placed in the Strait of Hormuz had been cleared, a claim Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi dismissed as "propaganda." The six-month-old US-Iran war — which began with American and Israeli strikes on February 28 — has killed 3,375 Iranians and 18 US service members, per [earlier BBC coverage](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z72x5z1po), and has effectively closed to traffic a waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's oil and gas used to flow.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and strategic case for de-escalation is serious and deserves a fair hearing. The human costs have been severe. A war begun with the stated aim of deterring Iranian nuclear and regional ambitions has produced, six months in, a hardened Iranian regime, a closed Strait of Hormuz, depleted US munitions stocks, and stalled diplomacy. Sunday's strike — the first in weeks — represents a re-entry into active military operations at a moment when the pause had raised hopes, however faint, of a negotiated settlement. The progressive reading is that Trump's maximum-pressure approach has demonstrably failed to achieve its stated objectives: Iran's government has not collapsed, the strait remains restricted, and the economic pain of high oil prices is falling hardest on the Americans the administration claims to be helping. Each US strike creates domestic pressure within Iran to respond, which in turn creates US pressure to strike again — a cycle with no natural terminus.

## A different read

The progressive critique captures the costs but underestimates the alternative costs that a different policy would have incurred.

The Larak Island strike was triggered by a specific and immediate provocation: IRGC forces preparing to deploy sea mines into the world's most important commercial waterway. Trump had warned publicly that any boat deploying new mines would be destroyed. Not striking in that circumstance would have undermined the credibility of that deterrent — a deterrent whose maintenance is the central logic of the entire US posture. As [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/30/us-strikes-irans-lark-island-in-first-attack-in-weeks), the IRGC was actively preparing to renew the mining campaign that the US had spent weeks and significant resources dismantling. Inaction would have invited its repetition.

The deeper strategic problem is one that afflicts the critics of this war as much as its architects: there is no obvious exit. Iran's government did not collapse after the most intensive bombing campaign the region has seen in a generation. The death of Supreme Leader Khamenei on the first day of strikes, and the killing of dozens of senior leaders, did not produce the state failure that some analysts predicted — rather, it appears to have triggered a consolidation of IRGC control over the residual state apparatus. This is consistent with what historians of air power have long argued: that bombing campaigns targeting leadership produce disarray before they produce capitulation, and that a decapitated authoritarian state is often replaced by something more radical, not less.

The Iran war has now lasted six months, a threshold at which historical precedents grow instructive. The Korean War at six months had produced a military stalemate after initial dramatic advances; the Vietnam air campaign at equivalent intensity produced no decisive outcome; the 1991 Gulf War — the most militarily decisive US air campaign on record — required ground forces to achieve its objectives. In none of these cases did strategic bombing alone produce political surrender by the targeted state.

What makes the current situation more dangerous than most of those precedents is the Strait of Hormuz variable. The strait's effective closure has not merely spiked oil prices and put pressure on the Federal Reserve; it has also created a political incentive for Trump to find a face-saving resolution before the November midterm elections. [The BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2z72x5z1po) that the Venezuela oil deal was explicitly framed by Trump partly as a response to the Iran-driven oil price spike. That kind of political pressure — midterms looming, domestic prices elevated — creates exactly the conditions in which rushed diplomatic deals get made. Whether that produces something durable or merely a pause before the next escalation cycle depends on whether the US and Iran can agree on what a genuine off-ramp looks like. Neither side has so far articulated one publicly.

The fresh sanctions campaign announced on August 24, targeting Iran's remaining economic linkages and expanding secondary sanctions against its allies, is the other half of the US strategy. Iranian officials have largely dismissed it as ineffective, but that dismissal may not hold if sustained pressure begins to bite the residual IRGC-run economy. The combination of military strikes and economic pressure is the textbook maximum-pressure playbook — but the textbook does not tell you how long it takes, or what happens if the targeted regime simply decides to absorb the pain indefinitely.

## What to watch

- The IRGC's promised "response and punishment" — whether it takes the form of fresh mining operations, proxy attacks in the Gulf, or cyberattacks on US infrastructure.
- The US diplomatic channel with Iran: whether Sunday's strike forecloses the quiet back-channel contacts that had reportedly resumed during the July pause.
- Oil price movements and their knock-on effects on the Federal Reserve's September rate decision, which now occurs against a backdrop of renewed Hormuz uncertainty.
- Congressional pressure: with midterms approaching, watch for Senate Republicans from high-gasoline-price states who may push for a diplomatic off-ramp regardless of the military situation.
