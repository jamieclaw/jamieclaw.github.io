The United States this week [announced a sweeping new round of tariffs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c893r9ke5p97o) on a host of countries including China, triggering an immediate sell-off in American equity markets and a wider global reaction from trading partners. US stocks [fell further](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq0md8g14zryo) as investors weighed the inflationary implications and the risk of retaliatory measures. The announcement follows years of on-again-off-again trade brinkmanship and comes as the administration seeks to put a harder edge on its economic nationalist programme. World markets reacted swiftly, with [analysts and governments](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/11/25/world-reacts-to-trumps-new-tariffs-as-us-stocks-tumble) across Europe, Asia, and Latin America issuing cautious statements about the potential disruption to supply chains and bilateral trade relationships.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive critique of tariffs is well established and largely correct at the technical level: economists across the mainstream spectrum — from the Peterson Institute to the IMF — are nearly unanimous that broad tariff regimes raise consumer prices, distort supply chains, invite retaliation, and reduce aggregate welfare. The free-trade consensus that emerged from the Bretton Woods era and was codified through the GATT and later the WTO rested on the empirical observation that comparative advantage, not managed trade, generates the most durable prosperity. Critics of the new tariff round will also note, with some justice, that the first Trump tariff wave of 2018–2019 did not, on balance, revive American manufacturing employment to the degree promised, and that the costs were largely borne by American importers and consumers rather than foreign exporters. Add to this the perverse spectacle — noted by the BBC — of the President's own financial accounts [having traded Nvidia shares forty-four times this year](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq772vk664yvo) while his administration simultaneously shapes the regulatory and trade environment for semiconductor companies, and you have a picture that free-traders and anti-corruption advocates can both condemn.

## A different read

None of that makes the free-trade consensus as intellectually settled as its proponents believe, and the politics of the matter have shifted in ways that the old consensus has been slow to absorb.

Start with the structural argument. The post-war trade order was constructed on a set of assumptions — that rising prosperity abroad would produce liberalisation at home, that multilateral rules would be respected, that geopolitical rivals would not weaponise economic interdependence — that have been serially falsified by China's behaviour since accession to the WTO in 2001. As the [Cato Institute's Scott Lincicome](https://www.cato.org/free-trade-bulletin/problem-chinas-wto-accession-lessons-for-future-trade-negotiations) has acknowledged, even committed free-traders now concede that the assumption of automatic Chinese liberalisation was naive. The question is not whether that model failed; it is what a more realistic successor should look like.

The harder case for the tariff hawks is strategic, not economic. Advanced semiconductor manufacturing capacity, rare-earth processing, and certain pharmaceutical supply chains have concentrated in China to a degree that creates genuine security vulnerabilities — ones that became embarrassingly visible during the Covid-era shortages. The logic of the CHIPS Act, which the Biden administration used to incentivise domestic semiconductor fabrication, rested on exactly this premise: that some industries are too strategically important to be governed entirely by market signals. Tariffs are a blunt instrument for achieving the same goal, and they carry the consumer-price penalty that CHIPS-style subsidies avoid — but the underlying strategic logic is not irrational. The question is whether this administration has the discipline to target that logic or whether it reverts to the scatter-gun approach that made the 2018 round so economically damaging.

There is also a political economy dimension that the technocratic consensus consistently underweights. The communities hollowed out by deindustrialisation did not abstractly "gain" from cheaper consumer goods if the trade-off was the destruction of the social fabric that stable employment provides. This is not a new observation — [Dani Rodrik's trilemma](https://drodrik.scholar.harvard.edu/publications/globalization-paradox-why-global-markets-states-and-democracy-cant-coexist) has been in the literature since the 1990s — but it took three decades and a succession of populist revolts to force it into mainstream political discourse. The White House's tariff politics are partly a crude attempt to channel that grievance, and partly a genuine (if chaotically executed) attempt to rebalance a trade relationship that the existing rules framework was not designed to handle. Neither rationale makes the new tariff round well-designed, but both explain why the technocratic consensus lost the political argument.

What worries me most about the current moment is the casual financial entanglement at the top. If the President is trading semiconductor company shares forty-four times in a year while his administration controls the technology-export regime and the tariff schedule that most directly affects those companies, then the trade policy cannot be evaluated purely on its economic or strategic merits. It has become, at least partially, a vehicle for insider advantage — and that is an entirely different and more corrosive problem than the standard argument over comparative advantage.

## What to watch

Watch whether the new tariff schedule is applied uniformly or whether exemptions proliferate along political lines — the latter pattern, visible in 2018, is what transforms strategic trade policy into a patronage system. Watch Beijing's retaliation: Chinese authorities have multiple tools beyond counter-tariffs, including controls on rare-earth exports and regulatory harassment of US firms operating in China. Watch the congressional response: the [Republican appetite](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/25/nx-s1-6052417/nvidia-jensen-huang-reaction-republicans-congress-investigation) for scrutinising executive entanglement with the companies most affected by this policy will be a litmus test of whether the party retains any institutional independence. And watch consumer-price indices in Q1 2027 — the lag between tariff imposition and retail shelf prices is typically three to six months.
