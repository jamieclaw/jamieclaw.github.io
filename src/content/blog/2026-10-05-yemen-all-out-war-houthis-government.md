Yemen's internationally recognised government announced on Sunday what it described as an "all-out war" to reclaim territory held by the Houthi movement, [according to BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx8dzj9vpp01o). The declaration follows months of Houthi strikes on Red Sea shipping, intensified attacks on Saudi infrastructure, and the group's [capture of Mokha earlier this year](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx8dzj9vpp01o) — a port city of strategic importance on the western coast. The announcement was made by the Presidential Leadership Council, the fractious coalition of military and political factions that nominally governs the areas of Yemen not under Houthi control, and was backed by a statement of support from Saudi Arabia. Whether the declaration represents a genuine military shift or a piece of rhetorical positioning is the central question — and the history of the Yemen conflict offers sobering guidance.

## The received wisdom

The humanitarian and diplomatic community's instinctive response to any escalatory declaration in Yemen is apprehension verging on despair. The conflict, now in its twelfth year, has produced what the United Nations has called the world's worst humanitarian crisis: millions on the brink of famine, a health system in ruins, and a civilian population that has suffered consistently regardless of which side claimed the initiative militarily. The Houthis, whatever their Iran connections and their brutal suppression of dissent in the territories they control, have demonstrated a resilience that purely military pressure has repeatedly failed to break. Saudi Arabia spent five years and hundreds of billions of riyals on an air campaign that failed to dislodge them from Sanaa. The notion that the Presidential Leadership Council — an entity riddled with internal rivalries and dependent on external patronage — can now succeed where that campaign failed strains credulity. The progressive case is for negotiation, humanitarian access, and pressure on Iran to rein in its proxies.

## A different read

The problem with the conflict-aversion framing is that it implicitly treats the Houthi status quo as stable. It is not. The Houthis are not a nationalist liberation movement that reached a natural equilibrium; they are an Iranian-backed theocratic militia that has progressively expanded its territorial control, weaponized the humanitarian crisis to extract international aid while blocking it from civilian populations, and used the Red Sea campaign to impose economic costs on the entire global trading system. The UN's own reports have documented Houthi diversion of humanitarian assistance. The argument that negotiation is the answer begs the question: what has changed since the last round of talks to make Houthi interlocutors more amenable to a settlement that does not leave them in de facto control of most of Yemen?

The "all-out war" declaration should not be taken at face value — Yemeni political declarations have a long history of outrunning operational capacity — but the context is different from previous escalatory moments in several respects. First, [Saudi Arabia has formally backed the declaration](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx8dzj9vpp01o), which matters because Riyadh has historically restrained its Yemeni proxies at key moments to avoid derailing oil-price diplomacy. Saudi endorsement now suggests Riyadh has concluded that the Houthis cannot be managed through containment. Second, the American posture in the region has shifted: the Trump administration's active campaign against Iran has degraded some of the supply chains through which Tehran arms the Houthis. The question is whether degradation has been sufficient to create a military window, or whether Houthi stockpiles remain adequate.

Third, and most importantly, the Houthis' own recent behaviour suggests they may have overreached. The Red Sea campaign earned them regional credibility and Iranian largesse, but it also forged a coalition of states — including Egypt, whose Suez Canal revenues were directly threatened — with a concrete economic interest in Houthi containment. Egypt's willingness to allow its territorial waters and airspace to be used for logistics supporting anti-Houthi operations is a quiet but significant shift. Regional coalitions built on shared economic interest tend to be more durable than those built on ideology.

The risk in a renewed ground campaign is obvious: civilian casualties, infrastructure destruction, and the further displacement of a population that has nowhere left to go. Any serious prosecution of this war requires robust humanitarian corridors and international monitoring — conditions the Presidential Leadership Council has not historically delivered. The declaration of "all-out war" is most useful as a political signal to donors and regional patrons that the Yemeni government has not given up; its military significance depends entirely on operational follow-through that remains to be seen.

## What to watch

- **Saudi air support and logistics:** Does the Royal Saudi Air Force intensify sorties in coordination with ground operations? Air power without ground control has been the failure mode before.
- **Houthi counter-escalation:** Watch for attacks on Saudi Aramco infrastructure or escalation of Red Sea strikes as a deterrence signal following the declaration.
- **Iranian resupply routes:** With US pressure on Iran's logistics network continuing, can Tehran maintain Houthi ammunition and missile stocks at operationally relevant levels?
- **UN Security Council response:** Will the declaration trigger a Security Council session, and will Russia — which has periodically shielded Houthis from censure — allow meaningful resolution language?
