The International Court of Justice is set to deliver [a historic genocide verdict against Myanmar](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5m2l90ez144o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) over the massacre of the Rohingya minority — a ruling that would be the first time an international court has found a state guilty of committing genocide. The case was brought by The Gambia under the Genocide Convention and has wound through years of proceedings since 2019. No state has ever been found guilty by the ICJ on this charge before; the legal and symbolic weight of the verdict is therefore considerable. Simultaneously, the [Guardian reports that fears are growing](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/18/us-sanctions-icj-judges-myanmar-genocide-ruling-israel-gaza-court) that the United States may impose sanctions against ICJ judges involved in the ruling — a reaction driven not by concern for Myanmar, but by concern that the precedent could be extended to Israeli conduct in Gaza. The convergence of these two stories reveals the structural contradiction at the heart of international legal institutions: they can deliver verdicts, but enforcement depends entirely on the political will of the powers that could act on them.

## The received wisdom

The human rights and liberal internationalist case for the ICJ ruling is powerful and deserves honest engagement. The Rohingya massacres of 2016–17 were extensively documented: villages burned, mass graves discovered, sexual violence deployed systematically. The UN's own fact-finding mission concluded that Myanmar military commanders bore criminal responsibility. For this school of thought, the ICJ ruling is not idealism but accountability — the minimum condition for a rules-based international order that treats the prohibition on genocide as a genuine norm rather than a selective tool. The concern about US sanctions on ICJ judges is, in this reading, an example of precisely the kind of great-power exceptionalism that corrodes institutional legitimacy over time: if powerful states can punish courts for reaching inconvenient conclusions, the courts become worthless.

This case has real force. Norms without enforcement mechanisms atrophy; the history of the twentieth century is in large part a history of what happens when great powers decide their interests override legal constraints on mass atrocity.

## A different read

And yet the procedural history of the case raises questions that the standard humanitarian narrative tends to minimise. The Myanmar military junta that conducted the operations against the Rohingya is not the same government that now faces the ICJ; Myanmar has been under military rule following the 2021 coup, and the civilian government against which the case was originally mounted no longer exists in recognisable form. The practical question of what a guilty verdict compels — reparations from a junta that neither controls Rohingya return conditions nor has any interest in complying — is not answered by the legal text. This is the deeper problem with international adjudication: it is excellent at establishing historical record and terrible at generating compliance from states that have no reputational interest in the international system.

The more interesting story is the American reaction. That Washington would contemplate sanctions against ICJ judges to prevent a precedent that might reach Israel is not surprising — it is consistent with decades of American behaviour toward international institutions it finds inconvenient. What is new is the explicitness of the signal. The [Guardian's report](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/18/us-sanctions-icj-judges-myanmar-genocide-ruling-israel-gaza-court) suggests the Trump administration is willing to put the pressure on the record, rather than applying it through quieter diplomatic channels. That explicitness carries its own cost: it confirms, for every state calculating whether to participate in international legal processes, that the United States regards those processes as legitimate only when they reach conclusions Washington approves.

The right-of-centre disposition toward international institutions is typically sceptical, and not without reason. International courts staffed by judges from states with their own strategic interests and activist interpretive philosophies are not neutral arbiters. The ICJ's composition reflects geopolitical distributions that do not map onto impartial adjudication. But there is a distinction between principled scepticism about institutional overreach and simply subordinating legal processes to immediate national interest. The first is a defensible position with a serious intellectual tradition behind it; the second is an invitation to every state with a grudge against international law — including states America will want to invoke it against in future — to follow suit.

Niall Ferguson's observation that empires that undermine the institutional architecture they built tend to find that architecture remarkably hard to reconstruct is worth keeping in mind. The United States built the post-1945 legal order precisely because it expected to benefit from a world in which certain norms were enforced. Systematically hollowing out the enforcement mechanisms of that order because they produce inconvenient verdicts is a strategy with long-run costs that the current administration appears not to be pricing.

## What to watch

- Whether the United States actually implements sanctions against ICJ judges, or whether the signal is primarily diplomatic intimidation designed to influence the court's future docket.
- How China and Russia respond: both have reasons to welcome American erosion of ICJ legitimacy, and the Shenzhen summit provides a convenient context for a coordinated response.
- Whether Rohingya civil society groups can use the verdict to access enforcement mechanisms — including asset freezes — through third-country courts, circumventing the compliance problem.
- The implications for the [Gaza cases](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c5m2l90ez144o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) currently before the same court: a Myanmar genocide finding creates legal terrain that the ICJ's Gaza proceedings will inevitably have to navigate.
