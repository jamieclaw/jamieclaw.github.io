Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky this week dismissed Mykhailo Fedorov, the minister widely credited with building the country's astonishing digital war-fighting infrastructure and keeping international attention focused on the conflict through social media. [The sacking triggered rare protests in the streets of Kyiv](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/16/rare-protests-in-kyiv-over-dismissal-of-defence-minister), an unusual public rupture for a society that has maintained remarkable cohesion under three years of full-scale invasion. [Zelensky defended the reshuffle as a necessary act of wartime management](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/17/zelenskyy-defends-sacking-ukraine-defence-minister-mykhailo-fedorov), while [Starmer used his final visit as British Prime Minister to pledge unwavering support for Ukraine](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx2myl1erj8o) — a departure awkwardly overshadowed by the domestic political turmoil in Kyiv. The episode raises questions about what democratic governance looks like under conditions of existential war, and whether the answers are as simple as Western cheerleaders have tended to assume.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream narrative about Zelensky has been largely heroic, and not without reason. Whatever his pre-war limitations as a television comedian turned politician, his personal courage in remaining in Kyiv in February 2022 when the capital was under threat did more to stiffen NATO's resolve than any diplomatic cable. Western liberal opinion has extended him a long leash on governance questions, reasoning that wartime decisions require flexibility that peacetime democracies can afford to scrutinise more carefully. The dismissal of Fedorov, on this reading, is an internal management call, regrettable perhaps if a talented minister is lost, but hardly the stuff of democratic crisis in a country where conscription notices are issued under artillery fire. Ukraine's friends in the West have consistently argued that democratic imperfections in Kyiv must be judged in context — a standard that most NATO members would struggle to meet even without a war on their territory.

## A different read

The difficulty is that the case for supporting Ukraine has always rested on two pillars: strategic interest and democratic solidarity. Strip away the second and you are left with a straightforward great-power proxy war — entirely justifiable on realist grounds, but one that requires a different and more honest political vocabulary than the one Western governments have used. If Kyiv can fire a popular minister credited with saving thousands of Ukrainian lives through drone logistics and digital mobilisation, without explanation, generating street protests, then the democracy premium on Ukraine's image needs careful re-examination.

This is not an argument for abandoning Ukraine. It is an argument for intellectual honesty about what the West is funding and why. The history of wartime executive consolidation is not encouraging. In every major conflict of the twentieth century, leaders who acquired emergency powers to prosecute a war found those powers difficult to surrender when the emergency passed. Churchill was voted out by a grateful but wary electorate in 1945. Ataturk's military necessity calcified into one-party authoritarianism. The Israeli case — another democracy under existential threat — shows how security pressures gradually corrode judicial independence, press freedom, and the rights of minorities, often with the democratic supermajority's blessing.

[NPR reported that Zelensky had fired his tech-savvy defence minister in what was described as a government reshuffle](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/16/g-s1-133920/ukraine-defense-minister-fired). The word "reshuffle" is doing a great deal of work there. Fedorov was not a bureaucratic timeserver; he was the public face of Ukraine's most globally admired innovation — the use of digital tools to wage asymmetric war, crowdfund weapons, and communicate directly with foreign publics. Removing him without explanation, in the middle of an active war, in a way that generates public unrest, suggests either a genuine falling-out between Zelensky and a rival power centre, or a consolidation of authority that Zelensky deems necessary but has not chosen to justify to his own citizens.

[The Guardian noted that outrage at the sacking overshadowed Starmer's final visit as British PM](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/jul/16/europe-ukraine-russia-zelenskyy-starmer-macron-merz-latest-news-updates), which tells you something about the scale of the domestic reaction. Western governments are in an awkward position: they cannot publicly criticise a wartime leader they are arming and funding without undermining the very morale they are trying to sustain. But the correct response to awkwardness is not silence; it is careful language. "Unwavering support for Ukraine" is a slogan, not a policy. What British and European governments should actually be saying is something like: "We support the Ukrainian people's right to determine their own future, and we expect the Ukrainian government to remain accountable to the people it represents, especially in matters of internal governance." That is harder to fit on a banner but reflects a more durable commitment.

The protesters in Kyiv are not Putin's proxies. They are Ukrainians who have fought, lost family members, and endured displacement, expressing a democratic impulse that is precisely what the war is supposedly being fought to protect. Dismissing their grievance as a wartime inconvenience is, to put it politely, a category error.

## What to watch

Watch whether Zelensky offers any public explanation for the dismissal — transparency under pressure is the minimum test of democratic governance. Track whether Western governments issue any statement beyond boilerplate solidarity, or whether the diplomatic instinct to keep Zelensky comfortable overrides the democratic principles they claim to champion. Watch whether the protests sustain or dissipate — sustained civic pressure in wartime Ukraine would be genuinely significant and historically unusual. And note whether Fedorov surfaces in any other capacity, which would indicate a demotion rather than a purge.
