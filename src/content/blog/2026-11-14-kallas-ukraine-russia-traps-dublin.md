European Union foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas used a summit in Dublin on Thursday to warn EU member states against walking into what she characterised as Russian "traps" in ongoing negotiations over a potential settlement to the Ukraine war. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/13/russia-ukraine-talks-kaja-kallas-eu-foreign-policy-dublin-summit) that Kallas, speaking alongside EU leaders gathered for the summit, cautioned that Moscow was attempting to use the peace process to achieve through diplomacy what it has been unable to secure on the battlefield — specifically, the progressive isolation of Ukraine from Western security guarantees and the normalisation of Russian territorial control over occupied Ukrainian regions. [A live account of the Dublin proceedings](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/nov/13/eu-leaders-negotiations-ukraine-russia-dublin-ai-summit-kushner-witkoff-latest-news-updates) confirmed that the summit also included discussions with US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, whose continued presence in the mediation process represents a direct White House investment in producing a diplomatic outcome before the American political cycle makes a settlement politically untenable. [The BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq39glwprknjo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) that Putin himself has been making uncompromising demands, escalating his stated conditions for peace in a pattern that Kallas's warning was specifically designed to name and resist.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream European diplomatic view holds that Kallas's warning is both correct and necessary. Russia's negotiating history — from the Minsk agreements of 2014 and 2015 through the Istanbul talks of April 2022 — demonstrates a consistent pattern: agreeing to processes that deliver time and tactical advantage while making no concessions of substance, then blaming the other side when progress stalls. The Minsk agreements provided a template: they codified a ceasefire line that acknowledged Russian-backed territorial gains, embedded language about Ukrainian autonomy that Moscow subsequently interpreted as a veto over Kyiv's foreign policy, and bought Russia eight years to rebuild its military before the 2022 invasion. Any peace framework that resembles Minsk is, by this reading, not a path to stability but a staged defeat with diplomatic trappings.

The more sceptical Western voice — not necessarily progressive but cautiously realist — would argue that at some point the question of what Ukraine can realistically recover militarily must be confronted. The front lines have been substantially static for over a year. Western military assistance, while significant, has not produced the decisive battlefield shift that would change Moscow's cost-benefit calculation. At some point, prolonging a war that is consuming Ukrainian lives and infrastructure at scale, in service of territorial positions that may never be recovered, becomes a moral failure of a different kind.

## A different read

Kallas's framing is strategically correct, but it creates a problem that she has not, to this point, fully resolved: telling your partners not to fall into Russian traps is useful advice only if you can specify the alternative. What does a non-trap peace framework actually look like? The Minsk parallels are instructive not just as a warning about Russian intentions, but as a demonstration of how limited Europe's options are when it needs to offer something to both a war-exhausted Ukraine and a Western political class that is increasingly impatient for resolution.

The Trump administration's engagement, through Kushner and Witkoff, adds a layer of complexity that the EU's diplomatic institutions are not well-designed to manage. Washington's interest is in a deal that can be presented as a win before the 2028 election cycle begins in earnest — a timeline that creates pressure for speed over durability. The EU's interest is in a settlement that does not reward aggression in a way that invites the next aggression, whether against the Baltic states, Moldova, or somewhere else along Russia's contested periphery. These interests are not identical, and the gap between them is precisely the gap that Moscow is trying to exploit.

The historical context matters here. The post-Cold War European security order rested on three implicit premises: that territorial borders were inviolable, that Western security guarantees were credible, and that economic integration would gradually domesticate Russian ambitions. All three premises were wrong, or at least insufficiently robust to survive a determined challenge. The Kallas-led EU response to the war has been commendably harder-edged than what the European mainstream was producing three years ago — she has been consistent in her support for Ukrainian sovereignty and her scepticism of Russian good faith. But hardness in rhetoric has not been matched by hardness in the specific security commitments that would give Ukraine the confidence to negotiate from something other than a position of existential anxiety.

[The BBC's reporting on Putin's escalating demands](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq39glwprknjo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) suggests that Moscow's current posture is to maximise its stated requirements in the expectation that Western fatigue will produce a progressive retreat toward a settlement that accepts the Russian position as the baseline. The correct European response to that strategy is not more warnings about traps — it is a credible commitment to what happens if negotiations fail: more arms, more economic pressure, clearer security guarantees for whatever Ukrainian territory remains unoccupied. Without that credible alternative, warning about traps is merely eloquent helplessness.

The parallel that presents itself is Munich, though one must be careful not to overuse it. The more precise historical analogy may be the Versailles negotiations of 1919 — where the victorious powers arrived with a correct analysis of German aggression and an inadequate answer to the question of what framework could contain Germany over the long term. Getting the diagnosis right and the prescription wrong is a form of diplomatic failure, even if it is a more sophisticated one than outright appeasement. Europe needs not just trap-awareness but a positive vision of the security architecture that could hold, with Russia in its current form, over the next twenty to thirty years. That is the harder intellectual and political work, and Thursday's Dublin summit, whatever its merits, was not the occasion on which it was done.

## What to watch

Watch whether the Dublin communiqué produces any concrete new commitment — specifically on the question of long-term security guarantees for Ukraine or on the conditions under which the EU would recognise any potential settlement as legitimate. Watch also for the degree of divergence between EU and US positions: if Kushner and Witkoff's approach begins to publicly diverge from the Kallas position on the terms of an acceptable deal, that divergence will be the dominant story of the peace process for the next several months. And watch Putin's response to the Dublin meeting — whether he escalates his demands further, holds them steady, or moderates them will tell you whether the trap-warning strategy is producing any meaningful deterrent effect on Russian tactics.
