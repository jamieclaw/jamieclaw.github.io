Donald Trump announced on July 31 that Hamas has agreed in principle to a phased disarmament deal that would see it surrender its weapons in exchange for a full Israeli military withdrawal from Gaza. [Trump told reporters](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/31/trump-says-us-has-understanding-with-israel-on-hamas-disarmament-deal) the US had reached an "understanding" with Israel, though he acknowledged the process would be "complex to deliver." Hamas confirmed it had accepted the framework, [prompting cautious optimism](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/31/world-reacts-with-cautious-optimism-to-hamas-disarmament-agreement) from Arab states, European governments, and UN officials. Yet Israel's far-right coalition partners immediately announced their opposition, and Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu has not formally accepted the plan. The BBC's Jeremy Bowen, reporting from the region, [noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c2dkje4p4klo) that "zero trust, the deaths of thousands and the destruction of Gaza" remain formidable obstacles even as the proposal offers "rare hope."

## The received wisdom

The mainstream framing of this development is one of long-overdue progress. After nearly two years of grinding warfare, more than a hundred thousand casualties, and multiple failed ceasefire attempts, a framework that envisages Hamas laying down its arms is seen by most Western commentators as the only realistic path to ending the conflict. The argument runs as follows: Hamas can never be a legitimate governing force, the Palestinian people deserve security and reconstruction, and any deal that removes armed groups from power is inherently stabilising. The Biden administration spent years trying and failing to broker hostage deals; Trump, it is said, has managed through sheer pressure and unpredictability what careful diplomacy could not. The [world's cautious optimism](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/31/world-reacts-with-cautious-optimism-to-hamas-disarmament-agreement) is therefore framed as earned — a tentative endorsement of the deal's strategic logic.

## A different read

One should not dismiss this moment. If Hamas actually disarms — surrendering rockets, tunnels, and command structures — it would be one of the most consequential events in the modern Middle East. The analogy is not Oslo, which preserved Arafat's armed Fatah apparatus as a governing instrument; it is closer to the IRA's decommissioning in Northern Ireland, a process that took seven years, multiple failed timelines, and an independent verification body before most of its weapons were destroyed. Even then, sceptics argued decommissioning was never fully complete.

The structural problem here is more severe. [Trump himself acknowledged](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/31/trump-says-hamas-disarmament-deal-will-be-complex-to-deliver) the deal would be "complex to deliver" — an unusual admission of difficulty from an administration that typically speaks in superlatives. The sequencing question is unresolved: Israel has not yet formally accepted the plan, and the [BBC's reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20e94k2ex5o) makes clear that Israel's far-right ministers regard the proposal as a betrayal of their stated war aim of total military victory. Netanyahu's coalition arithmetic has not changed: his government depends on ministers who believe the only solution is demographic transfer or permanent military occupation. Any deal that produces an Israeli withdrawal hands those ministers the leverage to collapse the government.

Then there is the question of what replaces Hamas in governing Gaza. The Palestinian Authority under Mahmoud Abbas is discredited, under-resourced, and has not held elections since 2006. A disarmed Hamas is not the same as a politically neutralised Hamas — the organisation's social networks, its schools, its charity infrastructure would all survive disarmament. The precedent of Hezbollah in Lebanon is instructive: a militia that formally participates in a state structure can rebuild military capacity while nominally complying with disarmament agreements. This is not to say a deal is worthless. It is to say that the hard work begins after the signing ceremony, not before it.

The Trump administration's transactional style — which produced this announcement — may also be its greatest liability in the implementation phase. Deals that depend on personal relationships between leaders and bypass institutional verification mechanisms tend to fray when the political incentives shift. [As the BBC noted](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c2dkje4p4klo), the "realities of politics, war, zero trust, the deaths of thousands and the destruction of Gaza" are not dissolved by a White House press conference. The pattern of Trump-era diplomacy — grand announcements followed by protracted, often failed implementation — should temper enthusiasm without negating it entirely.

The most honest read is this: the deal is significant because it establishes a framework both parties have nominally accepted, and frameworks matter even when they are imperfectly enforced. But the distance between a framework and a durable peace settlement is the distance between a declaration of intent and the patient, unglamorous work of verification, reconstruction, governance-building, and political management that only sustained institutional attention can provide.

## What to watch

- Whether Israel's cabinet formally endorses the plan or Netanyahu allows it to die quietly under coalition pressure from far-right ministers.
- The verification mechanism: who monitors disarmament, under what timeline, and with what enforcement power if Hamas stalls or hides weapons.
- Palestinian Authority capacity and willingness to fill a governance vacuum in Gaza.
- Whether the deal survives the first major incident — a rocket attack, a settler provocation, or an IDF operation that derails the framework before it can be implemented.
