Spain went to the polls this weekend in a snap election that [Pedro Sánchez called after several months of parliamentary deadlock](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/27/spains-pedro-sanchez-bets-on-left-wing-voting-surge-to-defy-election-polls), betting that a mobilised left-wing base would outperform surveys showing the conservative Partido Popular ahead. [Both Sánchez and his rivals rallied supporters in a final pitch for votes](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c8wy5p4gdnwvo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in a campaign marked by fierce argument over housing, immigration, and the terms of Catalan accommodation — the last of which fractured Sánchez's previous coalition and precipitated the election. The result will determine not only which party governs Spain but whether the kind of multi-party progressive government Sánchez pioneered can survive a second term of economic strain, regional nationalism, and an increasingly organised populist right under the Vox-PP competition.

## The received wisdom

The standard progressive framing of this election is straightforward: Sánchez is a beleaguered but principled social democrat, governing an ungovernable coalition in difficult times, facing a right that has been radicalised by its proximity to Vox and that has used procedural obstruction to paralyse government rather than offer a constructive alternative. His housing policies — price controls, expanded social housing commitments, rent freezes in overheated urban markets — are belated responses to a genuine crisis. [Spain's young people have been priced out of the housing market](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/cm9w9y8947xqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in a way that is not primarily of their own making; it is the product of decades of under-investment in social housing, speculative property markets, and an economic model that concentrated wealth in real estate. The left's answer — intervention — is at least proportionate to the scale of the problem.

On Catalonia, the mainstream line is that Sánchez's amnesty deal with separatist parties was a painful but necessary price for stability, trading short-term outrage for a longer-term reduction in constitutional tension. The alternative — permanent confrontation with a region that contributes disproportionately to national GDP and has a democratic mandate for greater autonomy — is worse. And on immigration, the progressive argument is that Spain's geographic position makes it a first-line recipient of flows that the EU has collectively mismanaged, and that the humanitarian obligations are real regardless of the electoral discomfort they create.

## A different read

That framing is not dishonest, but it systematically understates the costs of Sánchez's governing approach and overstates the right's radicalism. Begin with the housing crisis. Spain's property market problems are real, but they predate Sánchez and have been compounded — not relieved — by regulatory uncertainty. Rent controls have a consistent empirical record: they reduce supply, freeze mobility, and concentrate benefits among existing tenants while locking out new entrants, particularly the young renters Sánchez claims to champion. The economics here are not ideologically contested in the way that fiscal policy or trade is; study after study from Stockholm to San Francisco has shown that price controls in rental markets produce predictable distortions. Sánchez knows this, which is why his housing minister has repeatedly softened the implementation, then announced it again before each electoral cycle.

The Catalan amnesty is a more complex case, and the right's handling of it has frequently been disproportionate. But the core objection — that Sánchez negotiated constitutional settlements with parties whose explicit goal is secession in exchange for parliamentary support — is not extremism. It is a democratic accountability argument that any honest examination of the precedent requires taking seriously. The 1978 Constitution was written to end a dictatorship and accommodate regional diversity within a unified state; successive amnesties for people convicted of organising illegal referendums test whether that settlement can hold. The concern is not that Catalonia should be suppressed but that the method of accommodation — transactional, opaque, driven by parliamentary arithmetic rather than democratic mandate — corrodes the legitimacy of the framework it claims to protect.

On the right, the picture is also more complicated than the standard framing allows. The PP under Alberto Núñez Feijóo has explicitly distanced itself from Vox on a range of issues, including immigration enforcement and rule-of-law questions. The coalition arithmetic in Spain, as in much of Southern Europe, makes it difficult for centre-right parties to govern without some accommodation of their populist flank, but that is a structural problem of the multi-party system rather than evidence that the mainstream right has been captured. The comparison with countries where the far right has actually entered government — Italy, Hungary, the Netherlands — is instructive: Spain's Vox has not achieved that threshold, and the PP's refusal to include them in a formal coalition has been consistent.

The broader European context matters here. [Spain's young people on housing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/videos/cm9w9y8947xqo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) are part of a generation that has watched property prices detach from incomes across the continent, and whose frustration has become the primary fuel for the new populism on both left and right. The parties that survive this period will be the ones that offer credible structural answers — supply-side reform, planning liberalisation, long-term investment — rather than the short-term regulatory interventions that give the appearance of action without altering the underlying dynamics.

## What to watch

Watch the coalition arithmetic rather than the headline vote shares: Spain's fragmented parliament means that a plurality for either bloc does not automatically translate into a governing majority. Watch whether Vox performs above or below its poll numbers — the gap between polling and actual far-right performance has been positive in some European elections and negative in others, and the direction tells you something about whether the angry voter phenomenon is stabilising or still expanding. Watch the housing and rental data for Barcelona and Madrid in the months after the election, whatever the result: if intervention is extended, the supply effects will be measurable within a year. And watch whether Sánchez, if he loses, reconfigures the Spanish left around a less transactional model of coalition-building — or whether the lesson drawn is that he needed to be more progressive, not less.
