The United States [announced visa restrictions on certain South African officials](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqy0z5y7wnp2o) Wednesday, citing what the Trump administration characterises as anti-white racial discrimination in South Africa's government policies — specifically its affirmative action framework. The [announcement](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/16/us-visa-restrictions-south-africans-trump-claims-anti-white-discrimination), which did not specify named individuals, follows months of escalating Trump administration rhetoric about South Africa, including the President's repeated and [disputed claims](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/15/white-south-africans-denied-refugee-status-us) about "white genocide" in the country — a phrase that South African government officials and independent analysts have consistently rejected as a mischaracterisation of the country's complex post-apartheid political landscape. South Africa [formally condemned](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/16/south-africa-slam-planned-us-sanctions-over-alleged-racial-discrimination) the announcement. The move comes as Johannesburg separately deals with a [series of femicide cases near the international airport](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/16/discovery-women-bodies-anger-femicide-south-africa), a crisis that has prompted its own national reckoning.

## The received wisdom

The liberal framing is consistent and, on many of its facts, accurate: Donald Trump's campaign around South African Afrikaners is built in significant part on a false premise — the "white genocide" narrative is not supported by the data on farm murders, which, while serious and disproportionately affecting farmers, does not constitute a systematic campaign of ethnic extermination. South Africa's affirmative action policies — the Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment framework — are constitutional in South Africa, democratically enacted, and broadly consistent with the redistributive logic that most post-conflict societies adopt after a history of formal apartheid. On this reading, the US action is not principled human rights advocacy; it is political theatre aimed at a domestic audience, deployed by an administration that has simultaneously reduced refugee admissions for many of the world's most genuinely persecuted populations.

The Guardian's reporting adds a complicating empirical note: many of the white South Africans who applied for US refugee status — believing Trump's "white genocide" framing — have [been rejected by the very refugee programme](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/15/white-south-africans-denied-refugee-status-us) created ostensibly for them, which suggests that even within the administration's own framework, the policy is not functioning as advertised.

## A different read

And yet the easy liberal rebuttal — "this is just racist politics dressed up as human rights" — runs past several things worth engaging with directly.

South Africa's post-apartheid political economy is not a simple success story. The ANC's three decades in power produced one of the highest Gini coefficients in the world — a measure not of black-white inequality alone but of overall social stratification, including the emergence of a politically connected black elite alongside persistent mass poverty. South Africa's unemployment rate has hovered above 30 percent for years, which is not primarily a racial story but is a story of governance failure: corruption, mismanagement of state enterprises, and the failure to translate resource wealth and a capable private sector into broad-based employment growth. A country where youth unemployment exceeds 60 percent has a governance problem that transcends racial politics.

The specific instrument the US has deployed — [visa restrictions on officials](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqy0z5y7wnp2o) — is not unprecedented in American foreign policy. The US regularly imposes visa restrictions on officials of countries whose policies it objects to, from Nicaragua to Belarus (which [this week released 25 political prisoners](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/16/belarus-frees-25-political-prisoners-in-return-for-us-sanctions-relief) in exchange for US sanctions relief — an example of exactly this kind of pressure working). The question is not whether the tool is legitimate but whether the target and the justification are appropriate. Applying a human-rights instrument designed for authoritarian repression to democratic South Africa's affirmative action policies is a category error that degrades the credibility of the tool for its intended uses.

There is a deeper problem with racial statecraft as a foreign policy doctrine, and it applies not only to the Trump approach but to the progressive mirror image of it. Once a government commits to adjudicating other nations' domestic racial policies from the outside — deciding which affirmative action programmes are legitimate and which constitute discrimination, which histories of oppression are recent enough to justify remediation — it has entered a zone of essentially unlimited discretion. Every country has contested racial and ethnic histories. Every country's majority group has a political faction that feels aggrieved by post-conflict redistribution. The precedent of US visa restrictions for "racial discrimination" against a majority demographic group is one that could, in principle, be applied by a future administration against any country's race-conscious policies — including, potentially, those of allies with their own affirmative action frameworks.

South Africa's conduct at the International Court of Justice in The Hague — including its genocide case against Israel — has clearly contributed to the deterioration of US-South Africa relations under this administration. The [Philippines' Duterte appearing at the ICC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq986j7x1nd3o) this week is a reminder that international legal institutions can be used by states to advance geopolitical agendas as much as to deliver justice. The Trump administration's animus toward South Africa has multiple roots, and the racial framing may be partly a domestic-audience justification for a foreign policy posture whose real drivers are geopolitical.

## What to watch

Watch whether the unnamed officials subject to visa restrictions are eventually identified — the specificity or vagueness of enforcement will reveal whether this is a genuine policy or a rhetorical gesture. Watch South Africa's response at the UN General Assembly, which meets this week and provides Pretoria a global platform to articulate its position. Watch whether the EU or UK — South Africa's other major trade partners — make any formal statement, and whether the absence of such statements is itself a signal. And watch the South Africa-US bilateral trade relationship: the AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act) framework, which gives South African exports preferential US market access, remains a potential point of economic leverage in either direction.
