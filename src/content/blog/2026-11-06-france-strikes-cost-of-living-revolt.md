More than 600,000 people marched across France on Thursday, according to the interior ministry's own count — with union organisers claiming as many as 1.57 million. Five major trade unions had joined what began as a student protest movement over conditions at *lycées* to mount a coordinated national day of strikes. The Eiffel Tower closed. The Louvre shuttered exhibition rooms. Oil refinery workers walked out, and France had to import electricity as nuclear plant operations were disrupted. In Paris, the "black bloc" clashed with police near the Rhône while tear gas was deployed in Bordeaux. A teenager was stabbed in Seine-Saint-Denis. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, under mounting pressure, announced Thursday evening that he would convene an emergency ["social conference"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckx2qzv835pgo) with unions and employers for the following Monday — an implicit concession that the government no longer controlled the tempo of events. The unions' central demand: a rise in the *Smic* minimum wage, currently €12.31 an hour, and a net wage increase of at least €100 a month.

## The received wisdom

The sympathetic reading of Thursday's protests is straightforward and, up to a point, correct. France is experiencing a genuine cost-of-living crisis driven in large part by the global inflation stemming from the US-Iran war and the knock-on effects on energy prices. Working-class and lower-middle-class French households — the people who cannot work from home, cannot absorb an extra €200 on their heating bill, cannot opt out of the supermarket — are bearing costs they did not choose and cannot escape. The CGT's Sophie Binet was not wrong when she described the movement as having "reached a new level." The [Al Jazeera footage](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/11/5/mass-protests-in-france-driven-by-nationwide-strike-action) of ordinary families marching in provincial cities alongside students is a reminder that this is not Paris-bubble radicalism. And the government's own fiscal constraints — a budget deficit it is trying to bring down to 5% of GDP — mean there is limited room to simply spend the anger away. The structural pressures are real.

## A different read

And yet. The habit of reading French street mobilisation as the authentic voice of the people requires examination. The CGT's claim of 1.57 million participants against the interior ministry's 600,000 is a gap of nearly a million bodies — the routine arithmetic of French industrial action, where both sides negotiate with the numbers as much as with each other. More importantly, the demand for a €100 net monthly wage increase, while viscerally appealing, sits inside a French labour market already characterised by some of the highest non-wage labour costs in the developed world. French employers pay social charges of roughly 45% on top of gross wages. Every €100 of net wage increase costs an employer somewhere between €180 and €220 in total compensation. In a country where small and medium-sized businesses employ the majority of the private-sector workforce and are already squeezed by energy costs, the arithmetic is brutal.

The historical parallel that haunts this moment is not, as commentators reflexively reach for, May 1968. It is more precisely the *gilets jaunes* revolt of 2018-19. That movement, too, began with a fuel price grievance, rapidly absorbed broader economic anxieties, briefly terrified the political class, and ultimately secured a minimum wage increase and tax rebates from Emmanuel Macron — before dissipating without fundamentally changing the structural trajectory of French economic policy. Macron survived it. His successor governments have repeated the same essential pattern: emergency concessions, followed by a return to fiscal consolidation, followed by renewed popular frustration.

What the revived *gilets jaunes* movement — which has now [called for further blockades on Friday and Saturday](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/ckx2qzv835pgo) — suggests is that the 2018 concessions did not resolve the underlying tension. They deferred it. France's welfare state is extraordinarily expensive, its labour market is extraordinarily rigid, and its political system is structurally unable to choose between reforming the former or liberalising the latter. The street has become, in effect, a veto mechanism for reforms the unions will not accept and a demand mechanism for expenditure the state cannot afford. This is not a crisis of capitalism. It is a crisis of a specific variety of post-war social contract that has reached the limits of its fiscal sustainability while remaining politically immune from revision.

Lecornu's "social conference" will almost certainly produce some face-saving gesture — a *Smic* increase timed for January 1, perhaps a promise to review public-sector pay. It will not address the structural question. The question is whether France's political centre — already battered by a decade of Macronist disruption and now facing a resurgent hard left and a Marine Le Pen waiting patiently in the wings — has the capacity to run a different play. The evidence of Thursday suggests it does not.

## What to watch

Watch for three things over the next fortnight. First, whether Monday's social conference produces a concrete *Smic* increase or merely a commitment to "dialogue" — the latter will almost certainly re-energise the street. Second, whether the *gilets jaunes* weekend blockades of Friday and Saturday produce the kind of economic disruption that forces the government's hand before Monday, or fizzle as they sometimes do when unions are not formally behind them. Third, and perhaps most consequentially for European politics, whether the French government's inability to contain its fiscal deficit — now under pressure from both rising borrowing costs and potential wage concessions — triggers renewed scrutiny from European bond markets of the kind that has repeatedly haunted Paris since 2022.
