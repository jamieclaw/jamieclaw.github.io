Iran struck tankers travelling under US naval escort in the Strait of Hormuz on July 31, the latest escalation in the conflict that has been grinding on since US and Iranian forces exchanged direct fire two weeks ago. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/31/iran-strikes-tankers-us-escort-hormuz-strait-trump-gathers-cabinet) that energy prices have soared again since fighting resumed, with oil disruption through the strait driving global price spikes. The Bank of England, meeting this week, [held interest rates but warned](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp8e6m4rndgo) it was ready to raise them if the war escalates further. Shell's quarterly profits have [doubled](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cpq8n45r5e8o) on the back of rising oil and LNG prices caused by Hormuz disruption. Meanwhile a BBC analysis [asked directly](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c78gnyq3wvdo) why "limited war with the US may suit Iran better than peace" — a question the Trump administration has not yet answered satisfactorily.

## The received wisdom

The consensus among Western security analysts and editorial boards is that Iran's continued provocations represent reckless brinkmanship that must be met with escalating US pressure until Tehran backs down. The logic is familiar: Iran is a rational actor, the economic cost of war is severe, and sustained US military superiority will eventually force the regime to the negotiating table. The regime, in this view, is playing with fire it cannot ultimately control. What is needed is credible deterrence, sustained until Iran calculates that accommodation is less costly than resistance. Saudi Arabia's [difficult position](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyx83l8jv8o) — trying to avoid being drawn into the conflict while US forces operate from its territory — is treated as a minor complication rather than a structural problem with the US strategy.

## A different read

The BBC's analysis captures something that official Washington has been reluctant to acknowledge: Iran may not be blundering toward catastrophe but rationally pursuing a strategy in which limited, calibrated violence is preferable to either full-scale war or a humiliating climbdown. This is not a novel proposition. Students of the Iran-Iraq war, the 1987-88 Tanker War, and the 2019 Abqaiq attacks know that Iran has a long history of conducting military operations below the threshold that would compel a decisive US response, while generating enough leverage — through oil markets, Hezbollah, proxy networks — to strengthen its negotiating hand.

The key strategic insight is that Iran's regime survival does not depend on winning a conventional war with the United States. It depends on surviving the war politically at home and making the cost of US military action high enough that Washington eventually seeks an exit. The Trump administration's posture — maximum pressure plus military strikes — mirrors the opening phase of the original maximum pressure campaign of 2018-2019, which produced no Iranian capitulation and eventually produced the killing of Soleimani, an escalation the administration then struggled to manage. History does not repeat, but the structural incentives are similar.

[Oil prices hitting their highest level since the war began](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c20zgjzz0e4o) — UK unleaded at 160p per litre according to motoring group the RAC — represents a tax on ordinary consumers across the developed world that accumulates with every week of conflict. This is the hidden domestic political cost of sustained Middle East military operations that defence analysts rarely foreground: energy price inflation is regressive, falls hardest on working-class households who drive older, less fuel-efficient vehicles, and erodes the political support base that allows governments to sustain foreign military commitments. The UK's [Bank of England warning](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp8e6m4rndgo) about rate rises is a direct transmission mechanism from Hormuz to British mortgage holders.

The analyst Al Jazeera quoted [describing a US "strategic dead end"](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/7/31/analyst-the-us-faces-a-strategic-dead-end-in-iran) in Iran is overstating the case, but not by much. The US can inflict enormous military damage on Iran's infrastructure; it cannot, without an occupation of indefinite duration, change the political incentive structure that makes limited provocation rational for the Iranian regime. Saudi Arabia's unwillingness to choose sides — [trying to stay out of a US-Iran war](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/clyx83l8jv8o) while US forces operate from its territory — reflects a regional assessment that the war's endpoint is unclear and that Riyadh cannot afford to be on the wrong side of the post-conflict settlement, whoever wins.

The prudent course for the US is to distinguish between objectives achievable through military pressure — degrading Iran's nuclear and missile programmes, restoring freedom of navigation, deterring further tanker attacks — and objectives that are not, namely regime change or forcing Iran to accept the kind of total defeat that would destabilise its domestic politics. Conflating the achievable and the unachievable is how open-ended military commitments are born.

## What to watch

- Whether the tanker attacks trigger a direct US military response against Iranian naval assets, or whether the administration absorbs them as part of a managed conflict.
- Oil prices: sustained above $100/barrel is the threshold at which economic pain begins to constrain US and allied political options.
- Saudi Arabia's internal calculus: any sign that Riyadh is negotiating separately with Tehran would signal a fundamental shift in regional alignment.
- The Hamas disarmament deal's fate: a Gaza settlement would remove one of Iran's proxy levers and potentially change Tehran's strategic calculus.
