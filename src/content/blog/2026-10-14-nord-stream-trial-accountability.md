Germany has opened a criminal trial against a Ukrainian man accused of carrying out the [sabotage of the Nord Stream pipelines](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw99zpmnxzmjo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) in September 2022 — one of the most consequential acts of infrastructure destruction in post-Cold War Europe. The attack, which ruptured the twin undersea gas conduits linking Russia and Germany beneath the Baltic Sea, released the largest single methane-emissions event in recorded history and permanently disrupted European energy flows at the precise moment the continent was scrambling for alternatives to Russian gas following Moscow's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. German federal prosecutors have charged the defendant with participation in an attack on critical infrastructure and causing an explosion. Ukrainian officials have denied state involvement. The trial is expected to be fiercely contested, with significant legal and diplomatic implications extending far beyond the courtroom in Germany.

## The received wisdom

The mainstream European and American press has, since the earliest reporting on the Nord Stream sabotage, been deeply reluctant to pursue the story's most uncomfortable implications. The initial instinct — shaped partly by wartime solidarity with Ukraine and partly by the inconvenient geopolitical optics — was to treat the question of attribution as genuinely unresolved and possibly unanswerable. When [investigative journalist Seymour Hersh](https://seymourhersh.substack.com/p/how-america-destroyed-the-nord-stream) reported in February 2023 that the United States had carried out the attack with Norwegian assistance, the response from mainstream outlets was scepticism shading into dismissal. Subsequent German and Swedish investigations produced their own leads pointing toward a pro-Ukrainian network operating without (or perhaps with informal) state direction. The received wisdom now holds that this is a murky episode in a murky war, that the truth may never be fully known, and that prosecuting one Ukrainian diver is a manageable legal process that need not destabilise the broader Western alliance supporting Kyiv.

## A different read

The comfortable vagueness of that framing should trouble anyone who takes the rule of law seriously — not as a rhetorical flourish but as an actual organising principle of liberal democracies.

The Nord Stream attack was not a minor skirmish. It destroyed approximately €10 billion worth of infrastructure, triggered a cascading European energy crisis that drove inflation, shuttered industrial capacity, and accelerated Germany's deindustrialisation. It involved the apparently deliberate targeting of civilian infrastructure in international waters during a conflict in which the attackers were not formal belligerents. By almost any conventional definition, this was an act of terrorism or warfare — carried out by someone, against someone, with strategic intent.

The German prosecution now places at least partial attribution on the record. [BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw99zpmnxzmjo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) confirms that the suspect is a Ukrainian national, and prosecutors believe the attack was coordinated. What remains fiercely contested is whether the operative acted independently, under the direction of Ukrainian military or intelligence services, or as part of a network with deeper geopolitical sponsorship. Ukraine's government has consistently denied responsibility.

Here is where the conservative reading diverges sharply from the alliance-management consensus. A functioning rule-of-law order does not permit powerful states to destroy the critical infrastructure of third parties — even adversarial third parties — without legal accountability. The reluctance of European governments and the American administration to pursue the story robustly suggests a troubling double standard: the same Western institutions that have championed International Criminal Court accountability for Russian commanders appear indifferent to equivalent accountability when the actors may be allied governments or their proxies.

Niall Ferguson noted in his analysis of the Ukraine conflict's turning points that the failure to establish clear red lines — on both sides — creates a precedent library that future actors will exploit. The Nord Stream attack is already cited by Russian propagandists as justification for infrastructure strikes on Ukrainian civilian energy systems. The argument is legally incoherent but politically effective precisely because Western governments have been unable or unwilling to establish a clear, authoritative account of what happened and who authorised it.

The [NPR reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/13/nx-s1-6000051/ukraine-zelenskyy-npr-interview-russia-war) on the Trump administration's threats to cut intelligence support to Ukraine highlights an additional dimension: the fragility of the Western coalition supporting Kyiv. A trial that implicates Ukrainian state actors — even informally or circumstantially — at precisely the moment when American political support is weakening could provide political cover for further disengagement. German prosecutors are not acting in a vacuum; they are operating within a European political context where support for Ukraine, while still substantial, is under greater domestic pressure than at any point since February 2022.

The right-of-centre view here is not that Ukraine should be abandoned, nor that Russian infrastructure attacks are equivalent to Western ones. It is that the erosion of accountability norms — the willingness to look the other way when allies commit acts that would be condemned if committed by adversaries — is corrosive to the international order that Western democracies claim to defend. Germany deserves credit for proceeding with this prosecution even when the diplomatic convenience would have been to quietly close the file.

## What to watch

- **Attribution chain**: Whether German prosecutors can establish a direct chain of command from the defendant to Ukrainian military or intelligence leadership will determine whether this remains a lone-operative case or becomes a state-actor controversy.
- **Swedish and American responses**: Sweden closed its own Nord Stream investigation without charging anyone; the US has never addressed the Hersh reporting with a full, official rebuttal. Diplomatic pressure on both may intensify as the trial proceeds.
- **Impact on Ukraine aid debates**: Republican critics of Ukraine funding in the US Congress have already cited the sabotage as evidence of Ukrainian recklessness. A conviction with state links could harden that caucus.
- **Russian information operations**: Moscow will exploit every trial development for maximum propaganda value. Watch for Russian state media's framing to track the most politically damaging interpretations.
