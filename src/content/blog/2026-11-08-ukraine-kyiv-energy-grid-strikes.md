Russian missile and drone strikes hit Ukraine's power infrastructure overnight, causing [blackouts and water shortages across Kyiv](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/07/blackout-water-shortage-kyiv-russia-strike-ukraine-energy-grid) and wounding nine people in surrounding regions, according to Ukrainian emergency services. The attacks targeted substations and transmission lines, continuing a pattern of energy infrastructure strikes that Russia has deployed every winter since 2022. [BBC reporting](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cm4g1nq8158po?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) noted the timing: the strikes came even as preliminary diplomatic signals — including movement around [Qatari mediation efforts on Iran](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/07/strait-of-hormuz-qatar-plan-iran-nuclear-inspections) and American midterm results that reshuffle Washington's posture — have created a tentative optimism about renewed talks. Ukraine's grid operator confirmed the damage was severe, with repair crews working under emergency protocols.

## The received wisdom

Western liberal commentary on Russian energy strikes generally frames them as war crimes — deliberate targeting of civilian infrastructure prohibited under the Geneva Conventions — and calls for accelerated air defence deliveries to Ukraine. This framing is legally correct and strategically sound as far as it goes. There is no legitimate military justification for targeting power plants that heat apartment buildings and keep hospital generators running. The humanitarian cost is real, calculable, and documented by the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission. International humanitarian law exists precisely to constrain this category of coercion.

Advocates for Ukrainian support — a position this analyst broadly shares — argue that the pattern of infrastructure targeting proves the impossibility of good-faith negotiations with the Kremlin: you cannot negotiate seriously with a counterparty that uses civilian suffering as a bargaining chip. The timing of the strikes, just as diplomatic atmospherics improved marginally, is read as deliberate — Moscow signalling that any peace it tolerates will be on terms it dictates through force, not through mutual compromise.

## A different read

There is a harder, less comfortable analysis that Western governments resist publicly articulating, though their private assessments are reportedly more candid. Russia's energy targeting strategy is not irrational — it is coldly rational within its own strategic framework. By degrading Ukraine's infrastructure each winter, Moscow imposes costs on Ukrainian civilian morale, strains Western donor patience, and creates conditions under which Kyiv's negotiating position weakens year on year. The strategy worked after World War One: the Bolsheviks' willingness to accept Brest-Litovsk's humiliating terms in 1918 was predicated on their calculation that a breathing space, however costly, was preferable to total collapse. Moscow appears to be applying the inverse: make Ukraine's breathing space as costly as possible until Kyiv's calculus shifts.

The historical parallel that matters here is not the winter Blitz of 1940-41, which hardened British morale as Churchill predicted. It is the Siege of Leningrad — not because the situations are morally equivalent, but because prolonged civilian suffering in the absence of decisive military resolution does eventually produce political ruptures. Ukraine is not Leningrad, and Zelensky is not Stalin. But the political dynamics of a democracy prosecuting a grinding attritional war are different from an authoritarian state's — Ukraine's government must maintain popular legitimacy, manage coalition politics, and sustain Western support simultaneously.

What concerns the clear-eyed conservative analyst is not Ukrainian resolve, which has been remarkable by any historical standard, but the coherence of Western support architecture. The American midterms shifted the congressional balance; the UK's Burnham government is navigating its own [fiscal constraints](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6x2zrjl7kjno?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) while promising to maintain defence commitments; Germany's coalition politics remain volatile. The Kremlin's energy targeting strategy is calibrated not just to punish Ukraine but to stress-test Western unity — to determine at what cold, dark threshold European publics begin to pressure their governments toward a negotiated outcome on terms more favourable to Moscow than to Kyiv.

The conservative position here is clear-eyed about power: Russia will not accept a settlement that leaves Ukraine capable of joining NATO, and NATO will not accept a settlement that formalises Russian control of occupied territories under international law. This is not a gap that diplomatic creativity can bridge without one side accepting a fundamental strategic defeat. The energy strikes are Moscow's reminder that it believes time is on its side — that Ukraine's supporters will tire before Russia's will breaks. Whether that calculation is correct remains the central strategic question of the war's fourth year.

Supporting Ukraine means providing the air defence systems that can intercept these strikes. Patriot batteries, SAMP/T, and eventually long-range strike capacity — not out of idealism but because allowing Russia to successfully weaponise winter is the clearest possible signal that Western deterrence has a ceiling Moscow can exploit.

## What to watch

- The pace of Patriot and European air defence resupply to Kyiv — the bottleneck is political will, not manufacturing capacity
- Whether the Ukrainian grid's repair capacity holds through winter; Kyiv's engineers have adapted remarkably, but cumulative damage has a compounding effect
- Russian strike patterns in the coming weeks — a shift toward more precise military targets might signal genuine interest in negotiation; continued civilian infrastructure targeting signals the opposite
- European public opinion polling on Ukraine support as winter deepens and energy bills rise
